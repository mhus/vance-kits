# Emotional Thermometer — keeping the conversation in productive range

A difficult conversation runs at an emotional
temperature. Cold is fine — both calm, productive.
Warm is still fine — engaged, real. Hot is unproductive —
flooding, defensive, performative. If either side runs
hot too long, the conversation fails regardless of the
words.

The thermometer is the thing to monitor. Not the words;
the heat.

## Recognising your own temperature

Self-monitoring is harder than monitoring others, but
more important. Tells of running hot:

### Physical

- Heart rate up.
- Shallow breathing or holding breath.
- Tension in chest, throat, stomach.
- Hands cold or trembling.
- Voice tightening.
- Tears welling (anger or sadness).

### Cognitive

- Hard to find words.
- Repeating the same point loudly.
- Catastrophic thinking ("they hate me", "this is over").
- Mind racing through past grievances.
- Black-and-white framing ("they always", "they never").

### Behavioural

- Talking faster.
- Voice rising.
- Interrupting.
- Checking out / dissociating.
- Wanting to leave / hide / fight.

If you notice 3+ tells, you're hot. The conversation is
no longer productive even if the other side is calm.

## Recognising their temperature

Watching for:

- **Stiffening.** Body tension, shoulders raising.
- **Rapid talking.** Faster pace; talking over you.
- **Rigid stance.** "I disagree" without engagement.
- **Withdrawal.** Going quiet; eyes drifting; minimising
  responses.
- **Intellectualising.** Switching to abstract argument
  to escape feeling.
- **Counter-attack.** Bringing up unrelated grievances.
- **Tears / red face / shaking.**

When you see these, the conversation is at risk. Time to
modulate.

## Cooling moves — yourself

### Pause

The most reliable cooling move. "Give me a moment."
Three slow breaths. Resume.

The pause feels long; it isn't. Two-three seconds of
silence is barely noticeable to the other person; long
enough to lower your heart rate slightly.

### Name it

"I'm getting heated; I want to stay productive." Often
the act of naming brings you down a notch. The other
person also sees it as honest, not weak.

### Drop your shoulders

Physical reset. If you're tense, release. Body cools
mind.

### Slow your speech

Hot voices speed up; intentionally slowing helps. Each
sentence with a brief pause between.

### Drink water

Real and useful. Sips create a natural pause and
physical reset.

### Step out

For high heat: "I need to step out for a few minutes;
I'll come back." Different from leaving. Set a return
time.

Don't:

- Suppress visibly. "I'm not angry" while obviously
  angry reads dishonest. Acknowledge briefly; cool.
- Apologise for the feeling. "Sorry, I'm getting
  emotional" undermines you. The feeling is real;
  acknowledge without diminishing.

## Cooling moves — them

You can't cool another person directly. But you can
make space for them to cool.

### Slow your own pace

Speeding up when they speed up escalates. Slowing down
unilaterally creates a different rhythm; sometimes they
match it.

### Acknowledge the feeling

"This is frustrating." / "I hear that this hurts." /
"I can see this is hard."

Acknowledging ≠ agreeing. You can acknowledge the
emotion without conceding the substance.

Don't fake-acknowledge. "I understand you're upset" said
to dispatch the emotion isn't acknowledgement; it's
dismissal.

### Lower your voice

Whispering doesn't help; soft-but-clear speech does. The
other person often matches.

### Ask, don't tell

When they're hot, declarative statements feel like
attacks. Questions invite engagement at a calmer
register.

- "What feels most off here?"
- "What would feel right?"
- "What did I miss?"

### Offer pause

"Want to take a moment?" / "We can come back to this
tomorrow if you want."

Don't insist. The offer is the gift; them taking it is
their choice.

## When pause becomes break

For very hot conversations:

- Acknowledge the heat. ("I think we're both getting
  hot.")
- Propose a real break. ("Let's stop for an hour.")
- Make the resume real. ("We pick up at 4pm.")

A real break differs from "let's never talk about this
again":

- The topic isn't dropped; it's parked.
- The time is specific.
- Both people return to it.

If the other person says "I don't want to come back to
this" — that's information. It might mean cool-down
needed; might mean topic-avoidance. Different responses
for each.

## When the temperature stays unproductive

### One side, persistently hot

You're calm; they're escalating. Options:

- **Continue to acknowledge + slow.** Sometimes the heat
  burns itself out as you don't feed it.
- **Name the dynamic.** "We're not going to land this
  while we're both heated. Let's break."
- **Disengage.** "I want to talk about this when we're
  both calmer. Let me know when that is."

Don't engage in heat-matching. Two-sided escalation is
the worst dynamic.

### Both sides, persistently hot

You're both burning. Don't try to push through. Stop;
schedule; resume. The conversation will be there
tomorrow; productive talk now isn't possible.

### One side, persistently flat

The opposite problem: you're trying; they're flat /
withdrawn / minimising. Often a defence rather than
agreement.

- **Ask explicitly.** "What's going on for you right
  now?"
- **Allow space.** Some people process internally; give
  silence.
- **Don't push.** Pushing into withdrawal often
  backfires. Withdraw your own intensity and create
  room.
- **Schedule next.** If the flat state holds, defer.
  "Let's revisit this when you've had time."

## Specific high-heat scenarios

### Tears

Yours or theirs. Common in difficult conversations;
not failure.

If yours: pause; let them happen; resume when ready.
Don't apologise excessively.

If theirs: offer tissue / silence / question. Don't
rush; don't fix; don't withdraw uncomfortably. "Take
your time."

Then continue when they're ready. Tears don't end the
conversation unless one side wants them to.

### Anger flare

Voices rise; words sharpen. Real risk of escalation to
attack.

Counter:

- Lower your voice. Don't match.
- "I want to keep talking, but not like this."
- If safety is at risk (verbal abuse, fear of physical
  escalation): leave. Safety first.

### Crying-as-tactic vs. real tears

Sometimes hard to tell. The honest move: respond to
both the same way (compassion + room) without abandoning
the conversation. If real tears, you've been kind. If
tactical, you haven't been manipulated into folding.

### Dissociation / shutdown

The other person goes flat; eyes glaze; "uh-huh"
responses without engagement. They've checked out.

Continuing while they're checked-out wastes the
conversation. Pause; check in. "I notice you've gone
quiet. Want to take a break?"

## Anti-patterns

### Pushing through heat

"Let's just finish this." Heat-while-finishing produces
words you'll regret and decisions you'll need to revisit.
Pause when hot.

### Suppressing visible emotion

Pretending calm while obviously hot. Reads as
dishonesty. Better to acknowledge briefly: "I'm
struggling to talk about this calmly."

### Using their heat against them

"You're being irrational" / "This is exactly why we
can't talk." Weaponising the temperature reading.
Counter-productive.

### Cool-as-control

Some users use a cold register to feel in control;
they're actually disengaged. The conversation feels
like an interview with a robot. Real warmth isn't
heat; it's engagement.

### Failure to call the break

Knowing the conversation is in unproductive heat;
continuing anyway because pausing feels like
losing. Push through is the worse failure.

- "**Persistent dysfunction — defer the conversation.**

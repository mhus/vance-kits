# BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front

A military-derived structure that's become standard in
clear professional writing. Put the conclusion / decision
/ ask at the top, before the reasoning.

The discipline: lead with what the reader needs to know
even if they read no further. Most readers won't read
further; structure for them.

## The structure

```
BOTTOM LINE: [conclusion / decision / ask, in 1-2
sentences]

CONTEXT: [why this matters, briefly]

REASONING: [the support for the bottom line]

ASKS / NEXT STEPS: [what you need from the reader]
```

Variations:

- "Bottom line" can be implicit (lead sentence is
  obviously the conclusion).
- "Context" can be skipped if the reader already has
  it.
- "Asks" merge into "bottom line" if the request *is*
  the bottom line.

## When BLUF works

### Decision memos

> Bottom line: I recommend Vendor X for Phoenix.
>
> Context: We're choosing between three vendors before
> Friday's deadline.
>
> Reasoning:
> - X has the strongest track record in our sector.
> - X's pricing is mid-range; not lowest but most
>   transparent.
> - X passed our security review; Y did not.
>
> Asks: Could you confirm by Wednesday so I can
> notify them?

Decision-maker can read just the bottom line and act if
they trust your reasoning. Or they can read further if
they want to validate.

### Status updates

> Bottom line: Phoenix is on track for July launch;
> two issues need exec attention.
>
> Status:
> - Engineering: 80% complete, ahead of schedule.
> - Design: complete.
> - Issues: vendor pricing 15% over budget; legal
>   review pending.

Reader gets the headline; can drill where relevant.

### Requests

> Bottom line: Can you review the attached spec by
> EOD Friday?

The whole email might be one sentence + attachment.
Don't pad. The ask is the bottom line; everything else
is unnecessary.

### Bug reports / incidents

> Bottom line: Login broken for ~5% of users since
> 2pm; suspected cause auth-service deploy at 1:55pm;
> rollback in progress.
>
> [Detail follows.]

Reader on-call needs the headline first; details for
investigation.

### Recommendations / proposals

> Bottom line: I propose we drop Initiative Y from Q3
> in favour of accelerating Z.
>
> [Reasoning, evidence, next steps.]

Reader can react to the proposal immediately; details
for examining the case.

## When BLUF doesn't work

BLUF is the default for professional written
communication. There are exceptions.

### Sensitive personal contexts

Bad-news emails (firings, breakups, condolences) sometimes
need a softer landing. Pure BLUF — "I'm letting you go.
Here's why:" — can feel cold.

A measured opening that contextualises the message,
followed by the message, may serve better. Not throat-
clearing — actual context: "I want to talk about
what's been on my mind for a while."

That said: the message still needs to land clearly. Don't
hide it in paragraph 6.

### Persuasive writing where order matters

Some essays / arguments are designed to take the reader
on a journey — building from premise to conclusion. BLUF
ruins the structure.

This is the rare case. Most "I want to take them on a
journey" framing is actually procrastination on stating
the point. Honest writing for thinking-pieces uses
BLUF too:

> "This essay argues that X. The argument has three
> steps."

Then the journey, with the destination known.

### Cultures / contexts where indirectness is the norm

Some cultures (parts of East Asia, parts of British
professional life, some academic fields) lean indirect.
Pure BLUF can read brusque.

Calibrate: lead with the bottom line *softly* — perhaps
via context that points toward it — rather than
hammering it.

### Negotiation

In negotiations, leading with your final position can be
strategic mistake. The order matters; you might open
with context, surface the constraints, *then* propose.

Different from "burying the lead" — strategic order ≠
unclear writing.

## The "why-then-what" alternative

For some contexts, "why" before "what" works better:

> "We've been seeing customers churn at 8% month over
> month — primarily citing pricing concerns. To
> address this, I'm proposing a tiered pricing model.
> Details below."

The first sentence is context that *implies* the
direction. The proposal lands prepared.

This works when:

- The proposal alone would be confusing without context.
- The reader needs to be persuaded that the problem is
  real.
- The "what" follows obviously from the "why".

This isn't burying the lead — the lead is "we're
proposing a pricing change because of churn"; the
sentence carries both why and what.

## Length calibration

BLUF doesn't mean "short". Some BLUF documents are
multiple pages. The bottom line is at the top; the
detail follows.

A 5-page proposal can have a 3-sentence executive
summary at the top. The summary IS the BLUF.

For short messages, BLUF often *is* the message:

> "Vendor decision is X. Reason: Y. Confirming by
> Friday."

Three sentences; complete BLUF.

## Specific applications

### Replying to a request

You're replying to someone who asked you something. They
want the answer first.

> Bad: "Thanks for the question. I've been thinking
> about this for a while. Considering all the factors,
> and looking at our recent experience, I think the
> answer is yes."

> BLUF: "Yes. Here's why: ⟨reasoning⟩."

### Disagreeing

You're disagreeing with someone in writing. BLUF the
disagreement.

> "I disagree with the proposal. The main concern is X."

Then context and reasoning.

The reader can read just the headline if they trust you;
they don't have to wade through context to find your
position.

### Communicating bad news

Bad news warrants softer than "Bottom line:". But still
clarity:

> "I have hard news to share: ⟨bad news⟩. Here's what
> I know about why and what's next."

Acknowledge the weight; deliver clearly. Don't bury.

### Long-thread participation

You're 8 messages into a thread. New point. Lead with
the new point.

> "New observation, separate from the thread above:
> ⟨new point⟩."

Reader knows it's new; doesn't have to parse from
context.

## What to put after the bottom line

The reader has the headline. Now what?

### Reasoning (in service of the headline)

Why is the bottom line what it is? 2-5 reasons. Bullet
points often work; numbered list if order matters.

### Context (if needed)

What does the reader need to know to evaluate the
reasoning? Sometimes none; sometimes a paragraph.

### Concerns / counter-arguments

Acknowledge the strongest objections; address briefly.
Pre-empts the reader's "but what about X?" that would
otherwise prompt a back-and-forth.

### Asks / next steps

Specific request from the reader. Deadline if any.
Format: bulleted, action-verb-leading.

> "Asks:
> - Confirm by Wednesday EOD.
> - Forward to Alex if you're OK with it.
> - Or push back here with concerns."

### Attachments / links

Reference at the end (or via inline link). "See attached
for full reasoning." / "Details in [doc link]."

## Anti-patterns

### Lead-burying

The actual point is in paragraph 5. Reader misses it.
Especially common in emails written while the writer is
"thinking out loud" — they didn't pre-plan the
structure.

Counter: write the email; then move the actual point to
the top.

### BLUF + redundant repetition

Bottom line at the top; same bottom line restated at
the bottom; same bottom line in the closing
sentence. Reader bored.

Counter: state once; trust the reader.

### Pseudo-BLUF

A "bottom line" that's actually a header for general
context, not a real conclusion.

> "Bottom line: I'm writing about Phoenix today."

Not a bottom line; an opening sentence dressed in BLUF
clothes. Either you have a bottom line or you don't.

### BLUF as rudeness

Some recipients find pure BLUF cold. With those people,
warm slightly — not by adding throat-clearing, but by
choice of words. "I'd recommend X" softer than
"Decision: X".

### Hiding behind reasoning

You start with reasoning hoping the reader infers your
position. Often the reader doesn't infer; they get
confused.

Counter: state position; then reason.

### Bottom-line on something you haven't decided

You write "Bottom line:" because the structure feels
authoritative; the bottom line is "I don't know yet".

That's fine — say "I don't have a recommendation; the
trade-offs are X and Y." Honest "I don't know" is
better than performative authority.

## Output line

- "**BLUF structure complete:** bottom line, reasoning,
  asks identified."
- "**Lead is buried** — promote ⟨specific sentence⟩ to
  the top."
- "**No clear bottom line yet** — clarify the message
  before sending."
- "**BLUF wrong tool here** — sensitive context;
  soften opening."

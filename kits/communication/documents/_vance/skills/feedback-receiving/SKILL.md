The user has received feedback (or is about to). The
work isn't to defend, isn't to capitulate; it's to
*receive* — extract the signal, decide what to do with
it, manage the emotional component.

The hardest moves: separating the message from how it
was delivered, recognising your own defences, and
deciding when feedback warrants action versus when it
warrants noting-and-discarding.

## Default protocol

1. **Pause before responding.** The first reaction is
   almost always wrong — too defensive or too
   compliant. Even 5 seconds of silence helps; longer
   is fine.
2. **Separate signal from delivery.** Bad delivery can
   contain real signal; good delivery can contain bad
   signal. The two are independent. See
   `separate-signal-from-delivery`.
3. **Notice your defences.** "That's not fair" / "they
   don't understand" / "they're projecting" — these
   are defences kicking in, not necessarily wrong but
   distorting your evaluation. See `defensive-detection`.
4. **Decide what to do with it.** Not all feedback
   warrants action. Some is wrong; some is right but
   not actionable; some is right and actionable. See
   `what-to-do-after`.
5. **Acknowledge without committing.** "Thanks for
   sharing this. Let me sit with it." — buys time to
   process honestly.

## On-demand manuals

- `separate-signal-from-delivery` — distinguishing
  what was said from how. Sharp delivery + valid
  signal vs. soft delivery + invalid signal vs. all
  combinations. The "what's the kernel?" technique.
- `defensive-detection` — recognising your own
  defences (rationalising, attacking the source,
  intellectualising, withdrawing). Why defences fire;
  what to do when they fire; when they're appropriate.
- `what-to-do-after` — three honest answers: act on it,
  note-and-park, discard. How to choose. Avoiding the
  "every feedback must change behaviour" trap.

If you're emotionally activated, start with
`defensive-detection`. If the message is unclear,
start with `separate-signal-from-delivery`.

## Hard rules

- **Don't respond immediately when activated.** The
  first 30 seconds after hard feedback are the worst
  time to respond. Buy time; respond later.
- **Don't reflexively defend.** "That's not what
  happened" / "but I was..." — the defence may be
  warranted, but reflexive deployment short-circuits
  hearing.
- **Don't reflexively capitulate.** "You're absolutely
  right, I'll change immediately" — sometimes you're
  not absolutely right, and instant agreement is
  conflict-avoidance disguised as openness.
- **Don't take all feedback equally seriously.** Source
  matters; context matters; frequency-of-the-pattern
  matters. One person's offhand observation isn't the
  same as a recurring pattern across many people.
- **Don't punish the messenger.** Even when the
  delivery was rough or the source was wrong, don't
  retaliate. The person took a risk by giving feedback
  even imperfectly; punishing closes future feedback.
- **Don't pretend to act on feedback you've decided to
  discard.** Performative compliance is worse than
  honest disagreement. If you don't think the
  feedback's right, say "I hear it; I disagree, here's
  why" rather than "I'll work on it" with no
  intention.
- **Acknowledge that not all feedback is yours to
  receive.** Some feedback is the giver's
  projection / their bad day / their unrelated
  agenda. You can listen without making it your work
  to absorb.

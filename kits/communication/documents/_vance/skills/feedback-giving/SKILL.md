The user wants to give feedback. The challenge: feedback
that's clear enough to land *and* delivered in a way the
recipient can hear. Most feedback fails because it leans
too soft (recipient can't tell what's being said) or too
sharp (recipient defends instead of receives).

Feedback is information transfer. The metric is: did the
recipient now know what you wanted them to know, and
have an option to act?

## Default protocol

1. **What's the feedback for?** Improvement of behaviour
   / awareness of impact / repair of an issue. The goal
   shapes the form.
2. **Is feedback the right tool?** Sometimes the
   situation needs a directive ("don't do this again"),
   not feedback. Sometimes it needs a difficult
   conversation (`difficult-conversation`). Pick the
   right tool.
3. **Use the OIR / SBI structure.** Observation, Impact,
   Request — or Situation, Behaviour, Impact. See
   `observation-impact-request`.
4. **Time and context matter.** When and where you
   deliver shapes whether it's heard. See
   `timing-and-context`.
5. **Calibrate by direction.** Feedback up (to a boss),
   down (to a report), and sideways (peer) have
   different mechanics. See `asymmetric-feedback`.
6. **Land it; then listen.** Same as
   `difficult-conversation/landing-and-listening` —
   don't fill silence with softening; the recipient
   needs space to absorb.

## On-demand manuals

- `observation-impact-request` — the OIR / SBI frame.
  How to write each component. What makes a good
  observation (specific, verifiable). What counts as
  impact (real, attributable). When to skip the request.
- `timing-and-context` — when to give feedback (and
  when to wait), where (private vs. group), and how
  to surface that you have feedback (the "is now a
  good time" gate).
- `asymmetric-feedback` — feedback up the org, down,
  and sideways. Power asymmetry shapes risk and
  reception. Specific patterns for each direction;
  what's harder, what's easier.

## Hard rules

- **Be specific.** "You're doing a good job" doesn't
  inform anyone of anything. Specifics are what change
  behaviour.
- **Speak to behaviour, not character.** "When you did
  X" beats "you're an X person". Behaviour is
  changeable; character feels like an attack.
- **Don't sandwich.** The "compliment-criticism-
  compliment" sandwich is widely taught and widely
  wrong. Recipients learn to ignore the bread; the
  feedback is muddled. Direct beats sandwich.
- **Don't bring stale grievances.** If something
  bothered you weeks ago and you're surfacing it now,
  flag it: "this is from last month; I should have said
  it sooner." Don't pretend it's fresh.
- **Don't deliver feedback you wouldn't want to hear in
  reverse.** If the form would feel cruel coming the
  other way, redesign the form. The substance can be
  hard; the delivery can still be respectful.
- **Don't deliver feedback you don't believe.** Lukewarm
  positive feedback to make the conversation easier —
  recipient can usually tell. Honest "I don't have much
  to say here" beats fake positive.
- **Don't dress directives as feedback.** "I'd love it
  if you considered..." when the actual message is
  "stop doing this". Be direct; the recipient
  appreciates clarity.

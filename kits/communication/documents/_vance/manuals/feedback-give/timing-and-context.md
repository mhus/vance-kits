# Timing and Context — when and where feedback lands

The same words deliver differently depending on when
and where. A feedback delivered well in the right
context lands; the same words in the wrong context
generate defensiveness.

This manual is about reading the moment and shaping the
delivery to fit.

## When to give feedback

### Soon, not immediate

The general rule:

- **Not in the moment.** Mid-meeting, mid-disagreement,
  mid-flow — too hot. Both sides are reactive; nothing
  productive lands.
- **Not weeks later.** Stale grievances surfaced at
  week 3 are confusing for the recipient and read as
  ambush. Memory has decayed; defences have hardened
  around the original event.
- **Within ~24-48 hours.** The sweet spot. Recent
  enough that the situation is remembered; far enough
  that emotional heat has cooled.

For positive feedback, even sooner is fine. Saying
"that was great" right after the great thing reinforces
naturally.

### Wait if hot

If you're upset, give it time. Feedback delivered when
you're hot reads as venting — the recipient hears the
emotion, not the message. Cool first.

Test: can you state the observation factually without
your voice rising? If no, wait.

### Wait if they're hot

If they're stressed, in crisis, just received bad news,
or in a high-pressure moment — postpone. Their capacity
to receive feedback is low; whatever you say will land
distorted.

The "right moment" rule of thumb: when both of you have
breath, capacity, and time.

### The next-meeting trap

"I'll bring it up at our 1:1 next week." Sometimes
right; often becomes "next next week" / "after the
launch" / never. If it matters, schedule sooner.

If you keep delaying, ask why:

- **Genuinely waiting for context fit?** Fine.
- **Avoiding because uncomfortable?** Push through;
  it'll be more uncomfortable later.

## How to introduce that you have feedback

Cold-opening with feedback can feel like ambush. A
brief warm-up:

### The "is now a good time" gate

- "I want to share some feedback. Is now a good time?"
- "I noticed something I wanted to mention. Got a few
  minutes?"

The gate gives the recipient a moment to settle and
indicates that they're not getting ambushed. Most of
the time they say yes; occasionally they ask to defer,
which is fine.

When they say "no, not now" — respect it. Schedule.

### When the gate is too formal

For low-stakes positive feedback, the gate is overkill.
Just say it: "Hey, that thing in the meeting today —
you handled it really well, especially the part about
X." No setup needed.

### When the gate is necessary

For high-stakes / negative feedback, the gate prevents
the recipient from feeling ambushed. Worth the slight
formality.

## Where to give feedback

### Private vs. group

**Private** is the default for any negative feedback,
critical observations, or anything that could be seen
as criticism.

**Group** is appropriate for:

- Process-level feedback that everyone benefits from
  hearing ("we as a team should be better at X").
- Positive recognition that's appropriate to share
  publicly.
- Coordination corrections delivered in the moment
  ("let's all stay until we've covered the agenda").

Never:

- Negative feedback to one person in front of the
  group. Even gentle. The shame component contaminates
  the message.

### Meeting vs. 1:1 vs. spontaneous

- **Scheduled 1:1:** good for substantial feedback.
  Time, space, no audience.
- **Spontaneous private exchange:** good for small
  feedback delivered close to the event.
- **Group meeting:** only appropriate for content the
  group should hear.
- **In writing:** documentation of feedback after a 1:1
  is useful; substituting writing for a real
  conversation is usually not.

### Walking conversation

For some recipients, walking-and-talking lowers the
intensity. Eye contact is reduced; emotional pressure
is lower. Useful for difficult feedback if the
relationship and setting allow.

## When the context is wrong

Sometimes you have feedback to give, but the context
won't be right anytime soon (the recipient is leaving
the company; you're in a heated period; the
relationship is too distant).

Options:

- **Skip.** Some feedback isn't worth the effort.
- **Write it.** A clear written feedback can land where
  conversation can't.
- **Wait for the right moment**, even if it's months.
- **Find a proxy.** Sometimes the feedback is better
  delivered by their manager / a peer / someone they
  trust more.

## The feedback-by-emergency anti-pattern

The user has been sitting on feedback for months. A
crisis hits; they deliver in the heat ("now is finally
the time"). It lands as ambush, not feedback.

Counter: don't let feedback accumulate. Small / regular
feedback prevents the buildup that crisis triggers.

If you've already accumulated, deliver during a calm
moment with explicit acknowledgement: "I should have
flagged some of this earlier. I want to share it now."

## Cultural and personal context

Feedback culture varies hugely:

- **Direct cultures** (Dutch, German, Israeli) accept
  blunt feedback as normal. Hedging reads as either
  weakness or insincerity.
- **Indirect cultures** (Japanese, many Asian, much of
  British) prefer hint and context. Bluntness reads as
  rude.
- **High-power-distance cultures** make upward feedback
  much harder; downward more direct.

Calibrate to the recipient, not your default. If you're
unsure, lean slightly more direct than the recipient's
expectation; clarity beats over-hedging.

Personal preferences vary within cultures. Some people
appreciate "tell me straight"; some don't. Default to
the OIR frame; adjust delivery style to the recipient.

## Specific timing situations

### After a presentation / public event

Wait an hour or more. Right after, the presenter is
adrenaline-flooded; nothing lands properly. A brief
"that went well" or quick observation is fine; full
feedback later.

### After a mistake / error

Wait until the situation is contained. Feedback during
clean-up reads as kicking-while-down. The lesson lands
better when the immediate stakes have cooled.

### After good news

Some negative feedback is OK after good news (the
positive context absorbs); some isn't (taints the
moment). Use judgement.

### Just before they leave

Don't deliver feedback at end-of-day if the conversation
will need follow-up. They'll stew on it overnight; you
can't address it until next day.

Same for "just before vacation" / "before the weekend".

## Anti-patterns

### Drive-by feedback

You see them in the hallway; you mention something
critical; you walk on. They're left holding it without
the context to engage.

Counter: feedback worth giving is worth giving with
some care. Either schedule a moment or save it.

### Email feedback as substitute for conversation

Hard feedback in email. Recipient reads alone; defends
internally; can't ask clarifying questions in the
moment. Dignity-of-conversation matters.

If you write, follow up with a conversation. Don't let
email be the whole feedback.

### "Quick word" before a meeting

Five minutes before a meeting they need to focus on,
you grab them with feedback. They're distracted before
the meeting and unable to engage with you. Both
suffer.

### Feedback at high-emotion moments

You're celebrating their promotion; you slip in
feedback. You're consoling them after a setback; you
slip in feedback. Wrong moments. The contrast feels
manipulative.

### Pre-emptive defensive feedback

"I want to give you feedback before HR does." Often
true; also often condescending. If HR is going to
deliver it, sometimes the right move is to let them.

## Output line

- "**Timing right:** ⟨specific reason⟩. **Context fit
  for delivery.**"
- "**Timing wrong** — postpone to ⟨specific better
  moment⟩."
- "**Context wrong** — switch ⟨public → private /
  group → 1:1 / chat → in-person⟩."
- "**Feedback worth dropping** — context won't be right
  in usable timeframe."

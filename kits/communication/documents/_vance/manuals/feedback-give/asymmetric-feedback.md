# Asymmetric Feedback — up, down, sideways

Power asymmetry shapes the risk and reception of
feedback. The same OIR-formatted message reads
differently depending on who's giving it to whom. This
manual covers what differs.

## The three directions

### Down — to a direct report

You have authority over the recipient. Power is with
you.

What's easier:

- They're more inclined to listen because of position.
- They have less ability to dismiss you.
- They expect feedback from you (it's part of your
  job).

What's harder:

- Their incentive to defend / agree-without-engaging is
  high. They may say "yes, makes sense" without
  internalising.
- They may fear the feedback's career implications and
  optimise for survival rather than learning.
- Power dynamics can make even small feedback feel
  outsized to them.

What to do:

- **Be concrete.** Vague feedback from a manager amplifies
  anxiety because they can't tell what to do with it.
- **Distinguish flagged-vs-flagging.** Make clear when
  it's a "fix this" vs. an "FYI". They can't always
  read this from your tone.
- **Solicit what they observed too.** "How did you see
  it from your side?" — invites engagement past compliance.
- **Set up follow-up.** "Let's check on this in two
  weeks." Closes the loop; signals that improvement
  matters.
- **Watch for compliance-without-understanding.** If
  they seem to be agreeing too easily, dig: "What do
  you think will be different next time?"

### Up — to your boss

You're below them. Power is with them.

What's harder:

- Career risk is real (or felt). Even if your boss is
  receptive, you're calibrating defensively.
- Boss may not see the feedback as legitimate ("not
  your role").
- Fewer cultural defaults for upward feedback. Many
  workplaces don't expect / model it.
- Boss may have less time / patience for it.

What's easier:

- The feedback can be very valuable to them — they get
  less honest input than reports do.
- If they're a good boss, they actively want it.

What to do:

- **Ask permission first.** "Would it be useful to share
  some feedback?" Higher gate than usual; the gate
  matters more here.
- **Frame as observation + impact, not directive.** Skip
  the *request* sometimes; let them decide what to do
  with the input.
  - "I noticed in the all-hands, you cut Alex off mid-
    presentation. The team came out of that meeting
    confused about whether their input is wanted."
  - "I noticed you've been quieter in 1:1s lately. I
    want to flag that I'm not sure if I'm misreading or
    if there's something I should be doing differently."
- **Don't pile up.** Upward feedback works best
  one-at-a-time. Multiple critical observations in one
  sitting is overwhelming and can feel like a coup.
- **Watch tone.** Direct ≠ blunt; respectful directness
  is the goal.
- **Calibrate to your boss.** Some bosses really want it
  and respond well; some don't. Read the response
  pattern before committing more.
- **Skip if too risky.** If the org genuinely punishes
  upward feedback, sometimes silence is the safer
  choice. Note: it's a system problem, not your
  problem to solve via heroic feedback delivery.

### Sideways — to a peer

Equal power. The default-natural feedback direction.

What's easier:

- No power asymmetry to navigate.
- Mutual; you might be receiving feedback from them
  too.
- Often the relationship can absorb directness.

What's harder:

- No structural mandate. Why are you giving them
  feedback? If unclear, can feel meddling.
- Potential for relationship-cost if it lands badly.
- Cross-team peers may have less context for each
  other's work; observations may be wrong.

What to do:

- **Earn the right.** If you've never given feedback
  before, the first one is a recalibration of the
  relationship. Lower-stakes feedback first; build the
  habit.
- **State the relevance.** Why are you giving this?
  "Your work affects mine because X" / "I noticed
  because we co-led the project" — establishes
  legitimacy.
- **Be reciprocal.** Invite feedback back. "Anything
  you'd want to flag from your side?"
- **Don't manage them.** Peer feedback isn't directive.
  Leave action up to them.

## Specific patterns

### Skip-level feedback

Feedback going across an organisational gap: e.g. you
to your boss's boss; or you to a report's report.

Risky for both directions. The intermediate level may
feel bypassed; the recipient may feel ambushed. Default:
route through the proper level. Direct skip-level
feedback only with reason.

### Cross-functional peer feedback

You and they are peers, but your manager doesn't manage
them. Feedback is harder because:

- Their accountability runs elsewhere.
- Your observation may be partial — you don't see them
  at full context.
- Their manager may resent peer feedback that wasn't
  routed through them.

Default: deliver as observation; don't add request.
"I want to share something I noticed about how our work
intersected." Let them and their manager decide if
there's an action.

### Feedback to a junior far below you

They may not have the context to fully engage with the
feedback ("your strategic thinking on this was off" —
they didn't know what strategic thinking was expected).

Adjust: simpler, more concrete, more teaching-mode. The
feedback can include the context they may have missed.

### Feedback to a senior far above you

They may have heard variants of this feedback before;
the context they have is much richer than yours.

Adjust: humility about your view, but clarity. Senior
leaders often receive too-sandwiched feedback and
appreciate directness.

### Mutual / two-way feedback

Some 1:1s explicitly include "what should I do
differently". This frame is healthier than one-direction
feedback.

If your relationship doesn't have this norm, suggesting
it can be powerful. "Could we both share one piece of
feedback in our next 1:1?"

## Specific risk patterns

### Upward feedback that names a colleague

"My peer Alex has been doing X." You're giving feedback
about Alex to your shared boss.

Risky:

- Reads like backstabbing.
- You're triangulating instead of giving Alex direct
  feedback.

Better: give Alex direct feedback first. Only escalate
if direct doesn't work.

### Downward feedback that crosses into HR territory

Feedback about behaviour that's potentially harassment,
discriminatory, or otherwise serious. This isn't
normal feedback; it's an HR matter.

Don't try to handle it as ordinary feedback. Document;
escalate; involve HR / appropriate channels.

### Feedback to someone who's leaving

They've given notice. You have feedback. Worth giving?

Sometimes — if it would help them in their next role
and the relationship can hold. Often not — they're
checked out; the feedback won't change behaviour they
can act on.

If you give it, keep it brief and focused on
takeaways for them, not for the team they're leaving.

### Feedback to someone in crisis

A colleague is going through something hard. You have
work-related feedback for them.

Hold it unless the work issue is urgent. The feedback
will land eventually; their capacity to receive will
return.

## Direction-specific anti-patterns

### Down — feedback as punishment

The manager who only gives feedback when angry or
disappointed. Feedback becomes a punishment vehicle;
recipient learns to fear it.

Counter: regular feedback (positive too) so it's not
loaded.

### Up — flattery as feedback

Upward "feedback" that's just compliments dressed as
observations. The boss can't tell positive feedback
from sycophancy if it's all positive.

Counter: include real observations, even if all are
positive. "I noticed how you handled X" with specifics
is feedback; "you're a great boss" is a Christmas card.

### Sideways — feedback as competition

Peer feedback that's actually positioning. "I noticed
you struggled with X" used to make yourself look
better.

Counter: ask "is this feedback helping them, or
helping me?". If the latter, don't deliver.

### Any direction — feedback withholding

You have feedback that would help them; you don't
deliver because of risk / discomfort / "they should
already know". The withholding is its own choice; own
it.

If you keep withholding, ask: at what cost?

## Anti-patterns

### Pretending no asymmetry

"We're all peers here" — when you're the boss. Pretending
the power dynamic doesn't exist doesn't dissolve it; it
just makes it invisible. Recipients can still feel it.

### Asymmetry as excuse

"I can't give them feedback, they're more senior."
Sometimes true; often used as excuse for avoidance.
Upward feedback is hard but possible.

### Identical delivery across directions

Treating up, down, and sideways the same. Direction
shapes how feedback lands; same words, different
effect.

## Output line

- "**Direction:** ⟨up / down / sideways⟩. **Adjust:**
  ⟨specific⟩."
- "**Direction risk** — ⟨specific⟩. Reconsider whether
  to deliver."
- "**This isn't feedback territory** — ⟨HR / direct
  conversation / management decision⟩."
- "**Calibrate by recipient, not just direction** —
  individual matters too."

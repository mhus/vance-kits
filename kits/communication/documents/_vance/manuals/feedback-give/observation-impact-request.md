# Observation, Impact, Request — the OIR frame

The most reliable structure for feedback. Three parts;
each does specific work; together they communicate
without mistakes-of-interpretation.

(Sometimes called SBI: Situation, Behaviour, Impact —
same three components, slightly different framing.)

## The three parts

### Observation (or Situation + Behaviour)

What you actually saw / heard. Verifiable; specific;
factual.

- ✓ "In the team review on Tuesday, you cut Alex off
  three times when she was presenting."
- ✗ "You don't let people finish their points."
- ✗ "You always interrupt."

What makes a good observation:

- **Specific.** A time, a place, an instance. "Last
  week" beats "always".
- **Factual.** What happened, not your interpretation
  of what it means.
- **Verifiable.** If a third party were there, they'd
  agree the thing happened.
- **One occurrence.** Generalising ("you always do X")
  invites debate about whether it's true. One
  occurrence is harder to deny.

What doesn't qualify as observation:

- Inference. "I could see you were getting impatient"
  — you inferred impatience from observation.
- Mind-reading. "You weren't really listening."
- Character claims. "You're rude in meetings."

### Impact

What that behaviour produced — for you, the team, the
recipient, the work.

- ✓ "I noticed Alex didn't finish her two main points,
  and the team's discussion afterward focused on
  questions she hadn't gotten to."
- ✓ "I felt frustrated because the agenda I'd prepared
  didn't get covered."
- ✓ "Alex looked discouraged for the rest of the
  meeting."

What makes a good impact statement:

- **Real.** Something that actually happened (in the
  world or in you), not hypothetical.
- **Attributable to the behaviour.** A causal chain
  that's plausible.
- **Concrete.** "Frustrated" or "the work didn't get
  done" — not "this is generally bad".

What doesn't:

- Speculation about other people's feelings without
  evidence ("everyone felt uncomfortable").
- Inflated impact. "This destroyed team morale" — from
  one interruption.
- Hypothetical impact. "If you keep doing this, X will
  happen." (That's a warning, not a feedback item.)

### Request (optional but powerful)

What you'd like to be different next time.

- ✓ "Could you let presenters finish before raising
  questions?"
- ✓ "I'd appreciate hearing your concerns at the end of
  the section rather than mid-sentence."
- ✓ "It would help if we could agree to a no-interrupt
  norm in reviews."

What makes a good request:

- **Specific.** A different behaviour, not a different
  person.
- **Reasonable.** Within the recipient's control and
  capacity.
- **Open.** Stated as request, not demand. (Demands have
  their place — but those are directives, not feedback.)

What doesn't:

- Vague. "Just be more aware."
- Character-fix. "Try to be less impatient." (Asking
  for a personality change.)
- Out-of-scope. "Stop being like that with everyone."

## When to skip the request

Sometimes feedback is information without ask:

- "You crushed the launch — the way you handled the
  pivot at week 3 was the thing that made it work."

No request; pure positive feedback. That's fine.

- "I want to flag something I noticed: you've taken on
  three new initiatives this month and seem stretched.
  Just wanted to surface."

Awareness; not an action ask.

When request is forced where it doesn't fit, the
feedback feels prescriptive. Trust the recipient with
the information; they may decide what to do.

## OIR + check-in

A minor expansion: after delivering OIR, ask the
recipient how they see it.

- "How does this land for you?"
- "Did I get the situation right?"
- "Is there context I'm missing?"

The check-in:

- Confirms shared understanding of the observation.
- Surfaces context you don't have.
- Invites them into the conversation rather than just
  receiving.

Don't fake the check-in. If you're not actually open
to revising your view, asking is performative.

## Worked examples

### Negative feedback — meeting behaviour

**Bad:** "You're really overbearing in meetings.
People are getting tired of it."

**Better (OIR):**
- *Observation:* "In yesterday's planning, when Alex
  proposed her timeline, you talked over her twice and
  the discussion shifted to your version of the
  timeline."
- *Impact:* "Alex didn't finish presenting her version,
  and we made the timeline decision based on your
  proposal without comparing the two."
- *Request:* "I'd like for proposals to get heard
  fully before we discuss alternatives."

### Positive feedback — work product

**Bad:** "Great job on the proposal."

**Better (OI):**
- *Observation:* "Your proposal section on integration
  costs included three vendor quotes and the trade-off
  analysis."
- *Impact:* "The exec team moved straight to deciding
  rather than asking for more data — your prep
  shortened the meeting by what felt like 20 minutes."
- *Request: skip* (positive; nothing to ask).

### Positive feedback — interaction

**Bad:** "Thanks for being so helpful."

**Better (OI):**
- *Observation:* "When I came in confused about the
  spec yesterday, you walked through it for fifteen
  minutes without making me feel dumb."
- *Impact:* "I understood the spec, and I felt
  comfortable asking the next question."

### Negative feedback — pattern, not single incident

When you have to flag a pattern, anchor on a recent
specific:

**Bad:** "You're consistently late to everything."

**Better:**
- *Observation:* "In the last three weeks, you've
  arrived 5-15 minutes after the standup start time on
  most days. Tuesday was 18 minutes late."
- *Impact:* "We hold the agenda for you, which delays
  the rest of us. Yesterday I had to run to a 9:30
  meeting and didn't get to my updates."
- *Request:* "Could we agree on either you arriving
  on time or us starting without you and you joining
  late?"

A single specific anchor (Tuesday, 18 minutes) makes
the pattern claim concrete enough to engage with.

## OIR formats by medium

### In-person

State the observation; pause briefly; state the impact;
brief pause; request. Don't rush. The pauses give the
recipient time to track.

### Written

Write all three parts. Keep concise; one paragraph or a
short list:

```
What I noticed: ⟨observation⟩
Impact: ⟨impact⟩
Request: ⟨request⟩
```

Plain markdown / formatted text works in any tool. The
clear structure helps re-reading.

### Slack / chat

Often too short for full OIR. Either save for a real
medium or compress carefully:

```
Quick feedback: I noticed [behaviour] in [situation],
and the impact was [X]. Could we [request]?
```

Don't deliver hard feedback through chat unless small
or pre-agreed.

## Anti-patterns

### Sandwich

"Alex, I really value your work. (Bury feedback.) And
you're doing great overall."

Recipients learn to brace for the criticism between
compliments. The compliments stop reading as genuine.
Direct OIR is more respectful.

### "I" statement that's a "you" attack

"I feel like you're being passive-aggressive." The
"I feel" frame doesn't change the substance — you're
attributing intent. The recipient defends "I'm not
being passive-aggressive!" and the conversation derails.

Better: "I felt brushed off when X happened. Was
that the intention?"

### Anonymous feedback

"Some people in the team have noticed..." — no
attribution. Recipient can't engage; they're suspicious
of the anonymous coalition. Either attribute or own it
yourself.

### Feedback as venting

You're frustrated; you "give feedback" to discharge.
The recipient can tell; the conversation isn't really
about helping them improve.

Counter: process your own feeling first; *then* decide
if there's a feedback message worth giving cleanly.

### Quantifying your authority

"As your senior, I'm telling you..." — invokes
hierarchy as a defence against engagement. Speak to
the substance, not the rank.

### Implicit comparison

"Other people manage this." Implicit comparison stings
without being actionable. If a comparison is genuinely
illuminating, make it specific and useful. Otherwise
skip.

## Output line

- "**OIR drafted:** Observation: ⟨...⟩; Impact: ⟨...⟩;
  Request: ⟨...⟩."
- "**Observation not specific enough** — pin to ⟨specific
  instance⟩."
- "**Impact unclear** — what changed because of it?"
- "**Request out of scope or unfair** — re-scope or
  drop."
- "**This is a directive, not feedback** — switch
  framing."

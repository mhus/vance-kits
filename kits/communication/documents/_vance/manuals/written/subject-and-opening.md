# Subject and Opening — first-impression real estate

The subject line + the first line is what the reader
sees in their inbox preview. It determines:

- Whether they open at all.
- What they expect when they do.
- How quickly they triage.

This real estate is small and high-leverage. Most users
under-invest in it.

## The subject line

### What a good subject does

- **Names the topic.** Reader knows what's inside before
  opening.
- **Signals the action expected.** "FYI" vs. "Decision
  needed by Friday" vs. "Question" — different
  preparation.
- **Is searchable.** Future-them might search for this
  email; subject should contain the keywords they'd
  use.

### Rules

- **Specific.** "Project status" → "Project Phoenix
  status — week 12".
- **Lead with the topic noun.** "Decision needed:
  vendor X for Y" — the reader gets the topic in the
  first three words.
- **Length: 30-60 chars.** Mobile previews truncate
  beyond ~40; some clients show ~70.
- **No clickbait.** "URGENT" / "Important" / "Re:"
  with no original — these read spammy.
- **No emoji unless the recipient norm allows.**

### Action prefixes that work

When useful, prefix to signal type:

- `FYI:` — no action expected.
- `ACTION:` — specific request, often with deadline.
- `DECISION:` — needs a yes/no.
- `QUESTION:` — needs a response.
- `UPDATE:` — periodic status.
- `REVIEW:` — please look at attached.

Not all teams use these conventions; if yours does,
follow. If not, embed the type in the subject text.

### Examples

| Bad | Good |
|---|---|
| "Hi" | "Question about Q3 budget — 5 min to reply?" |
| "Quick question" | "Question: did vendor X confirm by 5pm?" |
| "Update" | "Phoenix project — week 12 status" |
| "RE:" | "Re: Phoenix vendor decision — counter-proposal" |
| "Important" | "ACTION: Sign vendor contract by Friday" |
| "Thoughts?" | "Phoenix architecture — design decision needed" |

## The opening line

### What a good opening does

- **Confirms the topic.** Reader knows immediately that
  this is what the subject promised.
- **Sets the register.** First line establishes whether
  this is formal / casual / urgent / informational.
- **Earns the attention.** If the first line is filler,
  reader scans past it.

### Skip the throat-clearing

These openings waste real estate:

- "I hope this email finds you well."
- "I hope you're having a great week."
- "Thanks for your time on this."
- "Sorry to bother you."
- "Following up on my previous email."
- "I wanted to reach out to..."

Most carry no information; reader skims past. Some
("sorry to bother") actively hurt — pre-apologising
diminishes the message that follows.

### What to lead with instead

- **The headline.** What's the email about, in one
  sentence?
- **The ask.** If the email asks something, lead with
  the ask.
- **The context** if it's necessary. Sometimes one
  sentence of context unlocks the rest.

Examples:

> Bad: "I hope this finds you well. I wanted to follow
> up on the conversation we had last week about the
> vendor selection for Phoenix..."
>
> Good: "We need to pick a vendor for Phoenix by Friday;
> I want to recommend X. The summary is below."

> Bad: "Hope your week is going well. As you know, the
> team has been discussing Q3 priorities..."
>
> Good: "I think we should drop initiative Y from Q3.
> Reason: it conflicts with the Z timeline we
> committed."

### When the throat-clearing is appropriate

Some contexts genuinely warrant it:

- **Cold outreach.** Reader doesn't know you; some
  warmth before substance is human.
- **Sensitive personal contexts.** Acknowledging the
  context before the substance.
- **Cultural norms** that expect the opening pleasantry.

Even then: keep it minimal. One sentence; then
substance.

### Personalisation

Reader's name, brief context-acknowledgement:

> "Alex — re your question yesterday about the Phoenix
> timeline:"

Cheap; effective. Especially in long-thread or
broadcast email, the personalisation signals "this is
for you specifically".

Don't over-personalise. "Hi Alex, I hope your weekend
was great, your kids are well, and the move went
smoothly. Now, about the timeline..." — manipulative.

### Subject ↔ opening coherence

The subject promises X; the opening delivers X. Don't
have a subject that says "Phoenix status" and an
opening that asks about a vendor decision.

If you're tempted, you have two emails, not one. Split.

## Email vs. Slack vs. doc — opening differences

### Email

Subject + opening as described above. Email expects
some structure.

### Slack

No subject; the opening *is* the subject.

- Direct: "Question — Phoenix vendor decision: by
  Friday OK?"
- Tagged: "@Alex — quick check on Phoenix vendor"

Slack rewards even more brevity than email. The
"opening pleasantry" is even more out-of-place; people
scroll past it.

### Long doc / RFC

The doc has a title and an executive summary. The
executive summary is the "opening" — it states the
core proposal in 2-3 sentences. Reader can decide
whether to read further based on summary alone.

### DM vs. group channel

DM: more relaxed; opening can be casual.

Group channel: more formal because of the audience;
opening should be self-contained (others may have
muted; can't assume context).

## When the opening is the entire message

For very short messages, opening = body = closing.

> "Phoenix vendor: I recommend X. Reasoning in attached
> doc."

Three lines; subject + opening + close blend. This is
fine and often best.

Avoid padding short messages to feel "long enough".
Length isn't proportional to importance.

## Subjects for replies

When replying:

- **Update the subject** if the topic has shifted. "Re:
  Phoenix status" can become "Phoenix vendor decision"
  if that's the new thread topic.
- **Keep the prefix** ("Re:") for actual replies; helps
  threading.
- **Avoid endless "Re: Re: Re:"** chains; many clients
  display the original subject regardless, but visually
  cluttering.

## Reply opening

A reply is in-thread; the recipient knows the context.
You usually don't need a fresh opening:

> Bad reply opening: "Hi Alex, thanks for your email
> earlier. I wanted to follow up on what you asked..."

> Good reply opening: "Yes — I'd go with X. Reasoning:"

If the reply needs to acknowledge something received:

> "Got it. The deadline shift is fine. New target: Friday
> the 15th."

Lean. The acknowledgement is the opening; substance
follows.

## Subject anti-patterns

### "Re:" with no original

Looks like spam. Add real subject content.

### Vague urgency

"URGENT" / "ASAP" / "PLEASE READ" without specifying
why. Trains the recipient to ignore your urgent flags.

Counter: when actually urgent, name the urgency:
"Decision needed by 5pm today — Phoenix contract
expires."

### Subject as opening

Subject is "Hey, can you..." and the body continues
"...send me the Phoenix specs by Friday?". Reader has
to combine subject and body to get the message.

Counter: subject self-contained; body has its own
opening.

### Same subject for unrelated topics

You always reply to the same email thread regardless
of topic. Recipient has 50 messages titled "Phoenix"
on different subjects. Searching is impossible.

Counter: new topic = new email or update the subject
mid-thread.

### Sales-style hook

"What if I told you..." / "You won't believe..." — fine
in marketing; out of place in colleague comms. Reads
as manipulation.

## Opening anti-patterns

### Pre-emptive apology

"Sorry to bother..." — diminishes you and the message.
Just send the message.

### Falsely-friendly

"Hope you're well!" when you don't actually know /
care. Reads scripted.

### Long context dump

Three paragraphs of background before the actual point.
Reader misses the point.

### Asking the question buried

"I had a question for you. Specifically, regarding the
Phoenix vendor decision, given the timing constraints
we discussed last week, and the concerns Alex raised
about...". The question never lands clearly.

Counter: lead with the question.

### Opening that doesn't match subject

Subject: "Phoenix vendor decision". Opening: "How was
the offsite?". Reader switches gears; momentum lost.

## Output line

- "**Subject + opening crafted.** Subject: ⟨specific⟩.
  Opening: ⟨first-line specific⟩."
- "**Subject vague** — sharpen to ⟨specific⟩."
- "**Opening throat-clears** — cut to substance."
- "**Subject and opening contradict** — align or split
  message."

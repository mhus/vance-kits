# What to Do After — three honest answers

You've received the feedback. Defences have been
recognised. Signal has been separated from delivery.
Now: do you act on it?

The default assumption is "yes, act on every
feedback". This produces churn — endlessly modifying
behaviour to match every criticism. The honest answer
is more often "sometimes act, sometimes note, sometimes
discard".

This manual is about choosing.

## The three honest answers

### 1. Act on it

The feedback is right; the behaviour matters; you can
change it.

Examples:

- Repeated, specific feedback about a behaviour you
  control. (You interrupt people in meetings; multiple
  sources have flagged it; you can choose differently
  next meeting.)
- A skill gap you can address. (You're weak on X;
  taking a course / practising would fix it.)
- A blind spot you didn't see. (Multiple people
  noticed something you didn't; you can be more
  attentive.)

When acting:

- Be specific about what you'll do differently.
- Set a checkpoint ("In two weeks, will I have changed
  this?").
- Don't over-promise. "I'll work on it" without a plan
  is empty.

### 2. Note and park

The feedback is interesting / partially valid /
worth-knowing, but doesn't warrant action.

Reasons to park rather than act:

- The behaviour may be more feature than bug. (You're
  "too direct"; some recipients don't like it; others
  value it. Whose preference wins?)
- The action would cost more than the problem. (Yes,
  you could rewrite emails to be longer / softer; the
  added effort outweighs the marginal communication
  gain.)
- The feedback is real but isolated. (One person, one
  time. May not warrant a behaviour change yet.)
- You don't yet have the data to act. (The feedback
  raised a question; you need more observation before
  changing.)

Parked feedback is *noticed* — not dismissed. If the
same feedback recurs, the file gets re-opened.

### 3. Discard

The feedback isn't yours to act on.

Reasons to discard:

- The source was wrong about what they observed.
- The feedback is the giver's projection / unrelated
  bad day / agenda.
- The behaviour is genuinely fine; the giver's
  preference is the outlier.
- The feedback contradicts a clear, considered choice
  you've made.

Discarding isn't ignoring — you've evaluated and
decided. Honest discard requires examination. Reflexive
discard is defence (`defensive-detection`).

## How to choose

The choice depends on:

### Fit with what you already knew

Did the feedback hit a spot you already half-suspected?
→ acting more likely warranted.

Did it surprise you completely? → could be insight or
could be wrong; warrants more examination before
acting.

### Recurrence pattern

First time you've heard this? → note-and-park usually
right; act if specific behaviour can be tested.

Heard several times from different sources? → pattern;
acting almost always warranted.

### Source quality

Trusted person who knows your work? → higher weight.

Distant / once-off interaction? → calibrate down.

### Cost-benefit

Cheap to act on? → easier to choose acting.

Expensive (time, identity, relationship)? → higher bar
for acting.

### Reversibility

Reversible behaviour change? → try and see.

Irreversible decision? → much higher evidence bar.

## Specific situations

### Performance review with multiple feedback items

A formal review often packages 5-10 separate feedback
items. Don't treat as monolithic.

For each item:

- Is this real? (Source-evaluate.)
- Is it action-worthy? (Cost-benefit.)
- What specifically changes if I act?

Result: maybe 2-3 items become action items; others
note-and-park; some discard.

Don't try to act on all 10 simultaneously. Pick the
2-3 with highest signal × tractability.

### Feedback you suspect is political

The feedback may be sincere or positioning. You can't
always tell.

Approach:

- Take the substance seriously regardless of motivation.
- Note the politics for context.
- Decide based on substance, not on motivation.

### Feedback that contradicts other feedback

One person says you're too direct; another says you're
too indirect. Now what?

Possibilities:

- Both are right for different recipients; you're
  miscalibrated relative to neither.
- One is right, one is projection.
- Behaviour varies by context; both observations are
  context-specific.

Honest answer: surface the contradiction. "I'm getting
mixed signals — what's the dimension I'm missing?"

Sometimes a third source can resolve.

### Feedback you partially agree with

Common case. The feedback is 60% accurate, 40% off.

Don't act on the whole thing; don't discard the whole
thing. Act on the 60%; flag the 40% as disagreement.

### Feedback about identity / personality

"You're not a team player" / "You're not leadership
material" — character-level.

Behaviours can be acted on; character is harder. Convert:

- "Not a team player" → which specific behaviours?
  (declines collaboration / hoards information / etc.)
- "Not leadership material" → which specific
  capabilities? (vision / influence / decision-making /
  delivery / etc.)

Now you have something to act on or push back on.

If the feedback genuinely is "you are X kind of
person" without behavioural anchor, you can decline:
"That's a claim about me as a person; I don't think
that's the conversation we should be having."

## What "acting on it" looks like

It's not committing to be different forever. It's:

### Specific behaviour change

- "Next three meetings, I'll wait until presenters
  finish before raising objections."
- "I'll send my report a day earlier going forward."

### Process change

- "Before sending Slack messages with critical content,
  I'll pause for an hour."
- "I'll add a peer review step to my process."

### Skill-building

- "I'll take this course / read this book / shadow
  this person on X."

### Awareness practice

- "I'll notice when I'm doing X; even noting without
  changing builds awareness."

In all cases: small, specific, testable. "I'll be
better" isn't a plan.

## Closing the loop

When you commit to acting:

- **Tell the giver.** "Thanks for raising this; I'm
  going to try X. Could you let me know in a few weeks
  if it lands differently?"
- **Set a check.** Calendar reminder; private journal
  entry; ask for follow-up.
- **Notice when you've actually changed.** Often you
  don't; the change atrophies. The check surfaces this.

When you parked or discarded:

- **Communicate that you took it seriously.** "I've
  thought about what you said. Here's my read." —
  honest engagement is respect even when you disagree.
- **Document for future you.** Save what was said,
  why you parked / discarded. If a pattern emerges
  later, you have the history.

## Anti-patterns

### Compulsive acting

Acting on every feedback feels responsive; produces
churn. The behaviour becomes shapeless because it's
optimised for whoever's spoken most recently.

### Compulsive discarding

Treating yourself as the authority on every feedback
("I disagree, so they're wrong"). Closes off learning.

### Performative acting

Saying "I'll work on it"; not actually doing anything.
Performative compliance is worse than honest
disagreement.

### Action without check

You commit; you don't follow up; nobody knows whether
the behaviour changed. The feedback dies; the giver
stops giving.

### Discarding because acting feels hard

The feedback is right; acting would be costly; you
discard with defence-tinged reasoning. Honest answer:
"yes it's right and I'm choosing not to act because
the cost is too high for now". That's a real choice;
disguised it's avoidance.

### Conflating "act on it" with "fix it forever"

Behaviour change is gradient. Acting on feedback once
is acting; you don't have to commit to permanent
transformation. Try; observe; iterate.

# Separate Signal from Delivery — what was said vs. how

Two independent dimensions:

- **Signal:** the substance. Was the feedback's claim
  correct or insightful?
- **Delivery:** the form. Was the feedback delivered in
  a way that was clear, kind, and well-timed?

The combinations matter:

| Delivery | Signal | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Good | Easy. Receive; act. |
| Good | Bad | Clarify; gently disagree. |
| Bad | Good | Hardest. Extract the signal anyway. |
| Bad | Bad | Discard the substance; address the delivery if relationship matters. |

The hardest case is "bad delivery, good signal" — the
delivery makes you want to dismiss the signal. The
discipline is: extract anyway.

## Why this is hard

When delivery is bad, defences fire. The defences
target the substance even though the actual problem
was the form.

> Manager (rude): "You're sloppy with details. Last
> week's report had three errors."

The recipient hears "rude". The defence wants to argue
"I'm not sloppy; you're rude." But the substance ("last
week's report had three errors") may be true.

Common pattern: dismiss the message because the form
was unkind. The form was unkind; the message may
still be right.

## The "what's the kernel" technique

After feedback you didn't like, ask:

> *"If a kind, well-meaning version of this person
> delivered the same message, what would it say?"*

The kindly-delivered version strips away the form
problems. What's left is the kernel.

If the kernel feels like a real observation: there's
signal worth examining.

If the kernel evaporates when stripped of the form:
the message was mostly delivery, not substance. Less
to act on.

Worked example:

> Bad delivery: "You're impossible to work with. Nobody
> wants to be on your team."

Kindly-delivered version: "I've heard from a few
colleagues that they find some of your interactions
challenging."

Kernel: there are people who find you challenging to
work with. Whether or not "impossible" / "nobody"
were accurate, the kernel is worth examining.

## When delivery alone is the problem

Sometimes the kernel really does evaporate. The "feedback"
was venting, was projection, was about the giver's day,
was off-target.

Tells:

- The "feedback" doesn't fit any pattern you've heard
  before.
- The feedback contradicts feedback from many other
  people.
- The giver is in a high-emotion state about something
  unrelated.
- The feedback escalates dramatically beyond observable
  facts ("nobody likes you" from one annoying meeting).

In these cases:

- Don't internalise. The feedback is more about them
  than about you.
- If relationship matters and is salvageable: gently
  flag the delivery problem. "I want to take what you
  said seriously, but I'm having trouble engaging
  with the way it was framed."
- If relationship is the bigger problem, that's a
  different skill — `difficult-conversation`.

## When signal exists but is buried

Often feedback contains a real observation wrapped in
extras:

- The observation is real.
- Plus character attribution ("because you're X").
- Plus inflated impact ("ruining the team").
- Plus prescription ("you need to change immediately").

Strip to the observation. Examine just that. The
attribution / inflation / prescription are commentary
that may or may not be valid, but the observation is
the kernel.

## Multiple signals in one feedback

Many feedbacks bundle several messages:

> "You're not getting the technical work done, and
> you're hard to manage, and your team doesn't
> respect you."

Three claims. Each evaluable independently. Don't
treat as monolith — it's three separate questions
hiding as one.

Pull apart:

- "Not getting technical work done" — is this true?
  what evidence?
- "Hard to manage" — what specifically? from whom?
- "Team doesn't respect you" — what observable behaviour
  shows this?

Some may be valid; some may not be. Bundling helps the
giver dump everything; separating helps you actually
process.

## Calibrating signal weight

Not all feedback warrants equal weight. Factors:

### Source

- Has the person been right about feedback before?
- Do they know the situation?
- Are they in a position to observe what they're
  observing?

A close colleague observing your interactions has more
weight than a distant manager who sees you in
quarterly reviews.

### Pattern

- Are you hearing this for the first time?
- Have multiple people said similar things?
- Have you said similar things to yourself?

A repeated pattern across multiple sources is much
stronger signal than a one-off.

### Specificity

- Concrete observation with example?
- Vague generalisation?

"In Tuesday's meeting, you cut me off three times" is
much stronger evidence than "you're rude in meetings".

### Emotional context

- Is the giver running hot?
- Is there a recent grievance distorting their view?
- Are they in crisis?

Hot feedback warrants more skepticism — not because hot
people are wrong, but because hot reduces the
calibration of their statements.

### Your own pre-existing concerns

- Have you suspected this might be a problem?
- Did the feedback land somewhere you knew was soft?

If the feedback hits a place you already half-suspected,
weight it higher. If it's totally surprising, weight it
slightly higher *for examination* — surprises are
informative, even if eventually wrong.

## Anti-patterns in evaluating signal

### "Source is bad, so signal is bad"

The annoying colleague gives you good feedback. You
discount because you don't like them. Mistake — bad
sources can have good signal.

### "Source is good, so signal is good"

Your trusted mentor gives you advice. You internalise
without examining because of the source. Sometimes
even good sources are wrong on a specific instance.

### "Many people said it, so it must be true"

Sometimes; sometimes there's a coordinated narrative
that's wrong. Multiple non-independent sources don't
add up to confirmation if they're echoing each other.

### "I haven't heard this before, so it's wrong"

Sometimes truth surfaces for the first time. Discounting
because it's new is its own bias.

### Signal-strength inflation

"They were right about X, so they're right about Y."
Specific accuracy doesn't transfer.

### Signal-strength deflation

"They've been wrong about Z, so I dismiss them on Y."
Same in reverse.

## Specific situations

### Performance review

Often a mix of formal, second-hand, and aggregated.
Pull apart:

- What did your manager personally observe?
- What did they hear from others?
- What's their interpretation vs. observation?
- What's the rating-driven framing vs. what they
  actually think?

The structure of formal review distorts signal; extract
what's real.

### Hostile criticism

Someone is angry; the feedback is shaped by anger.
Apply the kernel test. Often the anger has a real
observation underneath, even if inflated.

### Feedback you suspect is political

The feedback may be sincere or may be positioning
within a larger dynamic. You usually can't tell from
the feedback alone. Default: take the kernel seriously;
note the politics; act on substance, not on framing.

### Self-feedback

You give yourself feedback ("I should have known
better"). Treat with the same separation: what's the
kernel of substance vs. what's defensive self-flagellation?

## Anti-patterns

### Dismissing for delivery

The feedback was rude; you dismiss the substance. The
substance may have been right; the delivery was a
separate problem.

### Accepting because delivery was kind

The feedback was kindly delivered; you accept without
examining. Kind delivery doesn't validate substance.

### Tone-policing as defence

"I'd be more open if you said it nicely." Sometimes
true; often a way to deflect the substance. Recognise
when you're using delivery-criticism as a way to avoid
examining the message.

### Conflating disagreement with rejection

Disagreeing with feedback isn't dismissing the giver.
You can value the relationship and disagree with the
specific input.

### Black-and-white evaluation

"Either I take all of this or none." Most feedback has
some signal and some noise. Accept the signal; release
the noise.

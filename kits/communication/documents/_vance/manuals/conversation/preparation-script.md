# Preparation Script — plan, don't memorise

A scripted conversation reads scripted. The other person
can hear the rehearsal in your voice. They feel managed,
which makes them harder to talk to, not easier.

But total improvisation under stress is also bad. You
forget the central point; you accept distractions; you
end up apologising for things you didn't intend to.

The middle path: prepare *structure*, leave *language*
open.

## What to prepare

### The core sentence

The single sentence that captures what you want them to
walk away knowing. Memorise *this*; everything else is
flexible.

Examples:

- "I'm letting you go from this role, effective Friday."
- "I want to break up. I've been thinking about it for
  weeks."
- "I'm not comfortable with how you spoke to me on
  Tuesday."
- "We need to change how the team communicates because
  the current pattern isn't working."

The core sentence:

- **States the message.** No setup, no preamble.
- **Is in your voice** — not stiff or formal.
- **Carries the weight you intend.** Not softened; not
  inflated.
- **Is short enough to actually say** under pressure.

If you can't get the core sentence to one or two
sentences, the message isn't pinned. Pin it before
preparing further.

### The opening 15-30 seconds

How you start the conversation. Not the core sentence
yet — the framing.

- **Name the topic.** "I want to talk about ⟨topic⟩."
- **Acknowledge it's hard.** "This is hard for me to
  bring up." (Honest if true; skip if not — false
  admissions feel manipulative.)
- **Set the frame.** "I'm not here to argue, I want
  you to know X."

Good opening sentences avoid:

- **Bad-news disguised as casual chat.** "Hey, hope your
  weekend was good — so I have to let you go."
- **Question-as-opening.** "How would you feel if..." —
  manipulative. Just say what you mean.
- **Pre-apology cascade.** "I'm so sorry to bring this
  up, I hate having these conversations, I know it's
  bad timing..." — delays the message, raises anxiety.

### The 3 most likely responses

Imagine the other person's three most likely reactions.
For each, plan the *next move* — not a comeback line.
Examples of next moves:

- **They get angry.** Do you stay? Pause? Reschedule?
- **They cry.** Do you offer comfort? Stay quiet?
  Continue?
- **They argue the facts.** Do you re-state your
  point? Ask what they think actually happened?
- **They accept it.** Do you have a "what happens next"
  plan?
- **They ask "why now?"** Do you have an honest answer?

Three responses is the working set. Five is over-
preparation; one is under-preparation.

### What you won't do

Decide upfront on hard limits. Examples:

- "I won't reverse this decision in the room."
- "I won't argue about whether the past events
  happened."
- "I won't agree to a relationship change in this
  conversation."
- "I won't make a scene if they do."

The limits are for *your* clarity, not announcements.
You don't say "I won't reverse this" out loud unless
asked. But you've decided, so you don't accidentally
flip mid-conversation under pressure.

### What you will do afterwards

How does the conversation end? What's the after-state?

- **Do you stay in touch?** ("I'll send the paperwork
  by Monday.")
- **Is there a follow-up?** ("Let's reconnect in two
  weeks.")
- **Is this a final exchange?** ("I'd prefer no contact
  for a while.")

Plan the after-shape. Otherwise the conversation
dribbles to an awkward stop.

## What NOT to over-prepare

### Word-for-word scripts

Memorised scripts read mechanical. The other person
notices; trust drops.

Worse: a script you've memorised and they say something
unexpected. You either go off-script (the prep was
wasted) or stick to the script (you're not responding
to *them*).

Outline beats script. Core-sentence + opening + three-
response-plans is more useful than a transcript.

### Anticipating every possible response

Diminishing returns. Imagining 10 possible reactions
generates anxiety without preparation value. The first
3 cover most reality; further is rumination.

### Pre-rehearsing rebuttals

If you go in with five witty comebacks queued, you're
not in a conversation; you're delivering a debate.
Comebacks tend to *hurt* in the moment and *embarrass*
you later when you remember them.

Counter: prepare *understanding*, not *winning*.

### Memorising a justification list

"If they say I'm wrong, I'll list these 8 reasons why
I'm right." Even if you're right, the list-attack
escalates. Often the conversation isn't about who's
right — it's about whether you can talk about the
thing.

## Naming the elephant

When the elephant in the room is the conversation
itself — the meta-fact that this is a hard talk — name
it explicitly:

- "I know this is awkward."
- "This is uncomfortable for me to say."
- "I've been putting this off."

Naming reduces the meta-anxiety, freeing both sides to
focus on the substance. Don't dwell — name and move on.

Don't name if it's not actually true. False admission
of difficulty reads manipulative.

## When the prep reveals you don't want the conversation

Sometimes preparing reveals: you don't want to have
this conversation at all. You want a different
outcome — for them to change without you saying
anything; for the situation to resolve itself; for
someone else to deliver the message.

Honest signal: "I'm spending a lot of energy preparing
to avoid actually saying X."

Options:

- **Have it anyway.** Sometimes prep reluctance is just
  fear; pushing through serves you.
- **Reframe the conversation.** Maybe the difficult
  message can be a different message. ("I want a raise"
  instead of "you don't appreciate me".)
- **Decide not to have it.** Some conversations don't
  serve you. Living with the unspoken thing is a valid
  choice; sometimes the better choice.

The decision is yours. Surface it; don't auto-pilot
into a conversation you've decided against.

## When the prep reveals the wrong message

Preparation often clarifies what you actually want to
say — and reveals it isn't what you'd planned. The
draft message was about X; the real message is about
Y.

This is a feature of preparation. Re-pin the core
sentence to the real message. The conversation you
have should be about the real thing, not the surrogate.

## Anti-patterns

### Script-as-armour

The script grows; every clause is hedged; every
contingency is mapped. The script is now armour: the
user can't have the conversation without it, and the
armour itself blocks real exchange.

Counter: outline; trust yourself to improvise.

### Rehearsing in front of the mirror

Some users find this useful; many find it produces
stilted delivery. If you rehearse, do it through-
spoken (out loud, without watching yourself) rather
than performance-style.

### Multiple-revision script

The user revises the message script for hours. The
revision time is procrastination dressed as preparation.

Counter: 30 minutes of focused prep; then either have
the conversation or schedule it. Don't keep revising.

### Anticipating every objection

Five hours of "what if they say X". Five hours of
catastrophising. The conversation reality is simpler
than the imagined version. Plan three responses; trust.

### Pre-emptive concession

"They might say X, so I'll concede X up front" — and
now you're conceding things they hadn't asked for. You
weakened your position by anticipating their attack.

Counter: state your position; let them respond; concede
where genuinely warranted.

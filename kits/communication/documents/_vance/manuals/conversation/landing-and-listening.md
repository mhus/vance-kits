# Landing and Listening — what to do after delivering

The hard part isn't saying it; it's what comes after.
Most users prepare what they want to say, deliver it,
and then under-listen to the response. The other person
spoke; you didn't really hear; the conversation became
two monologues.

This manual is about the second half — landing the
message and then listening properly.

## Landing the message

You've prepared the core sentence. You've said it.
Three things matter at the moment of landing:

### Don't fill silence

The seconds after a hard message are uncomfortable. Your
instinct is to fill them — clarify, soften, explain
further, apologise. Resist.

The other person needs time to absorb. Two-three seconds
of silence after the core sentence is fine; ten is
acceptable; thirty is sometimes right.

Filling the silence does several bad things:

- **Dilutes the message.** Each soft addition undoes
  weight.
- **Robs them of processing time.** They're working on
  what you said; talking interrupts that.
- **Signals you're uncomfortable.** Which puts them on
  the spot to manage your discomfort.

If you have to do something: drink water. Look at them
without staring. Wait.

### Don't immediately ask "are you okay?"

The instinct is kind; the effect isn't great. They've
just received hard news; "are you okay" puts the
maintenance burden on them. They may say "yes" to
manage your anxiety even if not.

Better: just be present. Let them initiate the next
move. If too much silence accumulates, an open
prompt — "What are you thinking?" — works better than
a closed check.

### Don't re-deliver the message

Sometimes there's an urge to repeat in different words,
to make sure they got it. Usually unnecessary; they got
it. Repeating reads as either anxiety or as not
trusting them to hold it.

If they genuinely didn't hear (literally, e.g. noise),
re-state simply. Otherwise, hold.

## Listening — what most people miss

Listening looks easy and is actually hard. The default
mode under stress is *waiting to speak* — formulating
your next response while they're still talking.

Real listening is harder:

- **Receiving without rebuttal-formation.** Your job
  during their words is to *take them in*, not to plan
  your reply.
- **Hearing what they actually said** (not what you
  expected).
- **Noting where their reaction matches your prediction
  and where it doesn't.** Surprises are the most-
  important data; they tell you things you didn't
  know.
- **Catching the question behind the question.** The
  literal question often isn't the real question.

This requires you to slow down. Physically — let them
finish; pause before responding.

## Phrases that signal listening

- "Tell me more."
- "What do you mean by ⟨specific⟩?"
- "What's the part that's hardest?"
- "Did I understand X correctly?"
- "How does this land for you?"

Phrases that signal not-listening:

- "I hear what you're saying, but..." (the "but" cancels
  the "hear")
- "Yes, but..."
- "The point is..."
- "Let me explain again..."
- "What you're really saying is..." (telling them what
  they said)

## Distinguishing reaction from response

Their first words may be reaction, not response. People
often need to feel-and-vent before they can think-and-
discuss.

Reactions:

- "How could you?"
- "I knew this was coming."
- "After all I've done?"
- Tears, anger, withdrawal.

Responses come later:

- "Why now?"
- "What does this mean for X?"
- "Can we talk about ⟨specific⟩?"
- "Here's what I think we should do."

The mistake: responding to reaction as if it were
response. Treating "How could you?" as a literal question
to be argued; treating tears as a problem to be fixed.

The move: receive the reaction without combat. Let the
reaction discharge. The response comes after, often a
few minutes later, sometimes the next day.

## When their response is unexpected

Often the other person says something you didn't predict.
They take it differently; they raise something you didn't
think they'd raise; they have a question you didn't
prepare for.

Don't bluff. "I hadn't thought of that" / "I don't have
an answer to that right now" / "Let me sit with what
you just said" are honest responses.

Bluffing produces answers you'll need to revisit; honest
"I don't know yet" preserves trust.

## When they go silent

Silence after delivery isn't necessarily bad. People
process at different speeds; some need quiet to think.

Wait. Five seconds; ten; thirty. Don't fill.

If silence stretches past a couple of minutes:

- "Take your time."
- "What's going on for you?"

Avoid:

- Filling with explanation.
- Asking "are you okay?" repeatedly.
- Withdrawing yourself.

## When they want to argue the facts

A common reaction. "That's not what happened" / "You're
remembering it wrong" / "Actually..."

Possible responses:

- **Engage on facts** if they're checkable and matter.
  Sometimes it's worth it; sometimes not.
- **Decline gently.** "I don't want to argue about what
  happened. I want to talk about how we go forward."
- **Acknowledge and re-state.** "We may remember it
  differently; here's what I observed."

Don't get pulled into a relitigation. The conversation's
purpose isn't to establish historical truth; usually
it's to communicate something or change something.

## When the conversation needs to continue later

Many difficult conversations don't conclude in one
session. Honest endings:

- "Let's pause here. Let me know when you want to talk
  more."
- "I think we both need to sit with this. Tomorrow?"
- "I've said what I came to say. I want to hear your
  thoughts when you're ready."

Don't:

- Pretend resolution. "I think we're good now" when
  unclear.
- Close it off. "I don't want to talk about this
  again."
- Demand commitment. "Tell me right now what you're
  going to do."

Decisions made in the heat of the conversation often
need to be revisited anyway. Allowing space is honest.

## Specific patterns

### When they apologise

Don't reflexively accept ("It's fine.") if it's not
fine. Don't reject either. Acknowledge:

- "Thank you for saying that."
- "I appreciate the apology."
- "I'm not sure I'm there yet, but I hear you."

The honest version is yours.

### When they ask "what do you want me to do?"

Have a clear answer ready, or admit you don't yet.

Bad: "I don't know" when you actually do; or vague
"just be better".

Good: specific request; or "I'm not sure yet — let me
think and come back to you."

### When they cry

Don't fix. Don't fold. Offer tissue / silence / "take
your time". Wait. Continue when they're ready.

Often you also feel tears; that's fine. Don't perform
either way.

### When they react with anger

Stay grounded. Don't escalate (see
`emotional-thermometer`). Continue when temperature is
back in productive range.

If anger crosses into verbal abuse / threats / safety
risk: leave. The conversation isn't worth your safety.

### When they accept calmly

Sometimes the news lands; they accept; conversation
ends quickly. Don't fill the time with extra
explanation or apology. Honour the calm acceptance with
a clean ending.

## Anti-patterns

### Talking your way through their silence

Silence makes you uncomfortable; you fill. Each
filling weakens the message you delivered.

### Waiting to speak vs. listening

Their words are background while you formulate. They
sense it; the conversation deteriorates.

### Defensive listening

Hearing only the parts that confirm your position;
filtering the inconvenient. Bias-symmetric: apply same
attention to the parts you'd rather skip.

### Closing prematurely

You wrap the conversation while there are open threads.
Often because you want it over. The threads come back —
usually worse-formed and at a worse time.

### Demanding processing speed

"Just tell me what you think" while they're still
processing. Their silence is the processing; demanding
output during it produces shallow processing.

### Continuing past your own capacity

You're tired; you're hot; you're past listening. Continuing
because you "should finish" produces bad listening.
Pause and resume.

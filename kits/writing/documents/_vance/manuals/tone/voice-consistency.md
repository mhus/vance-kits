# Voice Consistency — distinguishing voice from register

Register is "how formal". Voice is "who's writing". A
writer can shift register across pieces (formal report
this morning, casual email this afternoon) and still
sound like the same person. That same-person-ness is
voice.

Voice is the most-undermined thing in editing. Most
"professionalising" passes flatten voice in pursuit of
register-correctness, and the writing loses what made
it distinctive.

This manual is about preserving and refining voice.

## What voice is made of

Voice shows in many small choices:

### Sentence rhythms

- Some writers default to short punchy sentences. Some to
  long curlicue ones. Some mix in distinctive ways.
- Try to identify: when this writer reaches for a long
  sentence, what does it look like? Three clauses
  joined by "and"? Embedded asides set off by dashes?
  Listing patterns?

### Word choices

- Latinate vs. Anglo-Saxon preferences ("commence" vs.
  "begin", "perceive" vs. "see").
- Fondness for specific words ("anyway", "actually", "in
  fact" — sometimes voice, sometimes filler; depends).
- Avoidances (some writers never use "you" / never use
  semicolons / never use exclamation points).

### Punctuation habits

- Em dashes vs. parentheses vs. commas for asides.
- Semicolons vs. periods.
- Exclamation point usage.
- Quotation conventions (single / double; nested style).

### Address

- "I" / "we" / "you" / third person — habitual
  pronoun.
- Direct address frequency.
- Rhetorical questions.

### Asides and parentheticals

- Frequency of digression.
- Marker style (parens, dashes, commas).
- Tone of the aside (wry, factual, contrasting).

### Argument shape

- Linear vs. spiral.
- Lots of evidence vs. trust-the-claim.
- Hedge style.
- How conclusions are reached (gradual vs. sharp turn).

### Emotional register

- Warm / cool / cold default.
- Humour presence and style.
- Self-disclosure level.

The combination is voice. No single feature; the pattern
across features.

## Identifying the user's voice

If the user has past writing: read 2-3 samples; note
recurring patterns. Voice is what's the same across
otherwise-different pieces.

If only the current draft is available: look at three
distinct paragraphs from the draft. Which features
appear in all three? Those are voice candidates.

The strongest signals are usually:

- Sentence-length distribution (mostly short; mostly
  long; mixed in a particular pattern).
- Punctuation habits (the writer who uses lots of em
  dashes won't suddenly stop).
- Asides (the writer who digresses parenthetically will
  digress in every paragraph).

## Voice vs. register conflict

Sometimes voice clashes with the register the piece
needs. Wry voice in a serious memo. Formal voice in a
chatty blog. When this happens:

### When voice should win

- The voice is what makes the writer distinctive in
  their field (column writer; columnist; founder
  whose blog is part of the brand).
- The audience has come for the voice, not the
  register.
- Sanding off voice produces text that's functional
  but forgettable.

### When register should win

- The piece is a routine document type with strong
  conventions (contract, court filing, scientific
  paper).
- The register-mismatch will read as amateur, not as
  signature.
- The voice-driven choice would distract from the
  piece's purpose.

### When to harmonise

Often the answer is neither pure voice nor pure
register: keep voice in the texture (rhythm, word
choice within the register's vocabulary, asides), shift
register at the surface (formal vocabulary where
needed, hedge where the piece-type demands).

A good edit preserves a writer-recognisable signature
inside the appropriate register.

## How to preserve voice during edit

### Read with voice-attention

Before editing, identify two or three voice features
from the draft that you want to preserve. Concrete
examples:

- "She uses dashes for asides — preserve that."
- "Sentences vary length deliberately — don't
  standardise."
- "She never uses 'utilise' — don't introduce it."

This list becomes a hold-fast during editing.

### Edit the line, preserve the rhythm

When tightening a sentence, keep the rhythm shape if
possible. If the writer's habit is "short setup; longer
development; punch ending" — edit to that shape, not to
"all medium length".

### Read aloud

Voice is often heard before it's seen. A passage that
sounds like the writer when read aloud is voice-
preserved. A passage that sounds generic is voice-
flattened.

### Check first and last sentences

Voice carries strongest at openings and closings. After
editing, check that openings and closings still sound
like the writer.

## Common voice-flattenings

### Standardising sentence length

A writer's signature includes sentence-length
distribution. Editing every sentence to the same shape
loses signature.

### Removing favourite words

The writer says "anyway" three times in the piece.
Editor cuts two — feels like overuse. But the third
"anyway" on its own loses something. Sometimes the
overuse IS the voice. Hold lightly.

### Rephrasing into editor's voice

The editor reaches for "this also means" where the
writer wrote "and what's funny is" — the cleaner phrase
is the editor's. Restore the writer's where the
voice-cost is real.

### Replacing punctuation habits

Em-dash writer; editor replaces em-dashes with commas.
Looks tidier; sounds different. Preserve the dashes if
the writer's voice runs on them.

### Removing asides

Writer parenthetically observes things — that's voice.
Editor cuts the asides for "tightness". Tightness wins;
voice loses; the piece reads professional and
forgettable.

### Apologising for voice

Writer's voice has a sharp edge; editor softens to
"avoid offence". Soft = nobody offended; nobody moved
either. Voice-edge often is the value.

## When voice IS the problem

Sometimes voice is genuinely an issue:

- **Stuck voice.** The writer's voice fits one context
  perfectly; doesn't fit any other. They write
  everything as wry-conversational; the formal report
  is awkward.
- **Mannered voice.** Voice feels affected — every
  sentence has a quirk; the quirks become tics. Less
  is more.
- **Borrowed voice.** Voice that sounds like someone
  else (a writer they read recently). Flatter; less
  distinctive than their own.

Honest signal to the writer: "This sounds like you /
sounds like X / sounds generic." Surface; don't try to
fix unilaterally.

## Helping the user find their voice

If the user asks "how do I find my voice?":

- **You have one.** Voice doesn't develop separately;
  it's already in the writing. The work is recognising
  and refining, not generating.
- **Read your old writing.** What's consistent across
  pieces, even when topics differ? That's voice.
- **Notice what you cut.** When you self-edit, what do
  you put back in? Those are voice features that fight
  to survive.
- **Don't model someone else.** Reading writers you
  admire is good; trying to write *as* them is a
  detour. Take influences; don't imitate.

## Anti-patterns

### Universal voice ideal

There's no "good voice" template. Match piece + writer +
audience. What works for a tech blog won't work for an
academic paper.

### Voice as quirk-collection

Adding quirks to "have a voice". Voice isn't a costume;
it's how you naturally write. Forced quirks read as
mannered.

### Editing voice to match editor

The editor's voice is the editor's voice; not the
writer's. Edit toward the writer's voice, even when you
disagree.

### Confusing voice with charm

Charm is a quality that some voices have. Plenty of
strong voices have no charm — sober, dry, urgent
voices. Don't push every piece toward charm.

### Killing voice for "audience fit"

Sometimes voice is appropriate for the audience even
when it seems edgy. Tech-skeptic writing for a tech
audience needs the skepticism in the voice; sanding it
off destroys the value.

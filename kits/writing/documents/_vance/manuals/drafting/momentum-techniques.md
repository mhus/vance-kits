# Momentum Techniques — keep words coming once started

The blank page is broken; the user is writing. Then the
typing slows, the sentence feels stuck, the doubts return.
This manual is about staying in motion through the middle
of a draft, not getting started.

Different from `getting-started` — these techniques assume
some text already exists.

## Don't look back

The single most reliable technique. While drafting:

- **Don't reread previous paragraphs.** They're acceptable
  by definition. Rereading invites editing; editing breaks
  flow.
- **Don't go back to fix a typo.** Note it (`[FIX TYPO]`)
  and move on; or just leave it.
- **Don't refine word choice mid-sentence.** Use the first
  word that comes; mark with `[?]` if you want to revisit.
- **Don't reorganise mid-draft.** If you suddenly want to
  move section 3 before section 2, write a note ("MOVE
  SECTION 3 EARLIER") and continue.

The discipline is hard. The reward: 3-4× speed.

## Sentence stems

When the next sentence won't come, start it with a stem and
let the rest follow:

- "What this means is..."
- "The reason for X is..."
- "But there's a complication..."
- "On the other hand..."
- "If you ask why..."
- "Concretely..."

Stems are scaffolds; the writer's brain finishes the
sentence even if the page-self can't start it.

Bad stems: "In conclusion...", "It is important to note
that...". They're filler stems; they say nothing. Use
content-stems.

## The next-word rule

When fully stuck mid-sentence: write *one word*. Any word.
The next word becomes possible because the previous one
exists.

Often the word doesn't even fit, and the user crosses it
out and writes a different one. The point is breaking the
"empty sentence" state; the bad word is a tool, not the
output.

## Scaffolding placeholders

Mid-draft, the user wants to mention X but doesn't know
the name / fact / quote. Don't stop to look it up. Write a
placeholder:

- `[CITATION TK]` — for missing references.
- `[FACT TK]` — for missing data.
- `[NAME TK]` — for missing names.
- `[BETTER PHRASE]` — for "I'll come up with something
  later".

`TK` ("to come") is the journalism convention; it's
greppable because no English word has TK in it. Useful
during the editing pass to find every gap.

## Time-boxing

A drafting session has a duration. Common pattern: 25
minutes of writing, 5 minutes break (Pomodoro). Other
patterns work; the constraint is the lever.

Within the box:

- No rereading (see "don't look back").
- No new tabs.
- No "let me check one thing".

When the timer ends:

- **Break, even if you're rolling.** Tempting to keep
  going; rejected. Breaks preserve the technique for the
  next session.
- **Save what's there.** Don't cut, don't rearrange.
  Continue next session.

Variant: "until N words". 500 words target; stop at 500.
Helpful for users who plan in word counts.

## When momentum stalls

If 5+ minutes go by without words:

### Switch from line-edit-self to draft-self

Often the user has slipped into editing without realising.
Tells: typing then deleting, choosing words slowly,
rereading the last paragraph. Surface it: "I notice you've
deleted three sentences. You're editing, not drafting.
Just write the next sentence."

### Lower the bar deliberately

"Write the worst possible next paragraph." Same trick as
in `getting-started`. Sometimes works mid-draft too.

### Skip the section

If a section won't come, leave a placeholder
("SECTION ON X TK") and write the next section. Often the
later section reveals what the stuck section actually
needed to say.

### Take a real break

Walking, hot drink, ten minutes away from the screen. The
forced 5-minute Pomodoro break is good; sometimes a
deeper break is better. Don't return to the screen
within 30 seconds of getting up — the body needs to fully
disengage to come back productive.

## What kills momentum

Avoid during drafting:

- **Tab-switching.** Email, Slack, "let me look something
  up". Each switch costs ~10 minutes of refocus.
- **Word-counter-watching.** Looking at the word count
  every paragraph slows momentum. Aim for the time-box,
  let the count be what it is.
- **Reading published examples.** "Let me see how someone
  good wrote this." Sometimes useful for `tone-and-voice`
  later; almost always destructive during drafting.
- **Asking the model to write the next sentence.** This
  skill is for the user's words. Worker delegation is a
  different request.

## Anti-patterns

### Mid-draft outlining

The user stops drafting to outline what comes next. Two
modes; one mode at a time. Either commit to outline-first
or commit to draft-first; don't oscillate.

### Editing-as-stalling

The user reverts to editing earlier paragraphs because
forward motion is hard. The earlier paragraphs are good
enough; later editing will fix them. The current sentence
is the work.

### "Just one more research item"

Drafting reveals what you need to look up. Note the gap;
keep drafting. Lookups go in the post-draft pass.

### Stopping at "I don't know"

The next sentence won't come because the user doesn't know
the answer. Write the question and continue: "(But why
does X happen? — answer this in next draft.)" The note
becomes the prompt for the next session.

## Output line

- "**Continue with:** ⟨specific stem / next word / next
  paragraph⟩."
- "**Editing detected** — switch back to drafting; mark
  ⟨specific concern⟩ as `[FIX]` and move on."
- "**Real stall** — break for ⟨specific duration⟩, then
  return."
- "**Out of momentum for today** — save and stop;
  schedule next session."

# Cutting Down — what to cut, how much, why

First drafts are 30-50 % over-length almost always.
Cutting is not loss; cutting is concentration. The piece
that survives is what stays after you remove what was
optional.

This is the highest-leverage editing pass. A pass of
careful cuts often improves a piece more than a pass of
careful word-polishing.

## What to cut, in order of cost-to-keep

### 1. Throat-clearing

Opening sentences / paragraphs that *prepare* to say
something rather than saying it.

- "It is important to note that..."
- "There are many ways to think about this..."
- "Before we begin, let me say..."
- "What I'd like to discuss in this piece is..."

Cut. The thing you'd like to discuss — just discuss it.

### 2. Repetition

Two paragraphs making the same point. Often one is
setup and one is the actual content; cut the setup.

If both paragraphs add a slight nuance, ask: is the
nuance worth a paragraph? Often the nuance fits in one
sentence somewhere; cut the second paragraph, fold the
nuance in.

### 3. Over-explanation

Same point made three different ways within a paragraph.
First-draft hedge — anxious that the reader won't catch
it. Trust the reader; cut to the cleanest version.

### 4. Qualifications

"It's worth noting that, in some cases, perhaps depending
on context, this may potentially..." — every clause
hedges. Result: the sentence asserts almost nothing.

Cut hedges aggressively. If the claim is too strong
without them, sharpen the claim — don't soften it.

### 5. Examples that don't add

Every example should make a point clearer or more vivid.
Examples that just illustrate an obvious point are
fillers.

Heuristic: if you can cut the example and the surrounding
prose still makes sense, the example wasn't pulling
weight.

### 6. Asides and digressions

The "interesting tangent" that's not on the spine of the
piece. They're hard to cut because they're often the
fun parts. They still go.

If the digression is so good you can't bear to cut: that's
its own piece. Save it; cut from this one.

### 7. Adverbs that weaken

"Very", "really", "quite", "rather", "extremely",
"actually", "basically". Most readings are crisper
without them.

Not always. "Really" can be voice; "actually" can mark a
real surprise. Cut by default; restore where the word
earns its space.

### 8. "There is" / "There are" openings

"There is a problem with this approach." → "This approach
has a problem."

Most "there is" sentences can be re-cast tighter. Not all
— sometimes the existential is the right move. Default to
cutting.

### 9. Long sentences that should be two

A sentence with three clauses joined by "and" / "but" /
"which" / commas can almost always become two clearer
sentences. Long sentences earn their length when the
length carries meaning (cumulative effect, build-up); not
when length is just stuffed.

### 10. Weak verbs with adjectives

"Made a decision" → "decided". "Came to the realisation"
→ "realised". "Has the ability to" → "can". Verb-noun
constructions hide the verb; restore it.

## How much to cut

Aim 30 % shorter on the structural pass. Not arbitrary —
a number. With a target, you cut deeply; without a
target, you trim cosmetically.

If 30 % feels excessive: try 20 %. Almost any draft can
lose 20 % without losing meaning.

If after 30 % cuts the piece feels gutted: you cut the
wrong parts. Restore selectively. The first round of cuts
should hurt; if it doesn't hurt, you didn't cut enough.

## What NOT to cut

### Voice

The tic that's distinctively yours. The unusual sentence
shape, the punctuation choice, the surprising aside.
Voice is what distinguishes your writing from generic
content.

Editing for voice-removal produces text that's
"professional" and forgettable.

### Specifics

"At 3:14 a.m. on a Tuesday in November" reads better
than "late at night". Precision is value; vague is
filler. Cut vague, keep specific.

### Examples that earned

If an example clinched a point the reader will remember,
keep. Example that's working is worth real estate.

### Repetition that's deliberate

Repetition for emphasis (as in this very sentence). Some
writers repeat for rhythm, for climbing rhetorical
structures (anaphora). That's not the cut-able kind.

### Counter-arguments

The objection-and-response pattern feels like it could
be cut ("we don't *need* to address that objection"). It
usually shouldn't be. Engaging with counters is what
distinguishes argument from sermon.

### Asides that are voice

Some writers' asides are the best part of the writing.
They aren't structural; they're the texture. Don't cut
because they're "off-topic" — the off-topic is the
appeal.

## How to do the pass

### Read with a delete key in hand

Go through the draft. For each sentence, ask: if I cut
this, would anything be lost? If "no", cut. Move on.

Don't re-read after each cut. Trust forward motion.

### Macro then micro

Pass 1: paragraph-level cuts. Drop entire paragraphs
that don't earn space.

Pass 2: sentence-level cuts. Drop sentences that don't
add.

Pass 3: word-level cuts. Drop adverbs, hedges,
filler-phrases.

Three passes is faster than one all-levels pass — each
pass is a focused mode, less context-switching.

### Read what's left

After cutting, read the piece end-to-end. Two reactions:

- "This still says everything important and reads
  faster." → ship the cuts.
- "I cut something I needed; the piece is now thin." →
  selective restore. Don't restore the full text.

## Anti-patterns

### Cutting timidly

Removing one adverb per paragraph. Pretty; useless. Cut
in chunks.

### "I worked hard on this" preservation

Effort isn't a reason to keep text. Cut what doesn't
serve the reader, regardless of how much effort went in.

### Cutting the wrong things

Cutting the specifics ("readers won't care about the
detail") and keeping the abstractions. Backwards. Cut
the abstractions; keep specifics.

### Length-bragging

"I cut the piece from 4000 to 2800 words." Length isn't
the metric; quality is. A 2800-word piece can be worse
than the 4000-word version if you cut wrong.

### Cutting after audience-fit

Cut for the piece's intent, not for an imagined "shorter
is better" goal. Some pieces want length (think-pieces;
academic essays). Most pieces want concentration.

# Weak Words and Fillers — specific cuts that improve

A list of words and phrases that almost always weaken
prose. Cuttable in 80%+ of appearances. Not absolute —
voice and context matter — but worth a pass.

## Hedge adverbs

These soften claims without earning the softening:

- "very"
- "really"
- "quite"
- "rather"
- "somewhat"
- "fairly"
- "pretty" (as in "pretty good")
- "extremely"
- "truly"
- "literally" (when used as intensifier, not literally)

Test: cut. Read. Same meaning? Ship the cut.

Exception: when the hedge marks genuine uncertainty
("the result is somewhat surprising — we're not sure
why"). The hedge does work; keep.

## Filler adverbs

These add nothing:

- "actually"
- "basically"
- "essentially"
- "in fact"
- "of course"
- "obviously"
- "clearly"
- "naturally"
- "indeed"
- "in essence"
- "fundamentally"

Cut. Almost always.

Exception: when the adverb genuinely contrasts (a thing
that's *actually* true vs. what was assumed). Keep.

## Throat-clearing phrases

Openings that prepare to say something:

- "It is important to note that..."
- "It should be noted that..."
- "It is worth mentioning that..."
- "I would like to point out that..."
- "What's interesting is that..."
- "The fact of the matter is..."

Cut the throat-clearing; start with the content.

## Time-marker fillers

- "At this point in time" → "now".
- "In the very near future" → "soon".
- "On a daily basis" → "daily".
- "At the present moment" → "now".

Pure inflation. Cut to the simple word.

## Verbal fillers

- "in order to" → "to" (almost always).
- "due to the fact that" → "because".
- "for the purpose of" → "for" or "to".
- "with regard to" → "about" or "regarding".
- "in light of the fact that" → "since".
- "in spite of the fact that" → "although" or "despite".
- "in the event that" → "if".

These are bureaucratic inflations of one-word
prepositions. Cut.

## Redundant pairs

Two words doing one word's work:

- "each and every" → "every" or "each".
- "free gift" → "gift".
- "advance planning" → "planning".
- "added bonus" → "bonus".
- "end result" → "result".
- "final outcome" → "outcome".
- "future plans" → "plans".
- "past history" → "history".
- "true facts" → "facts".
- "absolutely essential" → "essential".

Cut one of the pair; keep what does the work.

## "There is" / "There are" openings

Often refactor-able to a more direct sentence:

- "There are three reasons for this." → "This has three
  reasons." or just list them.
- "There is a tendency for X to..." → "X tends to..."
- "There exists a relationship between A and B." →
  "A and B are related."

Sometimes "there is" is the right move (existence is the
point); usually it's a default starter that pads.

## "It is" openings

Same problem:

- "It is necessary to consider X." → "Consider X."
- "It is the case that X." → "X." (just say X.)
- "It is important to remember that..." → "Remember
  that..." (or cut entirely).

## Verb-noun nominalisations

Hidden verbs trapped in nouns:

- "make a decision" → "decide".
- "give consideration to" → "consider".
- "hold a discussion" → "discuss".
- "perform an analysis" → "analyse".
- "reach a conclusion" → "conclude".
- "have an impact on" → "affect" or "impact".
- "make use of" → "use".
- "engage in" → "do" / a more specific verb.

Verb-noun is a sign of bureaucratic register; restoring
the verb makes the sentence direct.

## Weakening qualifiers before claims

- "It seems that..."
- "It appears that..."
- "I think that..."
- "It would seem..."
- "There is a possibility that..."

If you mean to hedge: "X may happen." Direct.

If you don't mean to hedge: just state the claim
without prefix.

If you're unsure about the claim itself, that's a
content issue, not a wording issue. Either claim or
don't; the prefix doesn't fix uncertainty.

## "The fact that"

A construction that almost always indicates an over-long
formulation:

- "The fact that he was late annoyed me." → "His being
  late annoyed me." Or: "He was late, which annoyed
  me." Or: "I was annoyed that he was late."

Three rewrites; pick what fits the rhythm. Cut "the
fact that".

## Empty intensifiers

Words used to make a sentence "stronger" but actually
make it weaker:

- "absolutely"
- "totally"
- "completely"
- "definitely"
- "entirely"
- "thoroughly"
- "incredibly"
- "extraordinarily"

The unhedged version is usually stronger:

- "I'm absolutely sure" → "I'm sure". (Stronger because
  more direct.)
- "It's totally clear that..." → "It's clear that..."

Adverb-intensifiers feel stronger to the writer; they
read weaker to the reader.

## Audience-flattering filler

- "as you know"
- "as we all know"
- "as everyone is aware"

Either the reader knows (cut the filler) or doesn't
(restate the fact properly).

## Self-narration

- "I'm going to argue..."
- "What I want to say is..."
- "Let me tell you..."
- "What follows is..."

Sometimes useful for orientation in long pieces; mostly
filler. Just argue the thing; don't announce that you
will.

## Connective filler

- "First and foremost"
- "Last but not least"
- "On a related note"
- "It goes without saying"
- "Needless to say"
- "Suffice it to say"

Mostly junk. "Suffice it to say" especially is a tell —
the writer has decided not to say something and is
hoping the reader won't notice.

Cut.

## How to do the pass

### Pattern-grep (mental or actual)

For each item above, search the draft. Each hit:
inspect. Most are cuttable.

### Watch for clusters

When a sentence has 3+ weak words, often the whole
sentence is weak — restructure rather than cut
individual words.

### Don't strip every one

A piece with zero adverbs reads stiff. Voice tolerates
some adverbs. Cut by default; restore the ones that
clearly earn space.

### After-pass read aloud

Read aloud after the pass. The piece should feel
crisper. If it feels gutted: you cut the wrong ones,
restore selectively.

## Anti-patterns

### Robotic pass

Cutting every "really" / "very" / "actually" without
reading. Sometimes those words are doing work. Read
in context.

### Replacing weak word with weak word

"Very good" → "extremely good". Same weakness,
different syllables. Restructure or cut.

### Substituting jargon for filler

"Make a decision" → "ideate on a strategic alignment
forward". No. Use "decide".

### Cutting down to telegraphic prose

Strip enough fillers and you can produce text that
reads telegraphic. "Cut adjectives. Now lacks colour.
Result mechanical." Keep enough texture for prose to
feel like prose.

- "**Voice-driven retention** — kept ⟨specific⟩ as

- "**No more weak-word cuts** — proceed to
  ⟨tone-and-voice / final read⟩."

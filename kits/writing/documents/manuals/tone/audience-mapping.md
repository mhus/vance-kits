# Audience Mapping — knowing who the piece is for

Before you can tune tone, you have to know who's reading.
"For developers" or "for executives" is too coarse — the
useful audience definition is sharper than that.

This manual is about getting the audience clear enough
that register decisions become obvious.

## What an audience definition looks like

Coarse: "engineers".

Sharp: "engineers who weren't on the team, opening a
postmortem to understand whether they should worry about
the same failure on their service".

Coarse: "marketing prospects".

Sharp: "marketing leads at SMBs, three months into
trying solutions, mildly skeptical, looking for a
2-minute read that gives them a confident next step".

The sharp version drives:

- **Length** the audience tolerates.
- **Vocabulary** they understand.
- **Register** they expect.
- **What they'll skim** vs. read carefully.
- **What questions they bring** to the piece.

Coarse audience definitions don't drive these — too many
plausible answers per audience descriptor.

## Useful axes to map

### Knowledge

- **What they know already.** Domain knowledge,
  jargon comfort, history.
- **What they want to know after reading.** A specific
  takeaway? A general orientation? An emotional
  experience?
- **What's the gap between what they know and what they
  want to know.** That gap is the piece.

If the audience already knows everything in the piece:
useless to them. If the gap is too big to bridge in one
piece: the piece is wrong-sized for the audience.

### Disposition toward the topic

- **Eager.** They sought the piece out; receptive.
- **Skeptical.** They're judging; expect resistance.
- **Hostile.** They disagree before reading; need
  conviction.
- **Bored.** They have to read this for some reason;
  hold their attention or lose them.

Different dispositions warrant different opening
strategies. Eager audience: jump in. Skeptical
audience: surface common ground first. Hostile audience:
acknowledge the disagreement openly. Bored audience:
give them a reason to keep reading by sentence three.

### Reading context

- **Where is this read?** Phone in subway / desk during
  work / laptop in evening / printed handout.
- **How much time will they spend?** 30 seconds /
  3 minutes / 30 minutes.
- **What state are they in?** Tired / focused /
  multitasking.

Reading context drives length and structure. Subway
phone reading wants 200-word digestible chunks; evening
laptop reading tolerates 3000-word essays.

### Relationship to writer

- **Stranger.** Audience doesn't know who you are; you
  have to establish credibility.
- **Acquaintance.** Some shared context; you can lean on
  it lightly.
- **Insider.** Shared jargon, references, history; you
  can move fast.
- **Subordinate / boss / peer.** Power dynamics affect
  register.

Stranger-audience writing has to do more orientation
work. Insider writing is dense in shared references;
sounds wrong if read by an outsider.

### Stakes

- **Optional.** They could stop reading at any time;
  hold them.
- **Required.** They have to read this (memo at work);
  they'll skim if you don't reward attention.
- **Decision-driving.** They'll act based on this; clarity
  is critical.
- **Reference.** They'll come back to it; structure
  matters more than flow.

Stakes drive the cost of unclear writing. Optional reading:
unclear = dropped. Required reading: unclear =
misinterpreted. Decision-driving: unclear = wrong action.

## Common audience-mismatch patterns

### Writing for the in-group when the audience is broader

The piece assumes shared context, jargon, references that
the actual audience doesn't have.

Tells:

- Acronyms unexplained on first use.
- "As we all know" / "obviously" before non-obvious
  things.
- References to past internal decisions without context.

Fix: define for the broadest plausible reader; use
asides parenthetically for in-group nuances. Or split
into two pieces — one for in-group, one for outside.

### Writing for the wrong sophistication level

Either:

- **Too elementary.** Audience is sophisticated; piece
  explains basics they know. Patronising.
- **Too advanced.** Audience is general; piece dives into
  technical detail without bridge.

Fix: probe what the audience already knows; calibrate.
Drop the explanations they don't need; add bridges where
they need them.

### Wrong register for context

A casual blog post in formal corporate-speak. A serious
incident report in jokey conversational tone.

Fix: match register to topic + audience expectation. See
`register-and-tone`.

### Forgetting the audience entirely

The piece is what the writer wanted to say, with no
modulation for who reads it. Common when the writer is
processing their own thoughts and treats the piece as a
journal.

Fix: ask "what would the reader do with this?" If no
clear answer, the piece is for the writer, not the
reader. Maybe that's fine (personal essay; journal); if
you wanted reach, you have to factor in reader needs.

## How to do an audience pass

### State the audience in writing

Before re-reading the draft, write down (or say aloud)
the sharp audience definition. Three to five sentences.

Then read the draft *as* that audience. Where you, as
the audience, would skim, get bored, get lost, feel
talked-down-to, feel out-of-depth — those are mismatch
spots.

### Bring up specific reactions

Don't just ask "is this clear?" — ask:

- "Where would the reader stop?"
- "What's the first sentence the reader doesn't believe?"
- "What's a question this leaves unanswered for the
  reader?"
- "What's a sentence that sounds wrong for this
  audience?"

Specific questions surface specific issues.

### Use a real test reader

Best signal: actual reader from the audience reads it
and tells you. Most useful question: "Where did you
get bored / lost / annoyed?" not "What do you think?"

If no real reader available: imagine someone specific
from the target audience. Imagine *them* reading;
imagine *their* reactions.

## Anti-patterns

### "Universal" audience

"This is for everyone." Almost no piece is for everyone.
Trying to write for everyone produces writing for nobody
in particular — flat register, vague claims, generic
voice.

### Fictional ideal reader

A reader who's perfectly attentive, infinitely patient,
familiar with everything. Doesn't exist. Real readers
have less patience and less context than you imagine.

### Audience-as-excuse

"My audience wouldn't get that." Sometimes accurate;
often a hedge to avoid hard ideas. Trust the audience
more than you reflexively want to.

### Register-imposing

"My audience expects formal English." Maybe; maybe they
expect formal *content* and tolerate (or appreciate) less
formal *register*. The two aren't the same.

### Audience as one person

"I'm writing for ⟨specific person⟩." Useful sometimes
(memo to one boss); usually too narrow if the piece is
public. Generalise to the type while keeping specificity.

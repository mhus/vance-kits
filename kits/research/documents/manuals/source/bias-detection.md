# Bias Detection — surface bias without disqualifying

Every source has bias. Some biases distort more than others.
The goal isn't to eliminate biased sources from your
research — that would leave nothing — but to understand the
biases, weight accordingly, and seek balancing perspectives.

A heavily-biased source can still be the best source for a
specific factual claim. A balanced-seeming source can be
biased by what it leaves out.

## Bias types

### Selection bias

The source's evidence is non-representative because of
how it was gathered.

- **Survey of users who finished the product** —
  excludes those who quit.
- **Reviews from a website where critics are
  self-selected** — skews toward the dissatisfied or
  the enthusiast.
- **Published studies only** — publication bias; null
  results often unpublished.
- **News reporting on dramatic outcomes** — boring
  outcomes don't make news, leaving an over-represented
  picture of dramatic ones.

Test: ask "what's missing?". What kind of evidence /
perspective / case is systematically *not* in the data?

### Confirmation bias

The source / author preferentially noticed and amplified
evidence supporting a prior; underweighted evidence
against it.

Tells:

- Lopsided citations — eight sources for the favoured
  side, one straw-man for the other.
- Counter-evidence framed as exceptions or "yet to be
  understood".
- The author's prior was clear before the analysis
  started.

Test: imagine a smart, honest person on the other side
of the question. Would they accept the source's framing?
What evidence would they cite that the source ignores?

### Motivated reasoning

The source has a stake (financial, ideological,
identity, career) in a particular conclusion. Reasoning
is shaped to land there.

- Industry-funded study finds in favour of the
  industry.
- Activist writer reaches the activist conclusion.
- Researcher needs result X for their grant / theory /
  career.

Doesn't disqualify; raises the bar. A motivated source
that nonetheless reaches a conclusion *against* its
motivation is unusually strong evidence (skin in the
game cuts the other way).

### Methodological bias

The methods chosen tilt the result.

- **Survey wording.** "Do you think there should be
  *more* funding for X?" vs. "Do you think funding for
  X should *increase or decrease*?"
- **Sampling.** Web survey on a politics blog samples
  only that blog's readers.
- **Analysis choices.** Multiple comparisons without
  correction; arbitrary cutoffs; outlier handling that
  flips results.
- **Pre-registration absence.** Choosing what to test
  after seeing the data.

Methodology bias often shows in the methods section
itself; sometimes you have to infer.

### Funding / sponsor bias

The funder's interests shape what gets studied, how, and
which results get published.

- Industry funding → industry-favourable conclusions
  more often than chance.
- Government funding → may have ideological tilt of the
  agency.
- Foundation funding → matches the foundation's mission.

Disclosure helps; the absence of disclosure is itself a
signal.

### Ideological bias

The source's ideology shapes what counts as evidence,
what frames are used, what arguments are taken seriously.

- Some publications are explicitly partisan; bias is
  declared.
- Some present as neutral but consistently land on one
  side.
- Some are sincerely neutral about the question but
  carry implicit assumptions of the writer's milieu.

Hardest to spot in sources whose ideology matches your
own. Easiest in sources whose ideology you disagree with.
This asymmetry is itself a bias to notice.

### Survivorship bias

Conclusions drawn only from the cases that "survived"
the selection process.

- "Successful founders all dropped out of college" —
  ignores the larger pool of college dropouts who didn't
  succeed.
- "Veterans of war say it built character" — the ones it
  destroyed aren't speaking.
- "These habits work because all top performers do them"
  — top performers also do many other things; the habits
  may be incidental.

Test: where are the cases that didn't make the data set?
What was different about them?

### Recency bias

Recent information is over-weighted relative to older.

- "Crime is rising" based on three months of headlines —
  may not be visible in long-term data.
- "Everyone is using technology X now" based on this
  quarter's hype.

Test: extend the time horizon. Does the claim hold over
years, not weeks?

### Anchoring bias

The first number / framing seen biases all subsequent
estimates.

- Sources that lead with a dramatic number anchor the
  reader.
- Negotiations, polling, journalism all use anchors.

Test: imagine the same data presented with a different
opening. Does the conclusion still feel obvious?

## How to detect bias

### Read multiple sources on the same topic

Bias often shows by comparison. One source says X is the
key issue; another barely mentions X. The asymmetry
flags both as having framing biases.

### Read sources with declared positions

A source that openly says "we advocate for X" is more
honest than a source that pretends neutrality while
advocating for X. Easier to weight.

### Watch for "however" patterns

Many sources structure as "Most agree X. However, some
argue Y. (X is implied better.)" The "however" position
is often pre-set.

Test: which side gets a paragraph, which gets a bullet?
Which gets the closing rebuttal?

### Look at citation profile

Who does the source cite? Mostly each other (echo
chamber)? Mostly opposed (engaged disagreement)? Mostly
neutral primaries (good)?

### Track the funding model

Who pays for the source's existence? Their interests
shape what gets emphasised.

## How to use a biased source anyway

Biased ≠ useless. Steps:

1. **Identify the bias.** Name it specifically.
2. **Identify what the source is reliable for despite
   the bias.** Often the factual claims are sound; the
   framing / interpretation is biased.
3. **Use the source for those parts.**
4. **Find a counter-biased source for what's
   shaped by the bias.**
5. **Triangulate.** Where the biases would push in
   different directions but the sources agree, the
   agreement is strong evidence.

This is how political scientists use partisan polling
data, how investors use industry research, how
historians use primary documents from biased witnesses.

## When bias rises to disqualifying

Some biases push past "weight differently" into "don't
rely":

- **Active deception.** Source has been caught
  fabricating; not a misjudgement bias, an integrity
  problem.
- **Total opacity about methods + reasons to suspect
  motivation.** Can't evaluate the work; suspicion
  warranted.
- **Pattern of retracted / debunked claims** in the
  domain.
- **Source funded by undisclosed party** with strong
  stake in the conclusion (when discovered).

These warrant dropping the source, not just discounting.

## How NOT to deploy bias detection

### Bias-cancellation as goal

"Source X has left bias; source Y has right bias; truth
must be in the middle." False symmetry. Sometimes one
side is largely correct; truth isn't a midpoint by
default.

### Disqualification by association

"This is published by group X, which has biases on
unrelated topic Y, so I dismiss X on this topic too."
Bias is per-topic; evaluate the specific claim.

### Ignoring bias on familiar sources

Bias-detection is for sources you don't know well. The
sources you've trusted for years probably also have
biases — apply the same lens to them.

### Bias as gotcha

Surfacing bias to dismiss the inconvenient finding. "Of
course, the funder benefits from this conclusion" —
true but not a refutation. Engage with the substance.

### Bias as armour

"My source is biased, but the biases of opposing
sources are worse, so my source wins." The asymmetric
trust isn't justified by the asymmetric criticism.

## Anti-patterns

### Treating "balanced sources" as bias-free

Balanced framing has biases too — false equivalence,
both-sidesing genuinely-asymmetric debates, framing
controversies as 50/50 when consensus is 90/10.

### Treating "expert" as bias-proof

Experts have funding pressures, in-group loyalties,
career stakes, methodological commitments. "An expert
said it" is data; not the end of the inquiry.

### Treating "data-driven" as bias-proof

Data choices reflect methodology bias. "The data
shows..." conclusions still depend on which data, how
collected, how analysed.

### Self-exempting

Forgetting your own biases. The strongest possible bias
is the one you can't see in yourself.

## Output line

- "**Bias detected:** ⟨specific type⟩. **Use source for:**
  ⟨specific claims⟩. **Don't rely for:** ⟨specific
  claims⟩."
- "**Multiple biases compound** — find balancing source
  before concluding."
- "**Bias rises to disqualifying** — drop source; find
  alternative for ⟨specific claim⟩."
- "**Bias understood; calibration noted** — proceed."

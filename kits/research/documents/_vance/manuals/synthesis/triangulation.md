# Triangulation — multiple independent sources for the same claim

A claim supported by one source is a single-source claim.
A claim supported by multiple *independent* sources is
much stronger. The strengthening comes from independence
— five sources copying each other don't triangulate.

This manual is about doing triangulation right and
recognising when it's failing.

## What independence means

Two sources are independent if their support for a claim
doesn't trace to a common origin.

Examples of independent sources:

- Smith (2024 cohort study) + Jones (2025 RCT) — two
  separate empirical studies on the same question.
- A government statistics agency + an industry survey —
  different methodologies, different funders.
- A Wikipedia article citing primary sources + the
  primary sources themselves — independent if you check
  the primaries, not if you only read Wikipedia.

Examples of *non*-independent sources (often mistaken
for independent):

- Smith 2024 + a blog post citing Smith 2024.
- Five news outlets all running the same press release.
- Three textbooks summarising the same canonical paper.
- A survey paper + the original studies it surveys
  (counted both — double-counting).
- Two papers from the same research group using the same
  dataset with slightly different analyses.
- AI-generated summaries citing each other.

## Why independence matters

If sources copy each other (or a common origin), errors
propagate. The original may be wrong; every copy
inherits the error. Counting copies doesn't validate the
original.

Independent sources fail in *different* ways. If three
methodologically different sources reach the same
conclusion, they're unlikely to all be wrong in the
same way. That's the strengthening.

## How to test independence

For each pair of sources supporting a claim:

1. **Different authors / institutions?** First filter.
2. **Different methodologies?** Survey + experiment +
   observational data is more robust than three surveys.
3. **Different time periods?** A recent finding plus a
   1990s finding suggests durability.
4. **Different populations / domains?** Same finding in
   US + EU + Asia suggests generalisability.
5. **Each source's citations on this claim — different
   primary origins?** Or do they all trace back to the
   same paper?

If all five "yes" → strongly independent. If most "no" →
weakly independent or actually dependent.

## What triangulation produces

### Strong triangulation — wide agreement

Three or more independent sources, varied methods, varied
populations, all reach a similar conclusion.

You can write the claim with confidence. Stating the
conclusion + a footnote of representative sources is
enough.

### Moderate triangulation

Two independent sources agree; a third is silent (not
contradicting; just not addressing).

Write with confidence; note that more direct evidence
would strengthen. Often the right level for "established
finding".

### Weak triangulation

Two sources agree but use the same methodology / share
authors / are close in time.

Better than one source; not as strong as it looks. Treat
as a single piece of evidence with a slightly raised
confidence; don't extrapolate.

### Asymmetric agreement / disagreement

Three sources support; one (well-conducted) contradicts.

Don't dismiss the contradicting source. Ask: under what
conditions might the contradicting source be right? What
makes it different? Often the contradiction is hiding a
real nuance (different population; different definition;
different time period).

### Pseudo-triangulation

A claim supported by what looks like multiple sources but
all trace back to one origin.

Worth detecting because the claim isn't really
triangulated. Often appears in popular-press claims:
"studies show X" cited by five outlets, all sourcing one
study.

## Detecting pseudo-triangulation

Click through to primary sources for each "agreeing"
secondary source. If they all trace to the same primary,
you have one source, not five.

Heuristic: if all the agreeing sources came out around
the same time, suspect a single underlying primary
(everyone reporting the same release).

## The triangulation traps

### Citation echo chamber

A community of sources mutually citing each other
without any of them grounded in primary work. Common in
ideological niches; easy to mistake for "the field
agrees".

Counter: identify the primary work. If the network has no
primary, the agreement is air.

### Methodological monoculture

All "independent" sources use the same flawed method.
Twenty studies all using a biased survey instrument
agree — but the bias is in the instrument.

Counter: cross-method triangulation is stronger than
intra-method.

### Sample / population monoculture

All studies done on US college students. The finding
agrees within that population; doesn't generalise to
others without further work.

Counter: when the question is general, demand sources
across populations; treat single-population findings
as scoped.

### Recency-only triangulation

All sources from the past two years agreeing. The
agreement may be because everyone read the same recent
papers, not because the finding has been independently
validated.

Counter: include older sources when available; durability
is part of strength.

### "Everyone knows" triangulation

A claim so widely repeated that nobody questions it; can't
locate the original source. Sometimes called "lazy
consensus".

Counter: if you can't trace to primary, treat as
unverified, regardless of how widely it's repeated.

## When you can't triangulate

Sometimes the question demands a claim that only one
source addresses well. The credibility ladder + trust
signals + bias detection bear most of the weight.

Be honest in the synthesis: "Smith 2024 is currently the
only study on X. Replication would strengthen
confidence." Acknowledge the single-source state; don't
inflate.

## Triangulation in qualitative research

Qualitative work has its own triangulation:

- **Different methods** (interviews + observation +
  document analysis).
- **Different participants / cases.**
- **Different researchers** independently coding the same
  data.
- **Different theoretical lenses.**

Same principle: agreement across genuinely different
approaches strengthens.

## When NOT to demand triangulation

- **Routine factual claims.** "The capital of France is
  Paris" doesn't need three sources.
- **Definitions.** A term's standard meaning is not a
  claim that needs triangulation.
- **Logical / mathematical claims.** Proof, not
  triangulation, is the relevant standard.
- **Author's own observations / experience.** "I tried
  X and it worked for me" doesn't claim
  generalisability; doesn't need corroboration to be
  honestly stated.

## Anti-patterns

### Triangulation as box-checking

"I have three citations" → call it triangulated. Three
non-independent citations isn't triangulation.

### Numerical inflation

Counting all citations as evidence; ignoring quality
and independence. "Cited by 50" isn't "independently
supported by 50".

### Selective triangulation

Triangulating the parts that support a prior; not
triangulating the parts that don't. Apply the lens
symmetrically.

### Impossible-standard demand

Demanding triangulation in domains where it's
genuinely scarce (single-occurrence historical events;
unique geological features). Use the highest standard
the domain supports; be honest about limitations.

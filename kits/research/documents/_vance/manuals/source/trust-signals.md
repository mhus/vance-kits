# Trust Signals — what to look at on the source itself

The credibility ladder gives you the source-type. Trust
signals tell you whether *this specific source* is good
within its type. A peer-reviewed paper from a discredited
author isn't a strong source even though peer-reviewed
papers usually are.

This manual is a checklist of what to inspect.

## Author / institution

### Who is the author?

- **Real-name attribution?** Anonymous sources can be
  fine in journalism (when explained), suspect in research.
- **Track record?** Has the author been right before?
  Wrong before? On this topic specifically?
- **Domain expertise?** Are they writing in their field
  or out of it? PhD in physics writing about economics
  is just a smart amateur in economics.
- **Conflict of interest?** Funded by parties with stake
  in the answer? Owning the company they're "analysing"?
- **Pattern of retractions / corrections?** Some authors
  have a history; checks like Retraction Watch surface
  it.

### Institution / publisher

- **Reputation in the field.** Different from general
  reputation. A specific journal can be top in one
  sub-field and weak in another.
- **Editorial standards.** Are corrections published
  prominently, or buried? Are retractions noted on the
  original article, or just removed?
- **Independence.** Wholly-owned by an entity with
  positions on the subject? Funded by donors who
  benefit from particular conclusions?

If you can't identify the author or the publisher takes
no editorial responsibility, calibrate down significantly.

## Date and currency

### When was this published?

- A 2008 paper claiming "consumers prefer X over Y" may
  no longer hold.
- A 1995 textbook on cryptography is dangerously
  obsolete.
- A 2021 piece on the LLM landscape is ancient.

Currency depends on field velocity. Maths from 1900 is
mostly current; CS from 2015 may be obsolete; biology of
the brain is in flux.

### Has it been updated?

- Some articles get edited as new information comes in
  (good, but check the change log if available).
- Wikipedia updates often (sometimes too often, in
  edit-war contexts).
- Some sources publish "v2", "revision 3" indicating
  evolution.

A 5-year-old paper still being cited in current work is a
positive signal; the field considers it foundational. A
10-year-old paper not cited recently may be superseded —
check.

## Citations within the source

### Does the source cite its sources?

- **Yes, with traceable references** → can be evaluated
  layer-down.
- **Citations to itself / sister publications only** →
  echo chamber; chase outside.
- **No citations on factual claims** → red flag,
  especially in academic / journalistic contexts.
- **"Studies show that..." with no citation** → at best
  lazy, at worst false.

### Are the citations accurate?

For high-stakes claims, click through. Common patterns:

- **Cited paper says the opposite.** Common in
  motivated writing.
- **Cited paper has been retracted.** The source didn't
  notice / didn't update.
- **Cited paper exists but says something nuanced; the
  citing source took a one-line summary that mis-
  represents.** Especially common when journalism cites
  research.
- **Cited paper is in a predatory journal.** Existence
  of a citation isn't enough; chase the cited source
  itself.

If you can sample-check 3-5 citations and they all hold
up, you can extend trust to the rest. If 2 of 5 misrepresent,
extend skepticism throughout.

## Replication, corrections, and retractions

### Is the finding replicated?

For empirical claims, replication is the gold-standard
trust signal. A study with a single result has weaker
evidential weight than a meta-analysis of 30 studies.

Replication standards differ by field:

- **Physics, chemistry:** typical to replicate.
- **Psychology, social sciences:** replication crisis;
  treat single-study findings cautiously.
- **Medicine:** replication uneven; meta-analyses
  important.
- **Economics:** difficult to replicate; treat with care.

### Has the source been corrected or retracted?

- Visible erratum / correction → professional source;
  good signal.
- Silent fix without note → less professional.
- Retracted but still online without retraction notice →
  bad sign (active misinformation).
- Retraction Watch is a useful check for academic
  papers.

## Methodological transparency

For empirical / data-driven sources, look for:

- **Methods section.** Detailed enough to replicate?
  Vague?
- **Sample size and selection.** Disclosed? Justified?
- **Conflicts of interest disclosed.**
- **Data availability.** Is the underlying data
  published / shared on request?
- **Pre-registration.** For studies; pre-registered
  hypotheses are stronger evidence than post-hoc
  ones.
- **Statistical methods.** Standard or custom? Custom
  raises questions.

A source can be right while opaque about methods, but
you cannot evaluate the source without knowing how the
result was produced.

## Internal consistency

### Does the source contradict itself?

Long pieces sometimes have internal contradictions.
Surface them; pick a side or note the inconsistency.

### Are claims proportional to evidence?

The "headline says everything; body says modest finding"
pattern. Especially common in journalism summarising
research.

If the headline / abstract / executive summary claims
strongly more than the body supports, calibrate down to
what the body actually shows.

### Are uncertainties acknowledged?

A source that pretends certainty in genuinely uncertain
domains is signalling something — usually overconfidence
or motivated framing.

A source that says "we don't yet know X; the data
suggests Y but with limitations" reads more carefully.

## Funding and conflicts of interest

Look for the funding model:

- **Public university / government grant** — usually
  least conflicted, though not without ideology.
- **Industry-funded research** — financial interest in
  particular outcomes. Doesn't disqualify; warrants
  scrutiny.
- **Think tank / advocacy organisation** — agenda-driven;
  often produces good work in service of a particular
  framing.
- **Commercial publication / outlet** — driven by
  attention; sometimes accuracy-aligned, sometimes not.

The funder is a hypothesis to test, not a verdict. Some
industry-funded research is excellent; some
government-funded research is weak. Use as input, not as
disqualifier.

## Specific source types

### Academic paper

- Peer-reviewed?
- Journal reputation in the sub-field?
- Author affiliations and history?
- Methods section detailed?
- Replication / preregistration?
- Citations both ways (who cites it, what does it
  cite)?

### News article

- Reporter's beat (do they cover this regularly)?
- Named sources or anonymous (and why)?
- Original reporting or aggregation?
- Date; updates if a developing story?
- Corrections published?

### Blog / Substack

- Author's track record on this topic?
- Citations to primary sources?
- History of corrections?

### Government report

- Agency's track record on this topic?
- Political pressure on the agency?
- Data backing the report (often appendix)?

### Twitter / social

- Domain expert? Track record?
- Quote-tweets / threads with sourcing, or hot take?
- Subject to platform-specific manipulation patterns
  (bots, brigading)?

### Wikipedia

- Article quality varies hugely.
- Check the talk page for active disputes.
- Click through to citations.
- Treat as orientation; not authority.

## Anti-patterns

### Aesthetic-driven trust

A clean PDF feels more credible than a hand-typed page;
not a substantive signal. Substance over polish.

### Author-disclaimer-driven trust

"I'm not a doctor / lawyer / expert, but..." disclaimers
sometimes signal honesty, sometimes signal "this is
unreliable but I'm telling you anyway". Read the
content.

### Brand-driven trust

"It's in the New York Times." Most NYT pieces are well-
reported; some have been wrong; the quality varies by
beat and writer. Brand-as-shorthand is sometimes useful
but not a substitute.

### Single-signal evaluation

Looking only at credentials, or only at citations, or
only at funding. Multiple signals together are more
informative than any one.

### Trust-by-association

"Cited by source-I-trust" → therefore trustworthy. The
trusted source may have made a one-time mistake. Click
through.

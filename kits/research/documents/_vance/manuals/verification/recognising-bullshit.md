# Recognising Bullshit — patterns of false / misleading claims

"Bullshit" here is technical: language that's
indifferent to truth (Frankfurt's definition). The
speaker isn't trying to lie; they're not trying to be
right either. They want a particular response from the
audience.

This produces patterns. Recognising them lets you
calibrate skepticism without having to fully verify
every claim.

## Brandolini's law

> *"The amount of energy needed to refute bullshit is an
> order of magnitude bigger than that needed to produce
> it."* — Alberto Brandolini

This means you cannot fact-check exhaustively against an
adversary who manufactures bullshit faster than you can
debunk. Allocate skeptical energy:

- Strong skepticism: claims that match known bullshit
  patterns (below).
- Standard skepticism: claims from less-trusted sources.
- Light skepticism: claims from well-trusted sources on
  their topics.

## Common patterns

### Statistic that's too clean

A real statistic from a real study has noise: 67.3%, or
a confidence interval of "23-31%", or "between 1.4 and
2.1 million". A bullshit number is suspiciously clean:
"exactly 90%", "1 million", "doubled".

Test: search the specific number. If it appears widely
without a source, suspicious.

### "Studies show" without citation

Generic appeal to studies; no specific study named. The
author either:

- Doesn't know the study (using the appeal as a rhetorical
  shortcut).
- Made it up.
- Misremembers.

A real "studies show" claim cites the studies.

### Surprisingly precise unverifiable detail

"68% of millennials would rather X than Y". A specific
figure, hard to verify, plausible-sounding.

The precision is part of the rhetoric — specifics signal
"someone measured this". Often nobody did.

### Misattributed quotations

"As Einstein said, ..." — most famous attributions to
Einstein / Mark Twain / Lincoln are misattributions.
Quote Investigator (quoteinvestigator.com) is good on
these.

The pattern: a quote that sounds like the famous person
gets attributed; nobody checks. If the famous person
didn't say it, doesn't matter for the claim's truth, but
it's a strong "this writer doesn't fact-check"
signal.

### Non-sequitur statistics

The argument: "X is bad. (Statistic about Y.)
Therefore X."

The statistic is real but doesn't support the claim. The
reader's brain integrates "X claim + statistic" into "X
proven", but the statistic was about Y.

Especially common in op-eds and ad copy. Always parse
what the statistic is *about*, not what's being claimed
around it.

### Causation claims from correlation data

"X is associated with Y" → "X causes Y".

The original study almost always says "associated with"
or "correlated with". The popular version drops the
hedge. Real causation requires controlled experiments,
mechanism, dose-response — not just correlation.

When you see causation in popular writing, find the
original; check whether the original claimed causation.

### Survivorship bias

"Successful people all do X" — but the failures who also
did X aren't in the dataset.

"Companies that adopted Y outperformed" — but the
companies that adopted Y *and went bankrupt* aren't in
the sample.

The claim feels like data; it's data with a
self-selecting cohort.

### Gish gallop

Many claims, rapidly. Each claim individually weak;
collectively the volume creates the impression of a
strong case.

Defence: pick *one* claim; verify or refute it
specifically. The Gish galloper depends on the
verification cost being too high to pursue each.

### Plausible-deniability hedging

"Some say X." "It is alleged that X." "X has been
suggested."

The author asserts X via implication while disclaiming
the assertion. Ask: who specifically says X? On what
basis? Often the answer is "the author thinks X but
can't say so directly."

### Manufactured controversy

Two sides presented as equally weighted when the actual
field has a 95/5 consensus. The "balance" creates the
impression of debate where there isn't one.

Especially common on settled science / history that's
politically inconvenient.

Test: what does the field's actual survey of
practitioners say? Is the "controversy" inside the field
or only in pop discussion?

### Anecdote-as-evidence

A vivid story stands in for a statistical claim. "I knew
a person who..." used to argue a generalisation.

Anecdotes are useful for illustrating concepts the data
already supports; bad as primary evidence.

### Authority-citing-self-citing

"As [respected institution] reports..." — and the report
is itself a press release based on [institution]'s
research, often unaudited.

Authority-by-association is weaker than authority-by-
methodology.

### Predictions with no track record

A predictor confidently asserts X will happen. No
mention of their previous predictions and how those
turned out. Often the previous predictions have no
follow-up.

Test: does this predictor have a history? How accurate
were past predictions? "Always confident; sometimes
right" is not a useful track record.

### Strawman counter-argument

The author raises an opposing view; presents it
weakly; refutes the weak version. "Critics say X" — but
nobody actually says X, or only fringe critics do.

Real engagement with opposition takes the strongest
version of the opposing view (steelman) and addresses
it.

### Anecdote stack

Many specific, vivid stories supporting a claim. Volume
of anecdote substitutes for systematic evidence.

Each anecdote may be true; the generalisation may not
be. "I have many examples" doesn't equal "this is the
typical case".

### Weasel word density

Claims wrapped in:

- "experts believe"
- "many say"
- "it has been suggested"
- "some sources indicate"
- "concerns have been raised"

Each phrase shifts attribution to vague third-parties.
The author asserts via implication while taking no
ownership of the claim.

### Misleading framing of true facts

The fact is true; the frame is misleading.

- "X has doubled!" (from 0.001 to 0.002 — both still
  trivially small)
- "Y is X% of Z!" (with Z chosen to make X% sound
  alarming or trivial)
- "Country A has more cases than Country B" (without
  population normalisation)
- "Ratio of N:1" (where 1 was already large; ratio
  itself misleading)

Test: would alternative true framings of the same data
give a different impression?

## Sagan's principles

Carl Sagan's "baloney detection kit" — useful self-tests
when evaluating claims:

1. **Independent confirmation.** Multiple independent
   sources?
2. **Substantive debate.** Has the claim survived
   serious challenge?
3. **No "argument from authority".** What's the
   reasoning, not who said it?
4. **More than one hypothesis.** What else could
   explain the evidence?
5. **Don't get attached to your hypothesis.** Treat your
   own conclusion as testable.
6. **Quantify.** Numbers more checkable than prose.
7. **Weakest links.** Every link in the argument has to
   hold; not just the strong ones.
8. **Occam's razor.** Simpler explanation usually
   right when both fit equally well.
9. **Falsifiability.** Is there evidence that would
   change the claim? If not, it's not science.

## Specific bullshit-rich domains

Domains where bullshit density is structurally high;
extra skepticism warranted:

- **Self-help / productivity.** Anecdote-heavy;
  "millionaire morning routines"; selective claims.
- **Diet / nutrition.** Conflicting study volume;
  industry funding.
- **Investment / financial advice on social media.**
  Survivorship bias dominant; advice from people who got
  lucky.
- **Political claims about the other side.** Motivated
  reasoning is the default mode.
- **AI claims.** Particularly capability claims;
  performance benchmarks; predictions about future
  systems.
- **Brain / neuroscience pop claims.** Often
  oversimplifications of weak studies.
- **Business / management advice.** Survivorship +
  storytelling + "this worked at Apple" type claims.

This isn't a list of "things to dismiss". It's a list of
"things to check more carefully than usual".

## What to do when you spot bullshit

### Don't share it

Even if you intend to share critically. The reach of
the claim grows; nuance often gets lost.

### If you must respond

Respond to the *substance*, not the bullshit pattern.
"Your claim X depends on study Y. Study Y actually
shows Z, which doesn't support your claim."

Pattern-naming ("you committed Brandolini's
fallacy!") rarely persuades; specific refutation can.

### Move on

Brandolini's law says responding takes 10× the energy
of producing. Sometimes the right move is "this is
bullshit; not worth the effort to refute". Saved energy
goes to substantive work.

## Limits of pattern-matching

Bullshit recognition is *hypothesis generation*, not
verdict. A claim can match a bullshit pattern and still
be true. A claim can avoid every pattern and be false.

The patterns generate suspicion → suspicion drives
verification → verification produces the verdict.

Don't skip verification because pattern matches. Don't
demand verification when patterns clearly aren't there.

## Anti-patterns in your own bullshit detection

### Reflexive skepticism

Doubting everything equally exhausts skepticism budget.
Calibrate: high-stakes, high-suspicion claims warrant
deep checking; routine claims warrant light checking.

### Tribally-targeted skepticism

Strong skepticism on out-group sources; light skepticism
on in-group sources. Symmetric application is the
discipline.

### Pattern-as-verdict

"This matches survivorship bias pattern, so it's wrong."
Patterns suggest; verification verifies. Pattern alone
doesn't refute.

### Fatalism

"Brandolini's law means we can't fight back."
Brandolini's law means we have to be selective. Within
selection, real refutation is possible and worth doing.

### Outrage farming

Recognising bullshit in others' claims; missing it in
sources you agree with. Apply the same lens both
directions.

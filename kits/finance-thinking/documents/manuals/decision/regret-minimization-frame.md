# Regret Minimization Frame — when expected-value optimization fails

The expected-value frame: pick the option with the highest
average outcome.

The regret-minimization frame: pick the option you'll
regret the least if it goes wrong.

For symmetric, repeatable decisions, expected value works.
For asymmetric or one-shot decisions — most consequential
financial decisions — regret minimization often produces
better choices.

## Why expected value misleads

Expected value assumes:

- The downside and the upside are similar in size.
- You can repeat the bet many times to converge to the
  mean.
- Variance doesn't ruin you.

Many money decisions violate all three:

- **Catastrophic downside.** Bankruptcy is
  asymmetric — recovery takes years; "average return"
  is irrelevant if you're broke.
- **One-shot.** Buying a house once; choosing one
  career; one chance at this contract.
- **Variance kills.** Even with positive expected value,
  variance can ruin a single trial.

The classic case: an opportunity with 80% chance to win
$10K and 20% chance to lose your entire life savings.
Expected value is positive; the realised outcome 1-in-5
times is catastrophic. Don't take the bet.

## Bezos's regret minimization

Jeff Bezos's framing for the Amazon decision: project
yourself to age 80, look back, ask: which version of
this story would I regret?

For Amazon: regret of trying-and-failing was less than
regret of not trying.

The frame doesn't tell you what to do; it surfaces which
side asymmetry sits on. For some decisions, regret of
not-doing is worse; for others, regret of doing-and-
losing is worse.

## How to apply

For a decision in front of you:

### 1. Imagine outcome A — you do it, and it works

Specifically. Not "it works" — what's the future like?
What did you give up to do it? What did you gain?

### 2. Imagine outcome B — you do it, and it fails

Specifically. Bankruptcy? Embarrassment? Time wasted?
Damaged relationship? What does the worst-realistic
case look like?

### 3. Imagine outcome C — you don't do it

Specifically. Status quo continues. What's lost? What's
preserved? Where are you in 5 years vs. outcome A?

### 4. At age 80, looking back: which would I regret?

Honest answer matters more than the calculated
expected value.

The frame asks **future-self** rather than
**present-self**. Present-self can be biased by current
mood, current fear, current pressure. Future-self is
more dispassionate.

## When regret-minimization points TO action

- **The thing wouldn't be available later.** A specific
  job; a specific house; a specific chance.
- **Reversibility is high; downside is bounded.** Even
  if it fails, you recover.
- **The non-action regret compounds.** Not starting the
  business; not asking for the raise; not taking the
  career risk in your 30s vs. 50s.
- **You'd never wonder "what if".** The not-doing
  itself is an ongoing cost.

## When regret-minimization points AWAY from action

- **Catastrophic downside that can't be recovered from.**
  Risking retirement savings on a single bet.
- **Action is permanent in a way the alternative isn't.**
  Marrying; moving cross-country; buying a house in a
  hard-to-leave area.
- **The "what if not" doesn't bother you.** Some
  opportunities sound exciting in theory; you wouldn't
  regret skipping in practice.
- **You're being pressured.** Decisions made under
  duress are usually mistakes; regret-minimization
  with full information would say "wait".

## The asymmetric-downside check

Before any consequential financial action, run:

- **What's the worst plausible outcome?**
- **Could I recover from that?**
- **How long would recovery take?**
- **Does the upside justify even the small chance of
  the worst case?**

If "worst case" + "small chance" = "ruined for years":
the bet's expected value doesn't matter.

This is sometimes called the "ruin problem" or "Kelly
criterion" thinking — bets that risk total wipe-out are
not justified by the average return, because you don't
get to play average; you play once.

## When regret-minimization conflicts with EV

A common pattern:

- EV: small expected gain; small chance of large loss.
- Regret-minimization: skip the small gain to avoid the
  loss-regret.

Most personal finance literature aligns with regret-
minimization here: avoid catastrophic risks even when
positive-EV.

The exception: if the catastrophic risk is partially
hedged or the consequence is bounded (e.g. through
insurance, structural protections), the EV frame may
become appropriate.

## Future-self vs. present-self

Some decisions weight present-self heavy:

- **Spending now vs. saving for retirement.**
  Present-you wants the experience; future-you wants
  the security.
- **Eating well vs. cheap food.** Future-you's body
  vs. present-you's wallet.
- **Living in the city you want vs. cheaper but less
  satisfying.** Present-you experiences daily life;
  future-you accumulates wealth.

Regret-minimization at age 80 often weights future-
self. But not always — some "I should have lived more"
regrets are real.

The frame is a tool for **getting present-self to
consider future-self**, not a verdict that future-self
always wins.

## The "what would my friend regret" check

When your own self-knowledge is muddied (you're
emotionally invested), imagine a thoughtful friend
making the same decision.

- What would they regret?
- What would you tell them?

The advice you'd give a friend is often clearer than
the advice you'll take yourself. If those advice lines
disagree, surface why.

## Regret about decisions you didn't have to make

A subset: decisions imposed by circumstances. Job loss,
medical emergency, divorce, family obligation.

Regret-minimization here is about the *response* you
chose, not the precipitating event. Did you respond
wisely? Could you have? Did you take the long view, or
the immediate-stress view?

Often these decisions are made under duress. The
regret-minimization frame is harder to apply but more
important — duress decisions are most likely to be
regretted.

When possible: delay duress decisions. Some consumer-
protection mechanisms (mandatory waiting periods,
cooling-off rights, attorney involvement) exist exactly
for this. Use them.

## When the frame doesn't apply

- **Trivial decisions.** What restaurant tonight. Don't
  invoke regret-minimization for low-stakes choices.
- **Truly symmetric decisions.** Two paths with
  equivalent outcomes — flip a coin or pick by
  preference; regret minimization doesn't help.
- **Lottery-class decisions.** Tiny chance of huge
  upside, nearly-certain modest loss. The expected
  value is the right framework; regret of having
  bought one lottery ticket is small.

## Anti-patterns

### Regret-as-sales-tool

"You'd regret not doing this" used in sales. Designed
to manipulate the regret-frame. The decision deserves
honest reflection, not panic about FOMO.

### One-sided regret accounting

Imagining the regret-of-not-doing vividly; imagining
regret-of-doing-and-failing dismissively. Imagine both
honestly.

### Confusing regret with embarrassment

Embarrassment fades; regret persists. Decisions to
avoid embarrassment ("what would people think") often
produce real regret later.

### Letting present-self always win

Regret-minimization isn't always future-self winning;
but always-now-want is its own bias. Calibrate.

### Sunk-cost regret

Continuing a bad decision because exiting "wastes
the investment". The investment is gone either way;
regret-minimize forward, not on already-spent
resources.

## Worked examples

### Substantial career risk

EV calculation says "stay in current job; avoid
income variance". Regret-minimization at 80: would
you regret never trying?

Often regret-minimization points toward the risk —
but only if downside is recoverable (you can find
another job; you have buffer for the gap).

If downside is catastrophic (you'd lose visa status;
or savings can't cover the gap), the answer may
flip.

### Buying a home in a market top

EV: housing on average appreciates; expected return
positive long-term. Regret-min: if market drops 30%
and you have to sell at a loss in 5 years, do you
regret? If yes (and you can imagine being forced to
sell): hold off.

### Major lifestyle expenditure

A $5K/year hobby vs. $5K/year more saved. EV-savings
wins on portfolio math. But regret-min at 80: do you
regret never having done the hobby? For some yes,
others no — depends on the hobby and the person.

## Output line

- "**Regret-min frame applied:** outcome ⟨A / B / C⟩
  is the one you'd regret most. **Implication:**
  ⟨specific⟩."
- "**Asymmetric downside detected** — EV frame
  insufficient; reconsider."
- "**Frame doesn't fit** — low-stakes / truly
  symmetric / decision under duress."
- "**Future-self / present-self conflict surfaced** —
  ⟨specific⟩."

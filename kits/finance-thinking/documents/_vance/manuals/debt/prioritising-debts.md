# Prioritising Debts — what to pay first

You have multiple debts; you have extra money to put
toward them; which gets it first?

Two well-known heuristics; a third hybrid; and several
exceptions. None is universally right.

## Avalanche (mathematically optimal)

Pay minimums on all debts; put all extra toward the
highest-interest-rate debt.

When the highest is paid off, move to the next highest.

**Pros:**

- Mathematically minimises total interest paid.
- Optimises return on extra-payment dollars.

**Cons:**

- Can take a long time to see the first "win" (paying
  off a debt) if the highest-rate balance is large.
- Motivationally harder for some users — feels like
  no progress for months.

**When it works:**

- User has discipline + motivation independent of
  visible wins.
- Interest-rate differences are large enough that the
  math savings matter.
- User is comfortable with patient, math-driven
  approaches.

## Snowball (motivationally tuned)

Pay minimums on all debts; put all extra toward the
**smallest balance** debt (regardless of rate).

When that's paid off, roll its old payment into the
next-smallest. Repeat.

**Pros:**

- Quick wins. First debt paid off in months, not
  years.
- Each payoff gives momentum.
- Studies suggest higher completion rate than
  avalanche for many users.

**Cons:**

- Mathematically suboptimal — you pay more total
  interest.

**When it works:**

- User struggles with motivation.
- User has many small debts (lots of payoffs to
  enjoy).
- Interest rate differences are modest (so the math
  cost of snowball over avalanche is small).

## Hybrid (practical compromise)

Pay highest-rate first **if** it's also reasonably-sized;
otherwise pay smallest-debt-with-decent-rate first to
build momentum, then graduate to avalanche.

Or: alternate — small win first for momentum; then
shift to avalanche.

**Pros:**

- Captures motivation benefit + most math benefit.

**Cons:**

- Requires some judgement about which is which.

**When it works:**

- Mixed debt picture.
- User wants to use both math and motivation.

## Variations / exceptions

The basic heuristics break down for several common
situations.

### Variable-rate debt

A debt with a variable rate that's currently low but
could rise — different treatment than a fixed-rate at
the same starting rate. The variable could become the
highest-rate by next year.

Treatment: factor in the *risk-adjusted* rate, not
just the current.

### Tax-deductible debt

A debt whose interest is deductible has lower
effective cost. If your bracket is 24% and a 6%
mortgage is deductible, effective cost is ~4.5%.

Treatment: use effective post-tax rate when
comparing across debt.

(But: deductibility rules are jurisdiction-specific
and change. Verify with tax pro.)

### Debt with risk-of-default consequence

A secured debt where missing payments means losing the
asset (car, home) deserves higher attention than its
rate suggests, especially if the asset is necessary.

Treatment: pay enough to stay current; don't strip
ahead-payments at the cost of risking foreclosure /
repossession.

### Family / friend debt

The 0% rate suggests "pay last". The relational
weight may suggest "pay early". This is a judgement
call.

### Debt nearing forgiveness / dischargeability event

Student loans nearing 25-year IDR forgiveness;
medical debt nearing statute of limitations;
business debt about to be discharged in bankruptcy
— paying early on these can be wasted money.

Treatment: understand the specific timeline before
prioritising.

### Time-bound 0% promotional rates

A balance-transfer card at 0% for 18 months, 22% APR
after — pay it down to zero before the 0% expires.
Rate during the promo is 0%, but after-promo it
becomes the highest-rate debt.

Treatment: front-load payments to get under the
balance before the rate jumps.

## What to NOT do

### "I'll pay them all proportionally"

Spreading extra payments equally across debts. This is
mathematically the worst approach — you pay all of them
slowly, none of them fast. The compounding bites.

### "I'll pay the most stressful one first"

Sometimes valid (relational debt; debt from someone
calling you), but often the "stressful" debt is just
the most-recent or most-emotionally-charged. Doesn't
correlate with what's costing the most.

### Pay off the secured / "real" debt first

Mortgage / car loan have lower rates than credit
cards; paying them off first is mathematically wrong.
The "secured = should be paid first" intuition is
backward.

### Refinance everything to one big loan

Sometimes good (lower rate, simpler, fixed schedule).
Sometimes bad (consolidation that doesn't change
spending; new debt added on top).

If consolidation is on the table:

- Verify the new rate is meaningfully lower.
- Check fees / costs of consolidation.
- Watch for "now I have credit available again" trap
  — many people accumulate new card debt after
  consolidating.
- Don't consolidate if it ruins refinance / forgiveness
  optionality (e.g. moving federal student loans to
  private kills programmes).

## Worked example

A user has:

| Debt | Balance | Rate | Tax? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Credit card A | $4,200 | 24% | No |
| Credit card B | $1,800 | 19% | No |
| Auto loan | $11,000 | 7% | No |
| Student loan | $22,000 | 6.5% | Maybe |
| Mortgage | $260,000 | 4.0% | Maybe |

**Avalanche:** pay minimums everywhere; extra toward
Credit Card A. After paid: extra to Credit Card B.
Then auto loan. Then student loan. Last: mortgage.

**Snowball:** pay minimums everywhere; extra toward
Credit Card B (smallest). Quick win in ~12 months.
Then Credit Card A. Then auto. Then student. Then
mortgage.

**Hybrid:** the rate gap between Card A (24%) and
Card B (19%) matters less than the absolute size; pay
Card B first for the win, then switch to avalanche
(Card A → auto → student loan → mortgage).

The "right" answer depends on:

- User's motivation profile.
- How long can they stay on plan?
- How big the rate gaps are.
- Tax effects (verify).

## When the question is bigger than prioritisation

Sometimes the question isn't "which to pay first" but
"what to do with overwhelming debt". Signs:

- Total monthly debt payments > 50% of after-tax
  income.
- Falling behind on basic bills (rent, utilities).
- Taking on new debt to pay old.
- Default / collection notices.
- Considering bankruptcy.

This is beyond prioritisation. Direct to:

- **Non-profit credit counselling** (in many
  countries; National Foundation for Credit Counselling
  in US, debt charities in UK, etc.). Often free or
  low-cost.
- **Bankruptcy attorney consult.** Many offer free
  initial consult.
- **Debt management plan.** Negotiated by counsellor;
  can lower rates / waive fees.
- **In extreme cases: consumer bankruptcy.** Last
  resort; substantial consequences but legitimate
  tool.

**Don't try to solve overwhelming debt with
prioritisation alone.**

## Anti-patterns

### Skipping the rate-by-rate map

User picks priority by feel without listing the rates.
Often pays mid-rate debt while ignoring the highest-
rate.

### Locking into one heuristic

The picture changes (rates change, balances change,
income changes). Re-evaluate priority every 6-12
months.

### Sacrificing emergency fund for debt payoff

Paying down debt aggressively while emergency fund is
empty. Next emergency forces new debt — usually at a
higher rate. Most authors recommend at least a small
emergency fund (1-3 months) before aggressive debt
payoff.

### Ignoring matched retirement contributions

Many employers match retirement contributions (US
401(k), etc.). Foregoing the match to pay down debt
faster is usually a mistake — match is ~100% return
on contribution.

The threshold: contribute up to match; then debt
payoff; then more retirement.

### Paying off mortgage before all other debt

Mathematically rare to be optimal. Mortgages typically
have low rates relative to other debt; paying them
off first leaves higher-rate debt running.

(Exception: very-near-retirement homeowners who want
mortgage cleared for psychological / cashflow reasons.)

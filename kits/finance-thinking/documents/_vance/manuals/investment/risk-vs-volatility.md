# Risk vs. Volatility — different concepts

Common usage conflates the two. They're related but
different, and the difference matters for thinking
about investment.

## Volatility

Price movement over time. How much does the asset's
price swing?

Examples:

- A stock that drops 8% in a quarter and recovers in a
  year: volatile.
- A treasury bond at maturity: minimal volatility.
- Crypto in a normal week: very volatile.

Volatility is **measurable**. Standard deviation, beta,
implied volatility — quantitative metrics exist.

Volatility itself isn't loss. The price drops; if you
hold, the price typically recovers; you're net the
recovery, not the dip.

## Risk

The chance and severity of permanent loss / inability
to meet your goals.

Examples:

- You buy stock at $100; price drops to $40; you sell
  at $40 because you needed the money: **realised
  loss**, real risk.
- You buy stock at $100; price drops to $40; you hold
  for 5 years; price recovers to $130: variance, not
  loss.
- You're 65 and need the money in 2 years; portfolio
  drops 30%: real risk, because you don't have time
  to recover.

Risk depends on:

- **Time horizon.** Short horizons make volatility into
  risk; long horizons let it average out.
- **Need for the money.** Forced sale at the bottom is
  what converts volatility to loss.
- **Concentration.** Single-asset risk has no recovery
  if the asset itself dies.
- **Sequence of returns.** A loss early in retirement
  hurts more than a loss late in accumulation.

## The "high risk tolerance" claim

Common in investment questionnaires: "How comfortable
are you with risk?" → "I have a high risk tolerance."

What people often mean: "I'm comfortable with
volatility — I won't panic-sell on a 30% drop."

What they sometimes don't account for:

- **They've never experienced a 50% drop personally.**
  Comfort claimed in calm conditions != comfort during
  the actual event.
- **Their stated tolerance reflects current life
  state.** Job security, no immediate dependants,
  long horizon. Life changes (illness, layoff,
  family) shift tolerance.
- **They're conflating volatility tolerance with
  ruin tolerance.** "I'm fine with 30% drawdown" is
  different from "I'm fine if my retirement
  disappears".

The honest test:

1. **Imagine a specific scenario.** "Your $200K
   portfolio drops to $100K next year. The $100K
   stays for 18 months. What do you do?"
2. **Imagine your reaction during the drop.** Not
   after; during. Three months in, you don't know
   if it's a recession or a crash.
3. **Account for life-stage changes.** "If you
   needed the money next year, would your answer
   change?"

People often claim higher tolerance than they
actually have. The claim costs nothing; the test
during a real drop reveals the truth.

## Specific risk types

### Market risk (systematic)

Whole-market drops. 2008, 2020 March, 1987, dot-com,
etc. Diversification across sectors / industries /
companies doesn't help — everything drops together.

Mitigation:

- Time horizon (long enough to ride out).
- Asset-class diversification (bonds, real estate,
  cash) — but correlations rise during crises.
- Cash reserves (so you don't have to sell at the
  bottom).
- Behaviour discipline (don't sell during the drop).

### Concentration risk (specific)

A single stock / sector / company dies or stagnates.
Examples: Enron, Lehman, recent SPAC carnage.

Mitigation: diversification across companies and
sectors.

### Inflation risk

Cash and fixed-income lose purchasing power if
inflation runs hot.

Mitigation: equities historically beat inflation long-
term; inflation-protected securities (TIPS in US,
similar elsewhere) provide direct hedge.

### Liquidity risk

Asset can't be sold when you need cash.

Examples: real estate (can take months / years), small-
cap illiquid stocks, alternative investments, private
equity.

Mitigation: hold liquid assets for needs that might
arise; treat illiquid investments as long-horizon
only.

### Sequence-of-returns risk

Order of returns matters when you're withdrawing.

A retiree starting retirement during a bear market
runs out of money sooner than a retiree starting
during a bull market — even if the **average** return
is identical.

Mitigation: asset allocation that reduces drawdown
near retirement; flexible spending (cut withdrawals
during bad years); cash buffer for early-retirement
years.

### Behavioural risk

You panic-sell at the bottom. Or buy at the top
because everyone is talking about it. The biggest
risk is often your own decisions during volatility.

Mitigation:

- Pre-commit to allocation; rebalance mechanically.
- Don't check portfolio constantly during turbulence.
- Have an investment policy statement; follow it.
- Plan for the bad months when calm.

### Single-asset / single-company catastrophe

Specific company goes to zero. Especially relevant if
substantial portion of net worth is in one company
(employer stock, founder equity).

Mitigation: diversify when possible; understand
constraints (vesting, lock-ups, taxes).

## Volatility doesn't equal risk if...

- Time horizon is long enough to ride out drops.
- You don't sell during drops.
- Your needs aren't tied to specific dates / amounts.
- The diversification holds.

For long-horizon investors with stable income, much of
"investment risk" reduces to "are you going to behave
during the next 30% drop?".

## Risk doesn't equal volatility if...

- Concentrated in single assets that could die.
- Borrowing to invest (margin call risk).
- Short horizon with required outcome (kid's tuition
  next year).
- Liquidity constraints (can't sell when needed).
- Behavioural pattern of selling on dips.

## Implications for thinking

### "What's my risk tolerance?"

Not a single number. Depends on:

- Horizon for this money.
- Other resources / income.
- Specific scenarios you'd face.
- Your behavioural history (real or imagined).

### "Is X risky?"

"Risky for what purpose?" An asset that's volatile
isn't necessarily risky for a long-horizon goal; an
asset that's stable isn't necessarily safe for a goal
threatened by inflation.

Match asset to goal, not to abstract "risk" label.

### "How do I get high returns with low risk?"

You don't, in any reliable way. Real-world investments
trade returns for variance.

The closest legitimate paths:

- **Diversification** reduces specific-company /
  specific-sector risk.
- **Tax-advantaged accounts** boost effective return
  without changing pre-tax risk.
- **Fee minimisation** keeps more of the return.
- **Time horizon** lets volatility average out.

Anything advertising "high return with low risk" is
either:

- Misrepresenting (high return claim isn't real).
- Hiding the risk (rare-event tail).
- Fraud.

## Anti-patterns

### Conflating volatility and risk in conversation

Talking about "risk" when meaning "volatility" or vice
versa. Causes muddled decisions.

### "I can handle drops" optimism

Stated comfort in calm conditions; actual behaviour
during the next real drop. Plan for the drop you
haven't seen yet.

### Risk tolerance based on questionnaire alone

Online quizzes give a number; the number is rough.
Real risk tolerance reveals during real downturns.

### Borrowing to invest

Margin / HELOC-to-invest amplifies returns but creates
ruin risk. A drawdown can force liquidation at the
bottom — converting volatility into permanent loss.

This is a different category than ordinary investing.
Direct to professional.

### "It's only on paper"

The drop "doesn't matter because I don't sell". True
unless something forces a sale (job loss, emergency,
margin call). The "I can hold forever" claim depends
on the surrounding life staying stable.

### Single-asset confidence

"My company is solid" — said about every company that
later imploded. Single-asset risk is real; diversify
when you can.

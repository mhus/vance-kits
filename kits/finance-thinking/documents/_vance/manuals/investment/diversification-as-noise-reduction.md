# Diversification as Noise Reduction — what it does and doesn't

Diversification is widely advised, often misunderstood.
"Don't put all your eggs in one basket" captures the
idea but obscures the mechanism — and the limits.

## What diversification does

### Reduces idiosyncratic risk

If you own one stock and that company dies, your
investment dies. If you own 50 stocks across
industries, no single bankruptcy ruins you.

This is **specific risk** or **idiosyncratic risk** —
the risk specific to a single company / industry /
asset.

Diversification removes most of this risk
mathematically. Beyond ~20-30 stocks, additional
holdings don't reduce idiosyncratic risk much; you've
captured the noise-reduction benefit.

### Reduces variance

Even if no individual asset dies, holding multiple
assets reduces year-to-year swings. When one drops,
another might be flat or up.

The reduction depends on correlation. Two assets that
move identically (correlation 1.0) provide zero
diversification benefit; two that move opposite
(correlation -1.0) provide maximum.

Real-world correlations are mostly between 0.3 and
0.9. Diversification reduces variance meaningfully but
not dramatically.

### Smooths the experience

Lower variance means smaller drawdowns, more
predictable trajectory. Especially helpful for
behavioural reasons — easier to stay invested when
the swings are gentler.

## What diversification does NOT do

### Eliminate market risk

When the whole market drops (2008, 2020, 1987), your
diversified portfolio drops too. Diversification across
US stocks doesn't help when US stocks drop together.

International diversification helps somewhat — but in
crisis periods, global correlations rise (everything
sells together).

For market-risk hedging, you need:

- Cash reserves.
- Long time horizon.
- Asset classes with genuinely different correlation
  (bonds, real estate, possibly commodities) — though
  even these correlate during crises.

### Help if you're concentrated in a single sector

"Diversifying across 50 tech stocks" diversifies away
single-company risk but not sector risk. If tech drops
40%, your "diversified" portfolio drops 40%.

True diversification spans sectors, asset classes,
geographies.

### Help short-term

Diversification's variance-smoothing is a long-run
phenomenon. Over a single quarter, anything can happen.
Don't expect short-term protection.

### Generate excess returns

Diversification reduces *variance*; it doesn't increase
*expected return*. The market gives roughly the same
return to a well-diversified portfolio as to a
concentrated bet — but the well-diversified portfolio
has much less risk of catastrophic loss.

Anyone claiming diversification will boost returns is
selling something.

### Help much with very-correlated assets

A "diversified" portfolio of US large-caps + US mid-
caps + US small-caps still has high correlation in
crises. Diversification by name only.

## Asset classes and correlations

Common asset classes and their typical correlations to
US stocks:

| Asset class | Correlation to US stocks |
|---|---|
| US stocks (other size / sector) | 0.7-0.95 |
| International developed stocks | 0.7-0.9 |
| Emerging markets | 0.6-0.85 |
| Investment-grade bonds | -0.3 to 0.3 |
| Treasury bonds | -0.4 to 0.0 (often negative) |
| Real estate (REITs) | 0.5-0.8 |
| Commodities | 0.0-0.5 |
| Cash | ~0 |

Lower correlations provide better diversification.
Cash + treasury bonds have historically been the best
diversifiers — but their correlations shifted in 2022
(both stocks and bonds dropped).

**Correlations aren't constant.** The "60/40 portfolio
hasn't lost money in any year" pattern broke down. Past
correlations don't guarantee future ones.

## The 60/40 frame and its limits

Classic balanced portfolio: 60% stocks / 40% bonds.

Logic: stocks for growth; bonds for stability;
combination smooths the ride.

**Strengths:**

- Simple.
- Captures most of the diversification benefit.
- Works in many environments.

**Limits:**

- Bonds aren't risk-free. 2022 showed bond losses with
  rising rates.
- Doesn't include real estate, commodities, foreign
  exposure (or does, depending on definition).
- The 60/40 numbers are arbitrary; "right" allocation
  depends on individual situation.
- Modern critique: 60/40 may be too narrow for current
  market structure.

The 60/40 isn't sacred. It's a starting point;
adjustments warranted by individual situation.

## Within-class diversification

Even within "US stocks" you can be more or less
diversified:

- **Concentrated:** 5 stocks (or 1 ETF tracking 5).
- **Sector-concentrated:** 50 stocks all in tech.
- **Cap-concentrated:** all large-cap.
- **Broadly-diversified:** 500+ stocks across sectors
  and sizes (an S&P 500 fund or a total-market fund).

Total-market index funds are the diversification gold
standard within an asset class.

## Across-class diversification

Beyond single asset classes:

- **Stocks** for growth.
- **Bonds** for stability and income (but not always
  uncorrelated with stocks).
- **Real estate** (REITs or direct) for inflation
  protection and different return drivers.
- **Cash** for liquidity and tactical flexibility.
- **Commodities** for inflation protection and very
  different return profile (but high variance).
- **International** exposure to spread across global
  economies.

The mix depends on goals, horizon, and risk tolerance.
**No single right answer.**

## When diversification is hard

### Concentrated employer stock

Vested company stock can be a substantial portion of
net worth. Diversifying may have:

- Tax consequences (capital gains).
- Vesting / lock-up restrictions.
- Concentration with career risk (if the company
  fails, both job and net worth implicated).

This is a real conflict. Direct to professional for
specific situations.

### Real estate concentration

Owning a home is investment in a single asset, single
geography, single property. Hard to "diversify" the
home; you can diversify the rest of net worth.

Watch for: very-high home-equity-to-net-worth ratio.
For some it's appropriate (long-time owners, high-cost
markets); for others it's concentration risk.

### Cryptocurrency / alternative assets

If user has substantial crypto / NFT / private equity
holdings, diversification thinking applies. These are
high-variance assets with idiosyncratic risk. Treat
as concentrated risky positions even within an
"alternatives" bucket.

### Founder / executive equity

Often illiquid; substantial concentration; tied to one
company outcome. Diversification is constrained;
managing concentration is the work.

## How to think about adding diversification

For each new asset class / position you'd add:

1. **What does it correlate with what I already have?**
2. **What does it add to expected return?** (often
   nothing or marginal)
3. **What does it reduce in variance?** (the
   benefit you're after)
4. **What does it cost?** (fees, complexity, tax
   complexity, monitoring)
5. **Net benefit?**

Most additional diversification beyond a basic
broad-market portfolio + bonds + cash is marginal. The
big gains are from the first few moves; later moves
are fine-tuning.

## Behavioural diversification

Some diversification serves behavioural rather than
mathematical purposes:

- **Multiple accounts** — easier to leave some
  invested while spending from others.
- **Multiple holding periods** — buying gradually over
  time (dollar-cost averaging) reduces buy-at-the-top
  regret.
- **Multiple buckets** — emergency / short-term /
  long-term / retirement, each in different
  vehicles.

These don't reduce mathematical risk but support
better behaviour during stress.

## Anti-patterns

### Diversifying for the sake of it

Adding many small positions to feel "diversified"
when most of them don't add real diversification
(other tech stocks; other US-cap variants).

### Closet concentration

Looks diversified; isn't. Two index funds with 80%
overlap; "international" fund that's 70% Europe and
none of Asia.

### Diversification + active management

Many actively-managed funds across the same market
exposure. You're paying many fees for what one index
fund does cheaper.

### Over-diversification

Beyond ~30 holdings within an asset class, additional
diversification is negligible. Some users hold
hundreds of mutual funds across dozens of accounts —
complexity without benefit.

### "International is its own thing"

International stocks correlate with US stocks more
than people expect. They diversify but not as much
as you might assume.

### Crypto as diversification

Crypto's correlation with stocks rose substantially
in recent years. It's no longer reliably uncorrelated.
And: variance is high. Diversification benefit may be
overstated.

### Bonds-equal-risk-free

Bonds have rate risk, credit risk, inflation risk. Not
risk-free. 2022 illustrated.

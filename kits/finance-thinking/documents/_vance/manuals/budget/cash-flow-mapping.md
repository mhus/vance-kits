# Cash-Flow Mapping — getting the actual picture

Before designing a budget, get the real picture. Most "I need
a budget" requests skip this and design categories on guesses
about spending. The categories then don't match reality;
the budget fails; the user blames discipline.

The cure: map first, design second.

## The four flows

Every budget structure rests on four kinds of money movement:

### 1. Regular inflows

Predictable, scheduled income. Salary, recurring contracts,
predictable royalties, regular benefits.

Map by:

- Source (employer / freelance / benefits).
- Amount per period.
- Period (monthly / biweekly / variable).
- After-tax, after-deduction net (this is what hits the
  account).

### 2. Regular outflows

Predictable, scheduled spending. Rent / mortgage,
insurance, subscriptions, utilities, loan payments.

Map by:

- Category.
- Amount per period.
- Period (monthly / annual / quarterly).
- Payment date (some bills cluster).

### 3. Irregular outflows

Spending that's not regular but is expected. Annual
insurance premiums, car repairs, dental / medical visits,
gift-buying seasons, vacations, occasional appliance
replacement.

Often missed. The classic budget failure: only counting
monthly bills + groceries, then being surprised by the
$1200 car repair every 18 months that "isn't in the
budget".

Map by:

- Category.
- Estimated annual / total cost.
- Last occurrence (anchor for the next one).

For irregular flows: **annualise**. A $1200 car repair
every 18 months is roughly $67/month equivalent.

### 4. Hidden flows

Money that moves without you noticing:

- **Auto-renewing subscriptions** (especially trial → paid
  conversions you forgot about).
- **Bank fees** (overdrafts, foreign transactions, ATM
  fees, monthly account fees).
- **App-store purchases** by the user / family.
- **Tipping inflation** when "20% suggested" became
  "default 25%".
- **Inflation drift** in subscription prices, rent
  escalators, insurance premium increases.

Hidden flows often add up to more than the user expects.
Map by going through 2-3 months of bank statements with
fresh eyes — look for charges that surprise.

## Method

### 1. Pull statements

Bank statements + credit card statements for 2-3 months.
Newer is better; longer is better for irregular-flow
detection.

### 2. Categorise inflows

Separate net pay from gross-with-deductions. The number
that matters for the budget is what reaches your account.

If income is variable: list per-month for last 12 months
to see the range, not an average. The lowest month is the
floor your budget can rely on.

### 3. Categorise outflows

For each transaction, assign a category. Resist the urge
to over-categorise — see `category-design`.

For each transaction, also note:

- **Recurring or one-off?** (helps separate regular vs.
  irregular).
- **Was this anticipated?** Surprise spending is a category
  on its own.

### 4. Spot the patterns

After categorisation:

- What's the regular outflow per month?
- What irregular flows hit and how often?
- Which categories surprise you (bigger than you thought)?

The pattern is what matters. The number is the input;
the pattern is the insight.

### 5. Compare to inflows

Net regular inflow vs. all annualised outflows. The gap
is what's "left over" — but be precise:

- **Surplus** if outflows < inflows.
- **Crunch** if equal or close.
- **Deficit** if outflows > inflows.

Each warrants different next steps.

## Common surprises

### "I make X but only have Y left"

The classic budget question. The math:

- Gross income.
- Minus taxes + deductions = net income.
- Minus regular expenses.
- Minus annualised irregular expenses.
- = what's actually discretionary.

Often the discretionary amount is much smaller than the
user assumed. That's not a failure — it's reality made
visible.

### "I don't know where it goes"

Hidden flows + small accumulating purchases. The "I
spent $500 on coffee last year" surprise is real for
many people.

### "My income covers it but I'm always stressed"

Often timing, not amounts. Income arrives monthly; bills
cluster; reserves run thin between paychecks. The cure
is reserve buffer, not necessarily cutting.

### "We're fine on paper but always behind"

Often joint-finance ambiguity (see `category-design`)
or one partner spending without the other knowing.

## Tools

For cash-flow mapping:

- **Bank export to CSV.** Most banks support; basic
  categorisation in spreadsheet works.
- **Personal finance apps** (YNAB, Mint, Lunch Money,
  etc.) — automate the mapping. Quality of categorisation
  varies.
- **Manual tracking** for a month. Heavier but produces
  awareness.

The tool isn't the technique. The mapping is the work.

## When mapping reveals a problem

The mapping itself is information; what to do with the
information is a separate question.

If you see a deficit:

- Surface it explicitly.
- Don't assume the user knows; many deficits surprise.
- Direct to next steps: cut, earn more, restructure debt.

If signs of crisis appear (rent overdue, payday loans,
cards maxed): this is beyond budget design. Direct to a
debt counsellor or consumer advice service. **Don't try
to handle this with a budget tool.**

## Anti-patterns

### Categorise without source

You start designing categories before pulling statements.
Categories then reflect imagination, not reality.

### Average month

You take 1 month and call it typical. Most months aren't
typical. Use 2-3 months minimum; 12 months for irregular
flows.

### Optimise during mapping

You see the spending; you adjust mid-mapping. Mapping is
data collection; optimisation is later. Mixing them
distorts the data.

### Ignore the small flows

"It's only $5/month" — true per-instance; multiplied by
many subscriptions and 12 months, the small flows are a
big chunk.

### Map once

Cashflow shifts. A 12-months-old map is stale. Re-map
quarterly or after major life changes (move, job change,
relationship change, child).

- "**Pattern reveals deficit / crunch / surplus** —
  next-step framing matters."
- "**Signs beyond budget design** — direct to debt
  counselling or specific professional help."

# What Budgets Fail At — common gaps

A budget that captures regular monthly bills and groceries
will still fail because reality contains structures the
basic budget doesn't see. This manual lists the failure
modes; designing a budget without naming them invites
them in.

## Irregular expenses

The biggest failure. The budget says "$3000/month
expenses". Then in March: $1200 for car repairs. April:
$800 for medical. June: $2000 for vacation. The
monthly budget collapses.

Counter: **annualise irregular expenses**. Estimate
yearly cost; divide by 12; treat as a monthly line.

Examples:

- Car repairs: $1500/year → $125/month sinking fund.
- Annual insurance premiums (health, home, auto):
  total ÷ 12.
- Vacation budget: planned $2400/year → $200/month.
- Gift / holiday spending: $1500/year → $125/month.
- Medical / dental: $600/year → $50/month.
- Pet care extras (vet visits, grooming): annual / 12.
- Home repairs / replacements: 1-3% of home value /
  year for owners.
- Car replacement fund: useful-life cost / months.

Most users underestimate irregular expenses by 30-50%.
Track for a year to refine.

## Joint finances without explicit structure

Two people, one household, no agreed structure. Each
assumes; both spend; arguments follow.

Failures:

- **"You bought what?"** — one partner makes a purchase
  the other didn't expect.
- **Income disparity ambiguity.** $80K and $120K, splitting
  rent 50/50 — one person feels squeezed; the other
  feels imposed-on.
- **Unequal mental load.** One partner tracks; one
  doesn't; the tracker feels resentful.
- **Hidden spending.** Personal hobbies eating into joint
  funds.

Counter: see `category-design` for joint-finance
structures. Pick deliberately; revisit when life
changes.

## Lifestyle creep

Income rises; spending rises proportionally; savings
don't change. After 5 years of raises, you have nicer
things and the same financial fragility as five years
ago.

Tells:

- Each raise gets absorbed within a month.
- Income up 30% over 3 years; savings rate unchanged.
- "Where did the extra go?" is mysterious.

Counter:

- **Pre-commit raise allocations.** Before the raise
  hits, decide: this much to lifestyle, this much to
  savings, this much to debt.
- **Track savings rate, not absolute spending.** A
  steady or rising savings rate is the discipline; the
  absolute spending number can grow.

## Irregular income

Freelancers, commissioned, seasonal, salary + bonus.
Average month = some-other-month, not this month.

Failures:

- **Smoothing through the lows.** A good month overspent;
  the lean month can't cover.
- **Tax surprise.** Self-employment tax / quarterly
  estimated payments not budgeted.
- **No buffer.** Month with $0 income → emergency.

Counter:

- **Budget on the floor income.** What's your worst
  plausible month? Live on that; everything above is
  buffer / savings.
- **Quarterly tax escrow.** Set aside 25-35% of
  self-employed income for tax (varies by jurisdiction
  and total income — confirm with tax professional).
- **6-month buffer minimum** for irregular-income
  households. 12 months is safer.

## Small purchases that accumulate

The "$5 here, $7 there" problem. Each individual
purchase rational; total surprising.

Common offenders:

- **Coffee out**: $5/day × 250 work days = $1250/year.
- **Lunch out**: $12/day × 250 = $3000/year.
- **Streaming services**: $10-15/month × 5 services =
  $600-900/year.
- **Apps / in-app purchases**.
- **Tipping inflation**: 18% defaults became 22-25%.
- **Convenience fees / shipping**: small per-instance,
  large cumulative.
- **Round-up tipping at registers**: < $1 per instance,
  but per-instance frequency adds up.

Counter:

- **Track per-category for a month**: discover the
  pattern without judgement.
- **Set a category budget**, not a per-purchase
  budget. "$200/month coffee" is more workable than
  "no $5 lattes ever".
- **Decide what you want to keep** — coffee may be a
  high-value purchase for you. Cut what's
  low-satisfaction; keep what's high-value.

## Subscription bloat

Modern subscription business model exploits
"reasonable monthly cost" framing.

Common bloat:

- 5 streaming services for the convenience of "the
  show I want is on whichever".
- Software subscriptions for tools used twice a year.
- Subscription boxes for things you don't need
  monthly.
- App subscriptions for apps you use twice a year.

Counter:

- **Quarterly subscription audit.** List all; for each:
  do I use this? Cancel what doesn't pay back.
- **Cancel before trial expires.** Most "free trial"
  conversions to paid are forgotten cancellations.
- **Bundle / share** where ToS permits.

## "I'll start the budget next month"

The cycle of perpetual postponement. Today is messy;
"next month" never arrives.

Counter: **start with the next 7 days.** Track for one
week; you have data; you can extend. The first week is
the hardest; the second is much easier.

## Emergencies that aren't really emergencies

The "emergency fund" gets used for vacation; "real
emergencies" find no buffer.

Counter:

- **Define emergency narrowly**: job loss, urgent
  medical, urgent household repair (heat in winter,
  not aesthetic).
- **Have separate funds**: emergency, vacation,
  car-replacement, big-purchase. Not one
  catch-all.
- **Replenish** after use. Drained emergency fund is
  an emergency on its own.

## Inflation on bills

Subscription prices creep up; insurance premiums
increase annually; rent escalates by lease clause.
The "$50/month subscription" is now $79; the user
hasn't noticed.

Counter:

- **Annual review.** Once a year, check all recurring
  bills against the original amount.
- **Renegotiate or switch** for inflation-creep
  bills (insurance especially — shopping around can
  save substantially).

## Couple-conflict ambiguity

Spending rules unclear → both partners spend "small"
amounts → both feel surprised by the joint number.

The anti-pattern: "We both agreed to be reasonable."
"Reasonable" means different things to each.

Counter:

- **Spend-without-checking threshold.** "Anything
  under $X each month, no need to discuss; over $X,
  joint decision." Picks a number; sticks.
- **Joint review monthly.** 15 minutes; review
  spending vs. plan; adjust.

## Tax surprises

The classic year-end surprise: "I owe how much?"

Common causes:

- **Self-employment** without quarterly estimates.
- **Investment gains** that triggered tax events.
- **Job change** with mid-year withholding mismatch.
- **One-off income** (royalty, bonus, settlement)
  pushing into a higher bracket.

Counter:

- **Estimate quarterly** if self-employed (work with
  a tax professional — rules and rates vary by
  jurisdiction, often substantially).
- **Adjust withholding** mid-year if life changes.
- **Track investment events** (rebalances, ETF
  distributions, employee stock vests).

**Important: tax planning is jurisdiction-specific and
beyond budgeting. For amounts that matter, work with a
tax professional.**

## "We can afford it on average"

The trap. Annual income $100K; monthly budget assumes
$8.3K each month. But income arrives unevenly; bills
cluster; reserves run thin in the gap.

Counter: **operate on lows, not averages.** If income
varies, plan from the worst plausible month forward.

## What budgets ALSO fail at

Even well-designed budgets miss:

- **Inflation overall** (not just on subscriptions).
- **Major life changes** (marriage, baby, job change,
  caretaking, illness).
- **Generational transfers** (helping aging parents;
  giving to adult children).
- **Big asset depreciation** (replacing the car at
  end of life isn't "if", it's "when").

Build the budget; revisit it; expect the rebuild after
major changes.

## Anti-patterns

### Set-it-and-forget-it

Budget designed once; never revisited. Reality drifts;
budget stays. After 18 months it's fictional.

### Budgeting as moralism

Categories named "wasteful" / "reasonable" /
"shouldn't"; user feels judged; abandons tracking.

### "I'll catch up next month"

A bad month happens. The user vows to "catch up". The
catch-up doesn't materialise. Better: zero-out, learn
what happened, continue from current.

### Couple-budget from one partner's data

One partner builds the budget alone; the other isn't
in the categories. Doesn't match reality; doesn't get
buy-in.

### Emergency fund eaten by lifestyle

The fund is at $5K; you raid for a "minor emergency"
(new TV); now the fund is at $1K; the actual emergency
arrives.

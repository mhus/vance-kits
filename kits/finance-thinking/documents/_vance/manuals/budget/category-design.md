# Category Design — categories that fit your life

A budget's categories are its skeleton. Wrong categories
produce a budget you can't navigate; the user gives up.

The trap: borrowed templates. Generic "groceries /
transport / entertainment" miss user-specific patterns
(eating out vs. groceries; rideshare vs. car; streaming vs.
events). The categories don't help the user notice what
matters.

## The 50/30/20 frame and its limits

Common starter:

- **50% needs** — rent, utilities, groceries, transport
  to work, basic insurance.
- **30% wants** — dining out, entertainment, hobbies,
  upgraded versions.
- **20% savings + debt repayment**.

Useful as orientation. But:

- The 50/30/20 split assumes a particular income level.
  In high-cost cities, "needs" can be 80%+ — the frame
  flattens.
- "Needs vs. wants" is contested at the boundary. Is a
  car a need? A streaming subscription? Therapy? The
  binary is artificial for many categories.
- The frame doesn't capture irregular costs.

Use as a sanity check. If your "needs" are 90% of income,
the situation isn't normal — surface it.

## How many categories

Trade-off:

- **Too few** (3-5): bucket too coarse to act on. "Other:
  $1200" tells you nothing.
- **Too many** (30+): tracking overhead exceeds value.
  You don't update; data goes stale.
- **Sweet spot: 8-15** for most users.

## Category dimensions worth distinguishing

Pick the dimensions that matter for your life. Common
useful axes:

### Necessity / discretion

- Rent, utilities, transport-to-work — fixed, hard to
  change short-term.
- Groceries (basic) — necessary but flexes.
- Eating out, entertainment, upgrades — fully
  discretionary.

This axis matters because it tells you what's negotiable
in a crunch.

### Recurring vs. variable

- Subscriptions (Netflix, gym, software, magazines).
- Variable bills (utilities that vary by season).
- One-off purchases (clothes, household items).

This axis matters because subscriptions are easy to forget
and accumulate; variable bills need a buffer.

### Joint vs. individual (in shared finances)

- Shared (rent, groceries, household).
- Mine (my hobby budget, my work clothes).
- Yours (your hobby budget, your professional dev).
- Joint discretionary (vacations together, gifts to
  in-laws).

This axis matters in any household with finance-sharing.
Skipping it produces "where did the money go" arguments.

### Time horizon

- Daily / weekly purchases.
- Monthly bills.
- Annual costs (insurance, vacations, gifts).
- Multi-year accumulation (replacement of major
  appliances, car, home repairs).

This axis matters for planning the buffer / sinking
funds.

## Worked example — categories for a single, US, urban

```
NEEDS (~60%)
  rent / utilities
  groceries
  health insurance
  transit / commuting
  phone / internet

DISCRETIONARY (~20%)
  eating out
  entertainment / hobbies
  clothing / personal care
  travel

REGULAR SAVING / DEBT (~15%)
  retirement contribution
  emergency fund
  loan payments

ANNUAL / IRREGULAR (~5%)
  insurance premiums (annualised)
  dental / medical (annualised)
  gift / holiday spending
  car / home repair fund
```

13 categories. Each has a real meaning; each surfaces
something distinctive about where money goes.

## Worked example — categories for a couple with shared
finances

```
SHARED (~70% combined)
  housing (rent + utilities)
  groceries
  shared health / insurance
  shared transport
  joint subscriptions
  shared entertainment / dining

MINE (~10%)
  my discretionary (hobbies / coffee / etc.)
  my professional development

YOURS (~10%)
  yours discretionary
  yours professional development

JOINT GOALS (~10%)
  shared savings (vacation / down payment / etc.)
  shared debt payments
```

Note: each partner has their own discretionary line. This
prevents the "I bought a book, you bought shoes" scoring
that derails joint budgets.

## Categories that often need their own line

These accumulate quietly and warrant their own bucket if
they apply to your life:

- **Subscriptions.** Streaming, software, gym, magazines,
  newsletter paid tiers. Easy to lose track.
- **Coffee / lunch.** Small purchases that add up
  meaningfully over months.
- **Apps / in-app purchases.** Especially with kids in
  the household.
- **Tipping.** In tipping-heavy cultures.
- **Shipping.** Online ordering with subscription delivery.
- **Bank fees / FX fees.** Especially for travellers.
- **Pet care.** Food, vet, grooming.
- **Childcare.** Whatever specific structure applies.

Add categories where the pattern matters; leave aggregate
buckets where it doesn't.

## What NOT to categorise heavily

If the category is a once-in-a-while spend with no pattern:
don't make it a permanent bucket. "Misc" or "discretionary"
is fine for these.

Categories should have at least 2-3 transactions per
quarter to earn their own line.

## Joint-finance category design

Joint finances introduce structural choices:

### Single shared account

Every shared expense from the joint account; each partner
keeps a personal account for individual spending.

Categories: shared expenses + personal allocations.

Pros: clear; minimises arguments.
Cons: needs explicit "what's my share" agreement; income
disparity makes equal split unfair, proportional split
needs explicit math.

### "I pay this, you pay that"

Each partner takes responsibility for specific
categories.

Categories: per-partner-by-category.

Pros: simpler psychologically.
Cons: can feel arbitrary; one partner pays "more
visible" expenses; doesn't equalize.

### Three-account model

His + Hers + Joint. Joint covers shared; personal covers
individual.

Categories: joint-shared + each personal.

The most common in finance literature; works well for
many.

### Pure pooling

All income to joint; all expenses from joint; both
spend freely up to discussion threshold.

Pros: simplest in trust.
Cons: requires high trust + similar values.

The structure choice affects the categories. Pick
deliberately; don't drift.

## Updating categories

Categories aren't permanent. Triggers to revisit:

- New recurring expense type (just got a pet; bought a
  car; started therapy).
- Life change (married, baby, retired, moved cities).
- Recurring spending pattern emerges (you keep eating out
  more; that line should grow).
- A category consistently shows ~0 (maybe combine it).
- A category consistently has unexpected outliers
  (consider splitting it).

Once a quarter is a reasonable rhythm. Don't redesign
every month — categories need stable history to be
useful.

## Anti-patterns

### Template adoption

Adopting someone else's category list wholesale. Their
categories reflect their life, not yours.

### Vanity categories

"Self-improvement", "investing in myself" — categories
that feel good but blur tracking. If a $200 course is
"self-improvement", what's a $5 magazine? The category
collapses.

Counter: name what it is. "Online courses" is a category;
"self-improvement" is a story.

### Punishment categories

"Bad spending", "stupid purchases". Moralising categories
make tracking feel like punishment; user stops tracking.

### Aspirational categories

"Investing 30%" when you're not. Categories should
reflect what *is*, not what *should be*. Use the gap
between is and should as input for change; don't fake
the categories.

### Hyper-precise

20+ subcategories of "groceries". Time on tracking >
value of insight.

### Co-mingled needs / wants

One category that's "groceries (including takeout)" —
loses the signal. Either keep them separate or accept
the bucket but not both.

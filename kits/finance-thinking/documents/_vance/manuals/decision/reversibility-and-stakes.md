# Reversibility and Stakes — one-way vs. two-way doors

The first dimension of any money decision: is it reversible
if it goes wrong, and how badly does the worst case hurt?

Reversibility-of-decision is often more important than
expected-value-of-decision. A reversible mistake is cheap;
an irreversible mistake compounds forever.

## Two-way doors

Decisions you can walk back from at modest cost.

Examples:

- **Buying a car.** Resell with depreciation hit. Lose
  10-30% relative to "if I'd never bought" — but the
  exit exists.
- **Renting an apartment.** Lease ends; you move. The
  cost is the months remaining + moving cost.
- **Picking an investment account.** Transfer assets to
  another provider; some friction; possible.
- **Joining a gym / starting a subscription.** Cancel.
- **Switching jobs (in normal labour markets).** If the
  new job is wrong, you can find another.

For two-way doors:

- **Decide faster.** The cost of a mistake is bounded;
  paralysis costs more than re-deciding.
- **Don't optimise hard.** Good enough beats analysis.
- **Try and learn.** Real data beats imagined data.

## One-way doors

Decisions whose reversal would cost substantially.

Examples:

- **Buying a home.** Selling has 6-10% transaction cost
  + uncertain timing + market exposure. The "I'll just
  sell if it doesn't work" math often produces tens of
  thousands of loss.
- **Major renovation.** Investment doesn't recoup at
  resale; can't be undone except by another renovation.
- **Quitting a job to start a business.** Jobs that
  matched your skill at that salary may not be there
  when you want back in.
- **Taking on substantial student debt** for a particular
  career path that may not work out.
- **Long-term lease commitments** in commercial /
  industrial spaces.
- **Marrying combining finances** vs. keeping separate.

For one-way doors:

- **Slow down.** You'll trade a longer decision time for
  much less downside risk if wrong.
- **Stress-test.** What if this goes wrong? Can you
  survive that?
- **Get more inputs.** Talk to people who made the same
  decision; talk to professionals.
- **Reduce reversibility cost where you can.** Some
  one-way doors have semi-reversible variants — leasing
  before buying, contract-to-perm before full
  employment, etc.

## Pseudo-reversible

Decisions that look reversible but aren't, in practice:

- **"I'll just refinance"** — refinancing depends on
  rates, credit, market timing. Sometimes you can; often
  you can't on the timeline you assumed.
- **"I'll just sell at break-even"** — break-even
  assumes the buyer side. Markets can be soft for years.
- **"I'll cancel before the trial ends"** — most don't.
  Subscription business model assumes inertia.
- **"I'll just stop"** — addictions, signed commitments,
  ongoing accountabilities don't always have a clean
  stop.
- **"I'll find another job if this doesn't work"** —
  during a downturn, sometimes you can't.

Treat pseudo-reversible as **slower than it claims**. If
the reversibility depends on uncertain external
conditions, factor that in.

## Stakes

Independent dimension from reversibility. How much are
you risking in absolute / proportional terms?

### Absolute stakes

- $500: small for most.
- $5,000: moderate; not catastrophic.
- $50,000: substantial; a year of savings for many.
- $500,000+: life-shaping; mortgage / home / college /
  retirement-ending if wrong.

### Proportional stakes

A $5,000 decision is moderate to someone with $100,000
saved; nearly catastrophic to someone with $5,000 saved.

Calculate: **decision cost / liquid net worth**. Above
20% is substantial; above 50% is high-stakes.

### Cashflow stakes vs. balance stakes

- **Cashflow:** ongoing monthly commitment. A $200/month
  car payment is small absolute cashflow; if it commits
  you for 6 years it locks $14,400.
- **Balance:** one-time. $14,000 cash purchase.

Both matter. Don't conflate.

## The 2x2 grid

Combining reversibility and stakes:

|  | Low stakes | High stakes |
|---|---|---|
| **Reversible** | Decide fast | Decide carefully but soon |
| **Irreversible** | Pay attention | Slow down; get help |

- **Low stakes + reversible:** sweat-of-decision exceeds
  cost-of-wrong. Just decide.
- **Low stakes + irreversible:** unusual combo. Often
  reflects category error — usually not really
  irreversible at low stakes.
- **High stakes + reversible:** worth real thought, but
  the reversibility is your safety net. Don't paralyse.
- **High stakes + irreversible:** the danger zone.
  Slow down; involve professionals; stress-test.

## How to test irreversibility honestly

Before assuming a decision is reversible:

### 1. Name the exit

How specifically would you reverse? "I'd sell" — at
what price, in what market, with what fees, on what
timeline?

If you can't answer specifically, the reversibility is
imaginary.

### 2. Name the trigger

Under what circumstances would you reverse? "If I lose
my job" — fine, but is the exit available exactly when
you'd most need it? Often the exit is hardest exactly
when you most need it.

### 3. Estimate the cost

Specific number for the exit cost. Transaction fees +
opportunity cost + relocation cost + lifestyle
adjustment cost.

### 4. Stress-test the timeline

Can you wait the time the exit takes? "I'll sell if it
doesn't work" — selling a house takes 3-9 months in a
normal market, sometimes years in a soft one.

### 5. Test the assumption

Talk to one person who recently exited. The exit they
describe is often slower / costlier / messier than you
imagined.

## How stakes affect rigour

For each stakes level, different rigour:

### Low stakes ($100s-low $1000s)

- Quick check: is this in budget?
- Decide; live with it.

### Moderate stakes (mid $1000s-$10K)

- Sleep on it.
- Compare to one alternative (the do-nothing).
- Talk with a partner / trusted friend.

### Substantial stakes ($10K-$100K)

- Get specific numbers (not estimates).
- Stress-test downside.
- Consider professional input.

### Life-shaping stakes ($100K+)

- Multiple weeks / months of consideration.
- Multiple professional inputs (financial advisor,
  tax pro, lawyer where relevant).
- Spousal / family alignment.
- "What if everything I'm assuming is wrong" exercise.

The mistake: applying low-stakes rigour to substantial
decisions ("we'll figure it out as we go") or
substantial-stakes rigour to low ones (paralysis over a
$200 purchase).

## Anti-patterns

### Confusing optimization with adequacy

You spend weeks finding the "best" option for a
two-way-door decision. Cost: weeks of life. Benefit:
modest improvement over "good enough" alternative.
Two-way doors don't earn the optimisation budget.

### Ignoring reversibility for high-EV decisions

"This has positive expected value" → ignore the
catastrophic worst-case. Variance kills before mean
helps. Especially for one-way doors.

### Treating reversibility as escape hatch

The escape hatch exists; you act as if its existence
makes the cost zero. The hatch has its own cost.

### Decision under duress treated as decision

Eviction notice + 7-day deadline → "decision" to take
predatory loan. This isn't a decision; it's coercion.
Surface the duress; consider whether the time-pressure
is real or constructed.

### Sunk-cost-pretending-to-be-reversibility

"I've already invested $X; if I exit now I lose it." The
$X is gone either way; reversibility is about the cost
from now-forward.

### Group-think reversibility

"Everyone says you can always sell a house." Everyone
hasn't tried recently in your market.

## Worked example — buying a home

- **Reversibility:** low. Selling costs ~6-10% of value
  + uncertain timing + tax events.
- **Stakes:** very high (often largest single
  purchase).
- **Pseudo-reversibility checks:** "I'll refinance if
  rates drop" — depends on your credit + market;
  "I'll sell if life changes" — sales take months,
  market may be soft; "I'll rent it out" — different
  business; landlord expertise; possibly tax events.

Implication: this is firmly high-stakes irreversible.
Slow decision; multiple professional inputs; spousal
alignment; stress-test.

## Worked example — switching jobs (with money
implications)

- **Reversibility:** medium-to-high in normal labour
  markets; low in downturns or specialised fields.
- **Stakes:** medium-to-high (your income for years;
  potentially career trajectory).
- **Pseudo-reversibility:** "I can always go back" —
  depends on labour market timing.

Implication: medium-rigour decision in normal markets;
high-rigour in tight markets. Beyond pure money: career
fit, growth, life balance. Often warrants
`decision-frame` from basic.

## Worked example — buying an expensive consumer good

- **Reversibility:** high (resell or use until end).
- **Stakes:** moderate (% of liquid net worth varies).
- **Pseudo-reversibility:** real — used markets exist for
  most consumer goods.

Implication: low-rigour decision. Sleep-on-it threshold;
beyond that, just decide.

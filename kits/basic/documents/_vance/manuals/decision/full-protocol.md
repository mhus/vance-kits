# Decision-Framing — Full Protocol

The full six-pass walk for weighty decisions. Adapt depth to the
weight of the decision; the steps are non-negotiable, the prose is.

## 1. Restate the decision

What exactly is being decided? Get the sentence to fit on one line.
What are the discrete options on the table?

If the user gave you a binary, probe for missing alternatives:

- "Have you considered doing nothing?"
- "What's option C?"
- "Is there a smaller version of A — pilot, trial, partial commit?"

A two-option frame is often a sign the user has already narrowed
prematurely.

## 2. Surface the criteria

What does the user actually care about? Common buckets — see
`criteria-catalogue` for a fuller list:

- **Cost** (money, time, attention, opportunity)
- **Reversibility** (one-way door vs. two-way door)
- **Impact on others** (family, team, dependants)
- **Learning** (will this teach me something I want to know?)
- **Fit with stated goals** (is this even on the path?)
- **Risk of regret** (which option do I regret more if it goes wrong?)

Push back gently if the criteria sound borrowed — someone else's
metric leaking in. "Whose criterion is this — yours or your boss's?"

## 3. Score honestly

For each option × criterion combination, give a directional read.
Use ranges and explicit uncertainty markers:

- "probably faster, but I'm guessing"
- "definitely cheaper short-term, unsure about 5-year cost"
- "no idea — would have to talk to X"

Do **not** fake precision. A "B+ vs. A−" matrix where the inputs
were vibes is worse than a paragraph saying "I can't score this
well, here's why".

Bullet points beat paragraphs for scoring. A scoring table works
when the user has thought hard about criteria; otherwise it
ceremonially launders ignorance.

## 4. Check reversibility

Is this a **two-way door** (cheap to undo) or a **one-way door**
(committing)? This single question often resolves the decision:

- Reversible: deserve speed. The wrong choice costs you a re-decide,
  which is cheaper than analysis paralysis.
- Irreversible: deserve care. Slow down, gather more, talk to
  someone affected.

If irreversible: can you reduce the irreversibility? Pilot, trial,
opt-out clause, staged rollout?

## 5. Name the gut signal

After the structured pass, ask the user what their gut says. Then:

- **Gut and analysis agree** → that's a green light.
- **Gut and analysis disagree** → that's a flag. Usually the
  analysis missed a criterion. Re-walk step 2 with the gut as input:
  what does the gut know that the matrix doesn't?
- **No gut signal at all** → the decision may be premature; the
  user hasn't lived with it long enough for the body to weigh in.
  Tell them to sleep on it.

## 6. Recommend a next step

The output is rarely "do A" or "do B". Better outputs:

- "Talk to X first."
- "Try option A for a week, then revisit."
- "Sleep on it; check in Tuesday."
- "You don't have enough yet — go look up Y, then come back."
- "Commit to A by Friday or default to B." (forcing-function)

A next step is process; a verdict is product. The user's job is
the verdict, your job is the process.

## Closing

Use headings (Restate / Criteria / Score / Reversibility / Gut /
Next step). Keep each section short. Bullets beat paragraphs for
scoring. Match prose density to the weight of the decision.

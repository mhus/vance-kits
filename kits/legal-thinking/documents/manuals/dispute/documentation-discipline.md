# Documentation Discipline — records that hold up

A dispute often unfolds over months. Your memory at
month 6 won't match what happened at month 1.
Documentation discipline preserves what actually
happened so the eventual decision (settle, escalate,
abandon) can rest on facts not reconstruction.

## Start before you "need to"

The single most useful documentation rule: **start
recording when something feels off**, not after the
formal dispute begins.

Reasons:

- Contemporaneous records are vastly stronger than
  reconstruction.
- The early signs often turn out to be the pattern;
  later events make sense in light of them.
- Starting late means losing the foundation.

If you have any sense that a situation might
escalate, start the record. Quick notes; emails saved;
photos when relevant. Better to have it and not need
it than need it and not have it.

## What to document

### What was said

- In conversations, immediate notes after.
- In meetings, formal minutes if appropriate.
- Specific quotes, attributed.
- Date, time, who was present.

If recording is legal in your jurisdiction (one-party
or all-party consent — check carefully), recording
serves as backup. Otherwise: written notes ASAP.

### What was written

Save:

- Emails (full thread, including headers).
- Text messages (screenshots; some platforms allow
  export).
- Letters (originals if possible; scans).
- Memos, official documents.
- Performance reviews, feedback documents (in
  employment).
- Contracts, agreements, lease documents.

Don't rely on platforms to keep these for you.
Download / forward / screenshot.

### What was done

Actions, not just words:

- Date X happened.
- What was scheduled but didn't happen.
- What was promised vs. delivered.
- What was paid; what wasn't.

### What was missed / promised / changed

Promises broken; deadlines missed; conditions changed.

- "Manager promised: training programme to be
  scheduled by April 1. As of April 30, no
  scheduling has occurred."

- "Lease specified maintenance turnaround: 7 days.
  Repair request submitted March 5; not
  addressed as of March 18."

These are factual statements about gaps.

### What you yourself did

- Communications you sent.
- Steps you took to address issues.
- Date you escalated.
- Date you sought outside input.

A complete picture includes both sides of the
exchange.

## Format that works

For each event, capture:

```
[YYYY-MM-DD] [Time]
What happened:
Who was present:
What was said (quoted where possible):
Documents / evidence references:
What I did in response:
Open questions / unverified inferences:
```

Plain text is fine. Searchable digital format better
than paper for long-running disputes.

## Capture quickly

The hardest part: documenting in the moment when
you're stressed.

Quick-capture techniques:

- **Email yourself.** Subject: date + topic. Body:
  what happened. Sent immediately.
- **Voice memo.** Record yourself describing the
  event. Transcribe later.
- **Short note in dedicated file.** Open file; write
  3 lines; close. Detail later.
- **Save the email / text** before doing anything
  else.

Imperfect but immediate beats perfect but delayed.
Fix the formatting later; capture the substance now.

## What evidence holds up

### Strong

- **Contemporaneous documents** with metadata
  (emails with headers, text exports with
  timestamps).
- **Formal records** (contracts, written warnings,
  certified mail receipts).
- **Witnesses** with independent recall + contact
  info.
- **Photos / videos** with timestamps + EXIF data.
- **Third-party records** (bank transactions,
  delivery confirmations, login records you can
  request).

### Medium

- **Notes you wrote contemporaneously**.
- **Witnesses who'll confirm in writing**.
- **Patterns from multiple events** (no single one
  conclusive but together suggest pattern).

### Weak

- **Reconstructed memory.**
- **Single-witness accounts where the witness has a
  stake.**
- **Inferences without supporting evidence.**
- **Generic claims** without specific dates / facts.

The goal: convert weak evidence to medium /
strong by adding contemporaneous documentation.

## Maintaining the record

### Append-only

Don't edit past entries. If you need to add
context / correction, write a new entry that
references the prior:

> "[2026-04-15 update]: re my note from 2026-03-10
> about the meeting time — I've now confirmed it was
> 3pm not 4pm based on calendar entry I located."

Editing creates "did they really write that
originally?" doubts. Append-only preserves
credibility.

### Backup

Keep records in multiple places:

- Cloud backup (Google Drive, Dropbox, etc.).
- Local backup (your machine).
- Print critical items + store offline.

Loss of records when you need them is catastrophic
and avoidable.

### Privacy and security

Records often contain sensitive information.

- Use password / encrypted storage.
- Don't share with people who don't need.
- For high-stakes disputes (employment, legal):
  consider attorney-managed storage (work product
  privilege).

### Don't share prematurely

In active disputes, sharing your records with the
other side can:

- Reveal your strategy.
- Be subject to discovery.
- Provide them time to respond / counter.

Generally: hold records until your attorney advises
sharing, or until formal process requires it.

For informal disputes (neighbour, vendor) without
litigation potential: less sensitive; share what
serves resolution.

## When to start a formal log

A formal log (organised, indexed, comprehensive) is
warranted when:

- Disagreement has become persistent.
- Pattern is emerging across events.
- You're considering escalation.
- The other side has begun documenting.
- An attorney has advised it.
- You expect or are facing legal action.

Before formal:

- Quick notes, saved emails, photos.

After formal:

- Indexed log with chronology, summary, evidence
  references.

The transition often coincides with attorney
involvement or considering attorney involvement.

## Specific document types

### Demand letters / formal communications

If you send formal communication:

- Send via method that proves delivery (certified
  mail, registered email, with read receipt).
- Keep copy of what was sent + delivery
  confirmation.
- For major matters: have attorney draft. Layperson-
  drafted demand letters can backfire.

### Internal complaints

If you make formal internal complaint (HR
complaint, regulatory complaint, etc.):

- Keep copy of submission.
- Note submission date.
- Keep response (or note non-response).
- Note follow-up dates.

### Settlement discussions

Often privileged ("settlement communications" —
varies by jurisdiction). Generally not used as
evidence in court if the matter doesn't settle.

But: discuss with attorney whether your specific
discussions are protected.

### Recordings

If legally permitted in your jurisdiction:

- Note recording date, all-parties consent (where
  needed).
- Save original file.
- Transcript helps for later reference.

If NOT legally permitted (without consent in your
jurisdiction): don't. The recording is unusable +
you may face liability.

## Record-keeping privilege issues

In litigation, your records may be discoverable. This
includes:

- Notes you thought were private.
- Texts to friends / family.
- Diary / journal entries.
- Therapist notes (sometimes; protections exist but
  vary).

What's protected:

- Communications with your attorney (attorney-client
  privilege).
- Documents prepared at attorney's direction
  (attorney work product).
- Spousal / clergy / certain therapist communications
  (varies).

For substantial disputes, ask attorney about
privilege early. The "I thought my journal was
private" surprise is real.

## Keep records of resolution

When the dispute concludes:

- **Settlement document** (if any) — signed
  agreement; what was waived; what was paid; what
  obligations remain.
- **Resolution timeline** — when did the relevant
  events occur.
- **Things still ongoing** — sometimes settlements
  include continuing obligations (non-disparagement,
  confidentiality, payment schedule).

Even after resolution, keep records — disputes
sometimes resurface, and the documented resolution
is your protection.

## Anti-patterns

### "I'll remember"

You won't; not in detail. Document.

### Edit the past

Going back to "fix" old notes. Always append; never
edit. Editing destroys credibility.

### Document everything

Excessive documentation of minor events is its own
problem — your records become noisy and unsearchable.
Focus on what's relevant to the dispute.

### Document only the bad

If you only record their failures, not your own
mistakes / acknowledgement of theirs / their proper
behaviour, the record is one-sided. A neutral record
is more credible.

### Sharing your records casually

Especially in active dispute: don't share with people
who might pass them along. Even well-intentioned
friends.

### Recording without consent (where required)

You record a meeting in a two-party-consent
jurisdiction without the other party knowing. The
recording is unusable + you may face criminal /
civil liability.

### Burying records

Disorganised collection across email / texts /
random files. When you need to pull together quickly
(for attorney, for hearing), can't.

Counter: even modest organisation (one folder;
chronological filenames) pays back enormously when
needed.

## Output line

- "**Records started / maintained.** Status:
  ⟨specific⟩."
- "**Documentation gap detected:** ⟨specific⟩."
- "**Discoverability concern surfaced** — review with
  attorney if substantial."
- "**Beyond layperson record-keeping** — direct to
  attorney for guidance on privilege /
  documentation strategy."

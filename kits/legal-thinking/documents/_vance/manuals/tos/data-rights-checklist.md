# Data Rights Checklist — what to look for in privacy policies

A privacy policy describes:

- What data the service collects.
- Why and how they use it.
- Who they share with.
- How long they keep it.
- What rights you have (access, correction, deletion).

Reading help: walk the checklist; surface what
matters; let the user decide.

**Important: privacy laws vary substantially by
jurisdiction. GDPR (EU), CCPA / CPRA (California),
PIPEDA (Canada), LGPD (Brazil), and others give
different rights. Generic reading is informative;
specific enforceability is jurisdictional.**

## Collection scope

What data do they collect?

### Account / identity data

- Name, email, phone, address.
- Date of birth.
- Government ID (sometimes for KYC).

Standard for any account-based service. Watch the
specifics:

- Required vs. optional?
- Verified (e.g. real name + ID) vs.
  self-reported?

### Usage data

- Pages visited, features used, time spent.
- Click-stream, search queries.
- App-launch frequency, session length.

Almost universal in web / app services.

### Device data

- IP address.
- Browser / OS / device model.
- Hardware fingerprint (sometimes).

Standard.

### Location

- Coarse (city / country from IP).
- Precise (GPS, when permitted).
- Inferred (based on other signals).

Watch for: precise location collected without obvious
necessity. A photo-sharing app needing precise
location all the time is suspicious.

### Communications

- Messages between users.
- Emails to support.
- Chat with AI.

Watch for: scope. Some services keep all messages
forever; others delete after reasonable time.

### Content uploaded / created

- Photos, videos, documents.
- Posts, comments, reviews.
- AI prompts and outputs (modern AI services).

Watch for: who owns this? See
`what-changes-when-you-click` for content licenses.

### Biometric data

- Face data (for unlock or verification).
- Voice data (for assistants).
- Fingerprint (some apps).

Heightened concern. Many jurisdictions classify as
sensitive.

### Inferred / derived data

What they infer about you based on other data:

- Demographics inferred from behaviour.
- Interests inferred from browsing.
- Mood / emotional state inferred (some services).
- Health-adjacent inferences (some platforms).

Often missing from policy disclosures; sometimes
mentioned in vague terms.

### Third-party data

Data they buy or receive from other parties:

- Marketing lists.
- Data brokers.
- Partner services.

Watch for: language about "from our partners" /
"public sources" — they may be augmenting your
profile with data you didn't directly provide.

## Use / purpose

What do they do with the data?

### Service operation

Standard. Provide the service you signed up for.

### Personalisation

Recommend content based on your behaviour.

Most services do this. Often beneficial; sometimes
manipulative.

### Service improvement

Aggregate / anonymous use to improve the product.

Standard. Often vague — "anonymous" varies in
strength.

### Security / fraud detection

Monitor for abuse, fake accounts, fraud.

Standard.

### Marketing — own products

Send you marketing about other products from the
same company.

Standard. Usually opt-out exists.

### Marketing — third parties

Sharing your data with other companies who market to
you.

Watch for: "share with our partners for marketing".
This is often where the data ecosystem starts
spreading your information.

### AI / model training

Modern concern. Is your content / queries / data
used to train AI models?

Watch for:

- Explicit consent vs. assumed-by-use.
- Opt-out availability.
- Whether your specific data persists in trained
  models (irreversible).

For sensitive data (health, financial, work-related),
training-on-data clauses warrant attention.

### Sale / monetisation

Some companies sell data to third parties.

Watch for:

- Clear "we don't sell data" claims (look for
  carve-outs).
- "Sale" defined in jurisdiction-specific way (CCPA
  has specific definition that's broader than common
  usage).

## Sharing / third parties

Who do they share with?

### Service providers

Companies they hire to operate (cloud hosting, email
delivery, analytics).

Standard. Watch for: scope of what's shared with
each.

### Affiliates

Companies in the same corporate family.

Standard but worth knowing about. Affiliate sharing
can substantially extend who has the data.

### Marketing partners

Companies they share with for advertising / marketing
purposes.

Watch for: opt-out availability.

### Government / law enforcement

Standard clause about complying with legal process,
subpoenas, court orders.

Watch for: scope of voluntary disclosure ("we may
share with law enforcement to investigate violations
of our terms").

### Business transfers

In M&A, your data goes to the acquirer.

Standard. Worth knowing if you care about a specific
acquirer.

### Public

Some content is public by design (posts, profiles).

Watch for: defaults. Are profiles public-by-default?
Posts public unless made private?

## Retention

How long do they keep your data?

### "As long as needed"

Vague. Often means indefinitely.

### Specific time periods

Better. "X years after account closure" gives a
specific window.

### Tied to legal obligations

"As required by law" + specific time periods. Some
jurisdictions require certain data retention.

### Deletion on account close

Sometimes promised; sometimes with carve-outs (legal
obligations, backup retention).

Watch for: backup retention. Even after "deletion",
data often persists in backups for months / years.

## Your rights

Depends heavily on jurisdiction. Common rights:

### Access

You can request a copy of data they have on you.

### Correction

You can correct inaccurate data.

### Deletion

You can request deletion of your data.

### Portability

You can request data in machine-readable format to
take elsewhere.

### Opt-out

You can opt out of marketing, sale of data, certain
uses.

### Withdraw consent

You can withdraw consent for processing requiring
consent.

In jurisdictions with strong rights (GDPR, CCPA):
exercising these is typically possible via specific
form / email / dashboard.

In jurisdictions with weaker rights: the policy may
say you have rights but mechanisms are weaker.

Watch for:

- **How to exercise.** Specific email / form
  required? Deadline for company response? Identity
  verification process?
- **Carve-outs.** "Subject to legal obligations" —
  some data may be retained despite deletion request.

### Children's data

Special protections in many jurisdictions:

- COPPA (US, under-13).
- GDPR special protections for under-16.
- Various others.

If the user is checking on behalf of a child, watch
for these provisions specifically.

## Cookie / tracking-tech specifics

Modern privacy policies often include cookie /
tracking-tech sections.

Watch for:

- **Strictly necessary cookies** — required for
  service.
- **Performance / analytics cookies** — service
  improvement.
- **Advertising cookies** — for targeted ads.
- **Third-party cookies** — set by other companies
  through this site.

Many jurisdictions (EU especially) require explicit
consent for non-essential cookies. The presence of
the consent banner is the easy part; the practical
opt-out is sometimes hidden.

## International data transfer

If you're in one country and the company is in
another, your data crosses borders.

Watch for:

- Specific countries listed.
- Adequacy decisions / SCCs / DPF (technical
  jurisdiction-level mechanisms).
- Data transferred to countries with weaker
  protections.

For most consumer use: this is opaque. For
business / regulated use, specific input from
privacy professional is warranted.

## Specific high-attention scenarios

### Health-adjacent services

Fitness apps, mental-health apps, period trackers,
sleep trackers, healthcare-adjacent platforms.

Privacy stakes are higher. Look for:

- HIPAA compliance claims (US, often weaker than
  expected — most consumer apps are NOT covered
  entities).
- Specific protections for health data.
- Whether fitness data flows to insurers / employers.

### Children's services

Already covered above. Substantial regulatory
attention. If you're managing a child's account,
read carefully.

### Financial services

Banks, investment apps, money-transfer services.

Often have stronger regulations + more sensitive
data. Specific privacy attention warranted.

### Workplace tools

Software your employer uses (HR systems, productivity
trackers, communication platforms).

You may have less control than over personal services.
Employer's contract with the vendor may govern; your
employment context governs further.

If concerned, ask HR / employer; sometimes better
options exist.

### AI / chat services

Modern concern. Your conversations / prompts may be:

- Used for model training.
- Reviewed by humans for quality / safety.
- Stored for indefinite analysis.

Watch the policy specifically. Reasonable services
provide opt-out; some don't.

For sensitive content, even with opt-out, consider
whether the service is appropriate.

## How to do the parse

For any policy:

1. **What's the scope of data collection?**
   (one-paragraph summary)
2. **What's the most concerning use I see?**
   (specific clause)
3. **What sharing is most concerning?**
   (specific party / purpose)
4. **How long is data kept?**
   (specific or indefinite)
5. **Can I delete?**
   (mechanism + carve-outs)

Five questions; usually 15-20 minutes for a typical
policy. Long enough to find issues; short enough to
actually do.

## Anti-patterns

### Skip-and-click

Most users do this. Sometimes it's the only practical
choice. For high-stakes services (health, financial,
children's), force a real read.

### Trusting the summary

Some services provide a "highlights" or "summary at
a glance". These are often genuinely helpful but
sometimes hide controversial provisions. Cross-check
with the actual policy.

### Outdated read

Policies change. Reading once and assuming
stable is an error. Watch for change-notifications;
reread on substantial changes.

### Comparing to "the standard"

There's no "standard" privacy policy. Each company
makes its own choices. Compare to your concerns, not
to other services' policies.

### Mistaking declared protection for actual

"We never sell your data" — sometimes true; sometimes
sale-as-defined-by-CCPA is happening anyway. The
declared and actual practice can differ; relying on
declared without scrutiny can mislead.

## When to escalate

- **Substantial business / regulatory exposure:**
  privacy professional / attorney.
- **Children's data with concerns:** privacy
  professional.
- **Health data with concerns:** healthcare-savvy
  attorney.
- **You're being asked to consent to something
  unusual:** at minimum, get a second opinion.

For ordinary consumer services: layperson reading is
the practical level.

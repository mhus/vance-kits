# Liability and Arbitration — what you give up by clicking

ToS / EULAs commonly include clauses that limit your
recourse if something goes wrong. These clauses are
where the most consequential surprises live.

This manual walks the standard patterns. Same caveat as
elsewhere: **this is reading help, not legal advice.
For substantial concerns, consult an attorney in your
jurisdiction.**

## Mandatory arbitration

The most consequential clause in modern ToS. You
waive your right to court; disputes go to arbitration.

### What this means

- **No court.** Disputes resolved by a private
  arbitrator instead of a judge.
- **No jury.** Arbitrator decides facts and law.
- **Limited appeals.** Arbitration awards are
  difficult to appeal.
- **Often confidential.** Arbitration proceedings
  not public.
- **Sometimes faster than court.**
- **Sometimes worse outcomes for the claimant
  (you).** Studies show arbitration often less
  favourable to consumers / employees than court
  for similar facts.

### Class action waiver

Often paired with arbitration:

> "You waive any right to participate in a class
> action..."

This means: small individual claims have no economic
recourse. A $50 grievance can't be aggregated with
millions of others; individually, $50 isn't worth
arbitration costs.

Class waivers are deliberately exclusionary of small
claims. Some jurisdictions limit enforceability;
generally enforceable in the US after key SCOTUS
decisions.

### Arbitration cost

The arbitration provider charges fees. ToS sometimes:

- Require you to share costs.
- Cap fees at "consumer-friendly" levels (e.g., AAA
  consumer rules cap fees).
- Attempt to make you pay all fees.

Fees not capped at consumer-friendly levels can be a
real barrier — arbitration of a $500 claim might cost
$2000 in fees.

### Arbitration provider selection

The ToS often names specific provider (AAA, JAMS,
NAM). Some providers have track records of
industry-favourable outcomes. The selection matters.

### Carve-outs

Some claims are typically excluded from mandatory
arbitration:

- Small claims court.
- Injunctive relief (sometimes).
- IP claims (often defined favourably to drafter).

Worth identifying which claims you can't bring in
arbitration vs. which you can.

### What you can do

- **Read carefully.** Note the arbitration provider
  + applicable rules + cost structure.
- **Opt-out window.** Some arbitration clauses
  include "you can opt out within 30 days of
  agreeing". Often done by mailed letter. Worth
  taking advantage of for substantial services.
- **In employment contexts:** non-negotiable in many
  cases; arbitration is widespread.

## Limitation of liability

Caps on what the company can owe you.

### Common patterns

- "In no event shall [Company] be liable for...".
- "Aggregate liability shall not exceed [$X]" or
  "fees paid in last 12 months".
- "Excluded categories: indirect, consequential,
  incidental, special, punitive damages."

The combination: even if they cause you significant
loss, they owe you a small amount.

### Why companies do this

- Predictable risk exposure.
- Insurance pricing.
- Investment-attracting (less tail risk).

Why it limits you:

- A service that causes substantial damage may owe
  little.
- Categories of damages most likely (lost profits,
  business interruption) often excluded.

### Carve-outs

Sometimes the cap doesn't apply to:

- Intentional misconduct / gross negligence.
- IP infringement claims.
- Confidentiality breaches.
- Indemnification obligations (the company
  indemnifying you).
- Privacy / data breach in some jurisdictions.

The carve-out structure determines what's actually
capped.

### What you can do

- For high-stakes business use: negotiate cap upward
  + carve-outs.
- For consumer use: cap is rarely negotiable; factor
  into your usage decision.

## Indemnification

You agree to defend / pay damages for claims
arising from your use of the service.

### Common pattern

> "You agree to indemnify, defend, and hold harmless
> [Company] from any claims arising from your use
> of the Service, including [list]..."

The list often includes:

- Your violation of the ToS.
- Your violation of laws.
- Your content / data uploaded.
- Your interaction with other users.

### Why this matters

If the company gets sued because of your behaviour
on their platform, you potentially owe defence costs
+ damages.

### Mutual indemnification

Some contracts (especially B2B) have mutual
indemnification — each party indemnifies the other
for their own misconduct.

ToS often only one-way (you indemnify them).

### Limits

Some indemnification clauses are bounded:

- Specific events only (your violation of laws, not
  general use).
- Capped amounts.
- Notice requirements before invoking.

Many ToS indemnifications are unbounded. The
exposure can be substantial.

### What you can do

- For consumer use: scope is usually bounded by your
  actual conduct (don't violate the ToS, don't
  upload IP-infringing content, etc.).
- For business use with employees / multi-user
  accounts: review carefully; consider attorney.

## Choice of law / forum

Specifies which law governs and where disputes happen.

### Common pattern

- Law of [drafter's home state / country].
- Forum: [drafter's home location].

### Why this matters

If you're in one jurisdiction and the contract
specifies another:

- Litigation requires you to travel / hire counsel
  remote.
- Different substantive law applies.
- Local consumer protections may not apply.

For example: US consumer in California signing ToS
that specifies Delaware law and New York forum.
Effectively reduces practical recourse.

### What you can do

- For consumer ToS: rarely negotiable.
- For business agreements: negotiate to neutral
  jurisdiction or your favour.
- Some jurisdictions (especially EU) limit the
  enforceability of foreign forum / law clauses
  for consumers.

## Acceptance / amendment

How can the ToS change after you sign?

### Common pattern

> "We may modify these terms at any time. Continued
> use of the service constitutes acceptance of the
> modified terms."

### Why this matters

The contract you "agreed to" can change. You may have
no notice; you may have notice but no real choice
(stop using or accept).

### Variations

- **Email notification + grace period before changes
  apply:** more user-friendly.
- **Posted on website only:** weaker.
- **Material changes notified vs. all changes:**
  varies.

### What you can do

- Subscribe to ToS-change notifications where
  available.
- Read change notifications when received (not
  automatic).
- Cancel before changes take effect if you object.

## Account termination

The company's rights to terminate your account.

### Common patterns

- Termination "at any time, for any reason or no
  reason".
- Termination for ToS violations.
- Termination on inactivity.

### Why this matters

Your access to the service can end:

- Without warning.
- Without recourse.
- Sometimes with consequences (loss of stored data,
  digital purchases, business continuity).

### Data on termination

Watch for:

- **Data export rights** — can you take your data
  with you?
- **Notice before deletion** — how long to retrieve?
- **Refund policies** — partial / full / none.

For services with substantial data (creative work,
business operations, financial records), termination
without data export is a serious risk.

### What you can do

- Maintain backups outside the service.
- Read account-suspension provisions before relying
  heavily.
- For business use: consider data-export and
  termination clauses heavily.

## Warranty disclaimers

The "AS IS" clause:

> "The service is provided 'AS IS' without warranties
> of any kind."

### What this means

- The service may have bugs / outages / errors.
- The company doesn't promise specific functionality
  or quality.
- You bear the risk of your reliance.

### Limits

Some jurisdictions (especially EU) limit the ability
to disclaim implied warranties for consumers.
Enforceability of broad "AS IS" varies.

For business use, "AS IS" is significant. For
consumer use, generally treated as routine but real.

## Combination effects

The clauses combine:

- **Mandatory arbitration + class waiver + remote
  forum:** practical access to recourse very limited.
- **Liability cap + indemnification + warranty
  disclaimer:** company exposure minimised; your
  exposure maximised.
- **Unilateral modification + at-will termination
  + no-warranty:** your "agreement" is rewritable
  by them.

When several appear together, the asymmetry
amplifies. This is structurally how adhesion contracts
work.

## When ToS issues warrant attention

For most consumer services:

- Read once.
- Note the major patterns.
- Decide use vs. no.

For business / regulated / high-stakes:

- Attorney review.
- Negotiation if possible.
- Alternative service evaluation if not.

For employment ToS (employer software, BYOD policies,
etc.):

- Read.
- Understand what data your employer can access.
- Know whether you can opt out / use alternative.

## Anti-patterns

### Click-and-go

The default. Sometimes the only practical option.
Don't pretend you read; do read for high-stakes
services.

### Trusting the marketing

The product page emphasises benefits; the ToS
emphasises risks. The two together are the deal;
reading only one is incomplete.

### Treating arbitration as harmless

Mandatory arbitration with class waivers is
substantively important. The "you can still get
help" framing can underplay it.

### Accepting unbounded indemnification

For business use, an unbounded indemnification can
be substantial financial exposure. Worth caps and
carve-outs.

### Skipping the changes

Continued use = acceptance of changes. Watch for
notifications; read them; act if needed.

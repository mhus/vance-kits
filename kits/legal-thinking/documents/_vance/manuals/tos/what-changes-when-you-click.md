# What Changes When You Click — broad rights you may grant

The "I agree" click can grant rights you might not
expect. This manual catalogues the broad rights
commonly embedded in ToS / EULAs that go beyond simple
"use this service".

## Content licenses

You upload content (photos, text, code, video,
documents). The ToS often grants the company a
license to use it.

### Common patterns

> "You grant [Company] a worldwide, perpetual,
> royalty-free, non-exclusive, sublicensable license
> to use, reproduce, distribute, display, and create
> derivative works..."

### What each part means

- **Worldwide:** geographic scope.
- **Perpetual:** doesn't end when you stop using
  the service or close your account.
- **Royalty-free:** they don't pay you.
- **Non-exclusive:** you can still license to
  others.
- **Sublicensable:** they can grant rights to third
  parties.
- **Reproduce / distribute / display:** they can
  use the content publicly.
- **Derivative works:** they can modify and create
  new works from yours.

### Common variations

#### Limited to "operating the service"

Better. License only for what's needed to make the
service work.

> "...solely for the purposes of providing,
> improving, and promoting the Service."

#### Termination on account close

Better. License ends when you leave.

#### Public-facing exception

Some services (social platforms) license publicly-
shared content broadly; private content less so.

### Why companies want broad licenses

- Operational flexibility (CDN, backup, search,
  recommendations).
- Marketing (showing your content as examples).
- Service improvement (training models on your
  content).
- Potential business uses (their published collections,
  partnerships).

The legitimate use is operational. The broader
licensing covers the optional and the
not-yet-imagined.

### What this means for you

For consumer use of mainstream services: broad licenses
exist; in practice the company doesn't usually exploit
them in ways you'd notice; legally they could.

For substantial creative content / business content:
the licensing matters more. Scrutinise.

For AI training: this is current frontier. Whether
your content trains AI models, whether opt-out exists,
whether the company commercialises model outputs
based on your inputs — varies.

## Content moderation rights

The company's rights to remove your content / suspend
your account.

### Common patterns

- "We may remove content that violates our policies."
- "We may suspend your account at our sole
  discretion."
- "We reserve the right to refuse service to anyone."

### What this means

- You can lose access to your content / account.
- Often without warning or detailed explanation.
- Recourse may be limited (some platforms have
  appeal processes; many don't).

### Variations

- Specific policies that constrain moderation
  decisions.
- Notice / appeal process with timelines.
- "At our sole discretion" with no constraint.

For platforms central to your business / livelihood
(creator platforms, marketplaces), the moderation
rights matter substantially. Single-platform
dependency creates risk.

## IP claims about you

Some ToS include claims about IP you bring to the
service:

> "You represent that you own or have permission to
> use any content you upload..."

This shifts liability to you for IP infringement.
Reasonable; broad indemnification for IP claims
follows from it.

But: watch for clauses that go further:

- "You grant the company an option to license your
  IP."
- "You agree that the company can register
  trademarks based on your contributions."

These are unusual and warrant attention.

## Behaviour requirements

ToS often includes requirements for how you can use
the service:

- No automation / scraping.
- No reverse engineering.
- No use for competitive analysis.
- Single account / no sharing.
- Specific rate limits (sometimes formal, sometimes
  technical).

### Why these matter

- Violation can trigger account termination.
- Violation can trigger ToS-based legal claims.
- Some clauses (like reverse engineering bans) may
  be unenforceable in some jurisdictions.

### What you can do

- Read.
- Comply or knowingly accept the risk.
- For substantial commercial use cases (especially
  scraping / API-like usage), get attorney input on
  whether the ToS allows what you want.

## Modification rights (theirs)

Many ToS allow the company to modify the agreement:

- Service features can change.
- Pricing can change.
- Terms can change (already covered above).

The downside: what you signed up for may not be what
you have a year later.

### What you can do

- Watch for change notices.
- Cancel before changes take effect if material.
- For business reliance: consider whether the
  service's modification rights are acceptable for
  your dependency.

## Account portability

What can you take with you?

### Data export

- Sometimes available (mature services).
- Sometimes only for paid tiers.
- Sometimes not at all.

### Settings / preferences

- Usually no portable export.
- Re-configure each time you switch.

### Social graph / connections

- Often not portable.
- Major lock-in for social services.

### Domain / handle ownership

- Some services let you keep your handle / username.
- Some reclaim handles after period of inactivity.

For services central to your identity / business,
portability matters. ToS often vague on this; reality
varies.

## Privacy commitments / dilutions

The ToS may modify what the privacy policy says:

- "Notwithstanding our privacy policy, you agree..."
  (rare but happens).
- "By using the service, you consent to data
  practices described in [privacy policy linked but
  not pasted into ToS]."

Read both ToS and privacy policy. They're often
separate documents; sometimes they conflict.

## Jurisdictional handling

Beyond choice of law / forum:

- **Tax obligations** in some cases.
- **Export control** compliance ("you agree not to
  use the service in countries subject to embargo").
- **Local law compliance** (your responsibility).

Not usually concerning for ordinary consumer use;
worth knowing about for business / international use.

## Payment / refund

- Auto-renewal terms.
- Refund availability (often "no refunds").
- Refund mechanism.
- Subscription management.

For paid services, the payment / refund terms often
substantively affect what you're really committed to.

## Other clauses worth knowing

### Class action ban

Already covered in
`liability-and-arbitration`.

### Governing copyright

Some platforms designate themselves as the proper
party for DMCA (US) and equivalent global
copyright takedown processes. Mostly procedural.

### Beta / preview terms

Some service tiers / features are "beta" with reduced
warranties. Read separately.

### Marketing consent

Often opt-in / opt-out for marketing communications.
The default varies.

## What's unusual and warrants attention

If you see any of these in a ToS:

- **Right to record / monitor your communications**
  beyond what the service requires.
- **Mandatory training data submission** for AI.
- **Right to take ownership of your IP** beyond
  licensing.
- **Right to use your name / likeness in marketing
  without further consent.**
- **Right to revoke your purchased content / digital
  goods.**
- **Mandatory employer disclosures** (in employment-
  related software).

These warrant attorney review or use-decline
depending on stakes.

## Reading approach

For a ToS:

1. **Skim length and structure.**
2. **Walk content licensing** — what do they get from
   what you upload?
3. **Walk moderation / termination rights** — what's
   your account exposure?
4. **Walk modification / amendment** — can the deal
   change?
5. **Walk dispute resolution** (already in
   `liability-and-arbitration`).
6. **List concerns.**

For most services, the balance is "use it knowing
what you've agreed to". For some, the answer is "no,
the cost is too high".

## When to refuse

Sometimes the ToS is clearly inappropriate:

- Broad licensing that goes beyond service operation
  for content you can't lose.
- Unbounded indemnification for personal use.
- Unusual rights that don't match the service's
  purpose.

For these: refuse use; find an alternative.

For most consumer services, the ToS is a known
package; you accept the package or don't.

## Anti-patterns

### Trust the marketing

The product looks great; the ToS is the small print.
Both are the deal.

### Treat all clauses as unimportant

Some clauses (mandatory arbitration, broad licenses)
are substantively important. Don't blanket-dismiss.

### Treat all clauses as critical

Most clauses are routine. Don't refuse every service
because the ToS is imperfect; everything has them.

### Skip what you don't understand

Vague clauses you don't understand are sometimes
exactly the ones to dig into. "What does this mean?"
is a real question; sometimes attorney input
clarifies.

### Believe "we don't actually do that"

The ToS allows X; the company says they don't actually
do X. They could; the legal authority is in the
agreement. The reassurance is good as long as it
lasts.

# Facts vs. Feelings — different artefacts

In a dispute, both facts (what happened) and feelings
(your emotional response) are real. They serve
different purposes:

- **Facts** are what you'll need to substantiate any
  formal claim, communicate with attorneys, or
  present to a third party.
- **Feelings** are part of your motivation, your
  decision about whether to proceed, and your
  ability to negotiate.

Mixing them produces:

- **Weak fact records** ("they were so rude" — what
  actually happened?).
- **Suppressed feelings** that come out at bad
  moments.
- **Communication that loses both audiences** — the
  fact-listener can't extract the facts; the
  empathy-listener feels lectured.

Separate them.

## Building the facts record

A facts record contains only what an outside observer
could verify.

### What goes in

- **Dates and times.** "On Tuesday, March 14, at
  about 3pm".
- **Specific actions.** "Landlord entered the
  apartment. The lease requires 24-hour notice. No
  notice was given."
- **Specific words spoken.** Quoted, with
  attribution. "Manager said: 'You're fired effective
  immediately. Pack your things.'"
- **Specific written communications.** Email chain;
  text exchanges; memos.
- **Specific witnesses.** Names of people present.
- **Specific physical evidence.** Photos, receipts,
  documents, video.

### What stays out

- "I was furious."
- "It was completely unfair."
- "They had no right to do that."
- "Anyone would have been upset."
- "It must have been their plan all along."

These are interpretations / feelings / assertions
about motive — important, but not facts.

### The third-party test

For each item, ask: would a neutral observer recording
this scene write the same thing?

- Observer would write: "Manager said X."
- Observer would not write: "Manager was being a jerk."

(Even if the manager was being a jerk; the observation
is that they said X. The "jerk" judgement is your
inference, not a fact.)

### Format that works

Chronological. Each entry:

```
[Date] [Time]
What happened (factually):
Who was present:
What was said (quoted where possible):
Documents / evidence:
```

Paper or digital both work; digital is searchable.

### When you don't remember exactly

Disputes often span months / years. You won't remember
exact times for everything.

What to do:

- **Note approximate** ("about 3pm", "early March").
- **Note your source of approximation** ("based on
  calendar entry", "I remember it was a Tuesday
  because...").
- **Don't invent precision.** "On Tuesday March 14 at
  3:07pm" when you don't actually know puts your
  whole record in question.

A record that's honest about uncertainty is stronger
than a record that fakes precision.

### Documents to capture

- Original contracts / agreements.
- Email and text threads (full, with headers /
  metadata where possible).
- Bills and receipts.
- Photos with timestamps.
- Audio / video recordings (subject to recording
  consent laws — varies by jurisdiction).
- Communication logs.
- Witnesses' contact info.

Capture as you go; reconstructing later loses metadata.

### What a neutral fact looks like

> "On April 3, 2026, I received an email from John
> Smith (manager) at 9:14am with subject 'Performance
> review schedule'. I responded the same day at 11:22am
> with the attached email confirming attendance. The
> meeting did not occur on the scheduled date; I went
> to the conference room at 2pm; no one was present. I
> emailed John at 2:30pm. He responded at 4:15pm
> saying the meeting was rescheduled to April 10. I
> have no prior notification of the reschedule."

Specific. Verifiable. Attached evidence (emails) is
referenceable. No interpretation.

## Building the feelings record

A feelings record is for you. It supports:

- Your decision-making (do I want to proceed?).
- Your wellbeing (processing the emotional weight).
- Your communication with people you trust (friends,
  partners, therapist).
- Your honest engagement with how this affects your
  life.

### What goes in

- "I felt humiliated when the meeting was missed
  without notice."
- "I'm worried this is part of a pattern of being
  excluded."
- "I'm angry at the way this was communicated."
- "I'm anxious about what happens next."

These are real. They matter. They aren't facts.

### Why a separate record

When you mix feelings into the fact record:

- The fact record reads as biased; observers
  discount it.
- The feelings get suppressed; come out at
  inappropriate moments.

When you keep them separate:

- The fact record stays clean; observers credit it.
- The feelings get acknowledged; you process them.

## Communicating different audiences

### To an attorney

Facts first. Feelings as context.

Attorneys need the facts to evaluate the claim.
They'll ask about how it affected you (relevant for
damages); they don't need extensive emotional
context to evaluate the legal merits.

A tight fact record + a brief context paragraph
(why this matters to you) is what they want.

### To a mediator

Both, balanced. Mediators work with both the legal
substance and the emotional component; the resolution
often involves acknowledgement of feelings, not just
exchange of value.

### To the other party

Tactical. Often just facts and asks; sometimes
acknowledgement of impact. Usually skip the deep
emotional content — they may not be receptive, and
emotional sharing in conflict can be exploited.

### To friends / partners / therapist

Both, freely. This is where you process. The
constraint is on third parties handling the formal
matter, not on you.

## Common mixing patterns to avoid

### "I feel like..."

"I feel like they did this on purpose to humiliate me."

The "I feel like" is grammatical; the substance is an
inference about motive. Make it explicit:

- Fact: "They didn't notify me of the reschedule."
- Inference: "I infer this might have been intentional."
- Feeling: "I felt humiliated."

Three different things. Sometimes all three are useful;
they should be labelled differently.

### "Anyone would have..."

"Anyone would have been upset by this."

Sometimes true; not a fact about what happened. It's a
defence of your reaction. The reaction is yours; you
don't need to justify it; in a fact record, it's noise.

### "Obviously..."

"They obviously did X because Y."

The "obviously" hides an inference. Make explicit:
"Based on Z, I think they did X." Now the listener
knows what's evidence vs. interpretation.

### Embedded judgement words

"They negligently failed to notify me."

"Negligently" is a legal conclusion. The fact is "they
didn't notify me". Whether that constitutes legal
negligence is a different question.

Strip judgement words from facts; use them in feelings/
inference if at all.

### "They knew..."

"They knew the meeting was scheduled."

Unless you have direct evidence (an email confirming),
this is inference. Reframe:

- Fact: "I sent calendar invite at [time]; received
  acknowledgement at [time]."
- Inference: "They knew."

The fact + the inference is stronger than the inference
alone.

## Time and the record

Memory degrades. Records contemporaneous with events
are vastly stronger than reconstructions:

- **Same day:** ideal.
- **Within a week:** good.
- **Weeks later:** acceptable for major events;
  weak for details.
- **Months later:** memory has reorganised; details
  are unreliable.

If you suspect a dispute is brewing, **start the
record now**. Even before formal action, the
documentation discipline pays off.

Re-reading old records reveals patterns and
substantiates timelines that "I remember it well"
can't.

## When records can hurt

Some considerations:

### Discovery

In litigation, your records can be requested by the
other side. This includes:

- Notes you wrote thinking they were private.
- Diary / journal entries.
- Emails / texts you sent (including to friends).
- Anything else "in your possession or control".

Implication: write your fact record assuming the
other side will read it.

For feelings: acknowledge they may also be
discoverable. Some users keep emotional processing
verbal (with friend, therapist) rather than written.

### Privilege

Communications with your attorney are privileged
(confidential, not discoverable, in most
jurisdictions). Notes prepared for your attorney
("attorney work product") have additional
protection.

If you have an attorney, ask whether your notes
should be marked / structured for privilege.

For laypeople without attorney: be aware your
records aren't privileged.

### Recording laws

Audio / video recording without all-party consent is
illegal in many jurisdictions. Don't record
conversations as evidence without checking the law.

### "Don't write that down" temptations

Sometimes you'll be tempted to record something
risky. Pause; think about discovery; consult attorney
if substantial.

## Anti-patterns

### Pure-feelings record

"What happened" is "they were terrible". Without
specifics, the record can't substantiate anything.

### Pure-facts coldness

"It was an interaction at 3pm. Several words were
exchanged." Doesn't help your decision-making and
doesn't acknowledge the impact on you.

### Mixing-as-default

Most people default to the mixed approach. Force the
separation; the records produce different artefacts
once you do.

### Reconstruction-as-fact

Writing today's recollection of three months ago as
if it were a contemporaneous record. Note when you're
reconstructing; honest about the timing.

### Privacy assumption

Treating notes as private. Discovery / disclosure
laws can require their production.

### Inference inflation

Adding interpretive language to make the facts seem
more damning. Often backfires when scrutinised.

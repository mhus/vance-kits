# Red-Flag Clauses — patterns that warrant scrutiny

Specific clause patterns that warrant careful attention
in any contract. Their presence isn't automatically
bad — many are routine — but they have outsized impact,
so understanding them matters.

**This is reading help. Whether a clause is enforceable,
fair, or worth negotiating in your specific situation
is attorney territory.**

## Indemnification

You agree to defend and pay for some category of trouble
if it happens.

Variations:

### One-way (you indemnify them)

You take responsibility; they don't. Common in
employment, services-where-you-are-the-vendor.

Watch for:

- Broad triggers ("any claim arising from...").
- Lack of cap on amount.
- No carve-out for their own fault.

### Mutual

Each party indemnifies the other for their own
fault. More common in arms-length deals.

### Carve-outs

Look for what's excluded — often "to the extent caused
by the indemnified party's gross negligence /
intentional misconduct".

### Why it matters

An indemnification can transfer huge financial risk.
A small contract can have unlimited downside via
indemnification.

For substantial indemnifications, attorney review is
warranted.

## Non-compete

Restriction on where you can work after this contract
ends.

Watch for:

- **Duration** (months / years).
- **Geographic scope** (city / state / country / world).
- **Industry / role scope** (any competitor / specific
  similar roles).
- **Compensation** (some jurisdictions require pay
  during the non-compete period).

### Enforceability

Varies enormously by jurisdiction:

- Some US states (California, North Dakota,
  Oklahoma): largely unenforceable.
- Other US states: enforceable if "reasonable".
- EU countries: enforceable with conditions.
- "Reasonable" is interpreted per jurisdiction.

Even where unenforceable, non-competes are often
included to deter. The chilling effect is real even
if litigation would lose.

For employment with non-competes: attorney review
warranted, especially for senior roles.

## Non-solicit

Restriction on poaching customers / employees /
contacts after the contract ends.

Generally more enforceable than non-competes.

Watch for:

- Duration.
- Definition of who counts (current customers vs.
  prospects? known to you vs. anyone?).
- "Solicitation" definition (active reaching out vs.
  receiving inbound?).

## Mandatory arbitration

Disputes go to private arbitrator instead of court.

Why drafters like it:

- Confidential.
- Often faster than court.
- Limited appeal rights (a feature for the side
  defending).
- Class-action-resistant.

Why it can hurt the other party:

- No public record / precedent.
- Often arbitration costs (sometimes shared,
  sometimes one-sided).
- Limited discovery (less ability to find
  documents).
- Arbitrators sometimes "industry friendly" because
  they're paid by repeat-player industry.
- Class waivers can mean small individual claims have
  no practical recourse.

Common in employment, consumer agreements,
financial services.

For substantial arbitration clauses, attorney review
warranted. In some cases, refusing arbitration
clauses is possible but uncommon in
take-it-or-leave-it contracts.

## Class action waiver

You agree not to participate in class actions.
Combined with arbitration, this is a strong tool
for limiting collective recourse.

Often unappealable in consumer / employment contexts
once signed. Some jurisdictions limit
enforceability; rules vary.

## Forum selection / choice of law

The contract specifies which jurisdiction's courts
hear disputes ("forum"); which jurisdiction's law
applies ("choice of law").

Often these are remote / favourable to the drafter:

- A consumer in California signing a contract that
  specifies New York courts and Delaware law.
- An employee in Texas with employment in Texas, but
  contract specifies California law and arbitration
  in San Francisco.

Effect: practical ability to enforce / dispute is
constrained.

Watch for:

- Forum that's far from you.
- Law that you can't easily understand.
- Combined with mandatory arbitration in that forum.

## Automatic renewal

The contract renews unless cancelled. Often common in
services, subscriptions, leases.

Watch for:

- **Notice period to cancel.** Some require 60-90
  days notice; missing the window means another year.
- **Cancellation method.** Some require certified
  mail; some require specific written form.
- **Renewal price changes.** Often the renewal is at
  a higher rate.

Consumer protection in some jurisdictions requires
clear disclosure / opt-in for auto-renewal. Standards
vary.

## Liquidated damages

Pre-set amount payable if a specific breach occurs.

Watch for:

- Disproportionate to actual likely harm
  (potentially unenforceable as penalty).
- Amount that would be ruinous (early lease
  termination = full remaining rent due).
- One-sided (you pay liquidated damages; they don't).

Some jurisdictions limit liquidated damages to
"reasonable estimate of harm" — but enforcement
varies, and the pressure of the clause is real even
if ultimately unenforceable.

## Limitation of liability / damages cap

Caps on damages the other party owes you.

Watch for:

- Very low caps relative to potential harm ("liability
  limited to fees paid in last 12 months").
- Exclusion of consequential / lost-profit damages.
- Mutual or one-sided?

In commercial contracts, liability caps are common and
often reasonable. In consumer contracts, very low caps
can be problematic.

## Broad release

You release ("forgive / waive") claims you have or
might have.

Watch for:

- "...known and unknown" claims.
- "...arising at any time".
- Releases of claims that haven't yet emerged.

A release of unknown claims gives up rights you don't
yet know you have. Some jurisdictions limit
enforceability of releasing unknown claims.

Common in employment severance, settlement
agreements. Always attorney territory.

## IP assignment (especially employment)

Employee assigns intellectual property to employer.

Watch for:

- **Scope** — work done on the job vs. side projects?
  In some jurisdictions, broad IP-assignment clauses
  even capture personal projects done off-hours.
- **Background IP** — what you brought to the job?
  Some contracts assign your pre-existing IP unless
  you list exceptions.
- **Employer-funded vs. unrelated** distinction.

For engineering, design, writing roles: pay close
attention. List your pre-existing IP in the
exceptions section if there's one.

## Force majeure

What counts as "we couldn't perform because of
circumstances beyond our control".

Watch for:

- Inclusions that protect their non-performance but
  don't excuse yours.
- "Acts of God" plus everything-else lists.
- Pandemic-specific clauses (post-COVID common).

## Severability

If one clause is found unenforceable, the rest of the
contract survives.

Almost universal. Not usually problematic. But: a
contract with severability + a clearly unenforceable
clause may suggest the drafter knows the clause is
problematic and is trying to keep it anyway.

## Survival

Some obligations continue after the contract ends.

Common surviving clauses:

- Confidentiality.
- IP assignments.
- Indemnifications.
- Non-competes (during the post-term period).
- Limitations of liability.

Watch which obligations bind you forever.

## Modification clauses

How can the contract be changed?

- "Only in writing signed by both parties" — strict.
- "By the company at its discretion with notice" —
  one-sided modification rights, common in ToS but
  also sometimes in services.

A unilateral modification clause makes the
"agreement" partially fictional — they can change it
later.

## Fee shifting / attorneys' fees

Who pays attorney fees in a dispute?

- **American rule (default in US):** each party pays
  their own.
- **English rule (UK and many others):** loser pays.
- **One-way fee shifting:** only one party owes the
  other if loser. Common in adhesion contracts to
  discourage suits.

If the contract has attorneys-fees clauses, understand
which way they cut.

## Most-favoured-nation / most-favoured-customer

You agree to give the other party your best terms or
match terms you give to anyone else.

Watch for: business consequences when you're trying
to negotiate with future customers.

## Termination for convenience asymmetry

One side can terminate for any reason; the other
can't.

Watch for: you're locked in; they can leave anytime.

Common in services contracts (vendor often can't
terminate; client can).

## Most-egregious patterns

Combinations that warrant heightened scrutiny:

- Mandatory arbitration + class-action waiver +
  remote forum + their choice of law.
- Broad indemnification with no cap + IP assignment
  including background IP + non-compete + non-solicit.
- Auto-renewal + unilateral modification + remote
  forum.

These show up especially in adhesion contracts (ToS,
employment, take-it-or-leave-it). Sometimes
unavoidable; always worth knowing about.

## How to flag a red flag

When you find one:

- Note the specific clause and section.
- Note your specific concern in plain English.
- Don't try to interpret enforceability or strategy —
  surface for attorney consult.

Output: a list of "items for attorney review" that
you can hand off intact.

## When red flags should trigger attorney review

- **One major red flag** (e.g. broad indemnity, non-
  compete, mandatory arbitration with high stakes):
  consider review depending on context.
- **Multiple red flags in combination:** review
  warranted.
- **Contract with substantial value / commitment:**
  always review.
- **Employment with non-compete:** review.
- **Settlement / release:** always review.
- **You don't understand the clause:** review.

## Anti-patterns

### Treating "standard" as safe

"It's a standard non-compete" — standard for what
purpose? In your jurisdiction? Standard doesn't mean
fair to you.

### Ignoring asymmetry

"They have the same clause about themselves" — but
do they? Many contracts that look mutual have subtle
asymmetries. Read both directions.

### Pretending you understand

You read the clause; you sort-of understand; you
sign. For substantial contracts, "sort of" is
inadequate.

### Single-clause focus

You scrutinise one red flag and miss another.
Walk the whole contract; flag everything; review the
list together.

### Trusting the explanation

The other party explains the clause as benign. Their
explanation may be honest; may be incomplete; may be
self-interested. Read what's written, not what's
explained.

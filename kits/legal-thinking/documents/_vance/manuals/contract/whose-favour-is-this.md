# Whose Favour Is This — recognising asymmetry

Most contracts are drafted by one side. The drafter
optimises for their interests; the other party gets
the version designed by the drafter's lawyers.

This isn't necessarily nefarious — it's how contract
drafting works. The drafter saves time / money / risk
through careful drafting; the counterparty signs.

But: recognising the asymmetry is part of reading.
Knowing whose favour the contract is drafted in tells
you what to scrutinise and where to push back.

## Who drafted it

In most situations, you can guess:

- **Lease:** landlord (or landlord's attorney /
  property management).
- **Employment:** employer (or HR / employer's
  counsel).
- **Software ToS:** the software company.
- **Bank loan:** the bank.
- **B2B services agreement:** depends — vendor often
  drafts; sometimes client wins this.
- **NDA:** often the party sharing more confidential
  info.
- **Settlement:** the party paying (usually the
  defendant; but varies).

The drafter's identity is sometimes literally
mentioned ("agreement provided by [Company]") but
often clear from the structure and the favourable
clauses.

## What favours the drafter

When you see these, they often signal drafter-favour:

### Liability caps that benefit them

"In no event shall [Drafter] be liable for...". Common
in services and ToS. Caps the drafter's exposure.

### Exclusions that benefit them

"[Counterparty] expressly waives any claim for
consequential damages". You can't sue for the kind
of damages you're most likely to suffer.

### Termination asymmetry

Drafter can terminate for convenience; counterparty
can only terminate for material breach with cure
period. Drafter has flexibility; counterparty is
locked in.

### Indemnification asymmetry

Counterparty indemnifies drafter; drafter doesn't
indemnify counterparty (or indemnifies only narrowly).

### Forum / law in drafter's home

Drafter is in California; contract specifies
California law and California forum. Counterparty
in another state has to litigate in California.

### Modification rights

Drafter can modify the contract unilaterally;
counterparty cannot. Common in ToS; less so in
B2B contracts.

### Confidentiality asymmetry

Counterparty's information protected at lower
standard than drafter's; or drafter's info protected
forever while counterparty's isn't.

### Information rights asymmetry

Drafter has audit rights; counterparty doesn't (or
counterparty has limited).

### Renewal mechanics

Auto-renewal benefits the drafter (predictable
revenue). The counterparty has to act (cancel) to
prevent.

### Definitions favour drafter

The defined terms can be subtly tilted. "Service
levels" defined to exclude scheduled downtime, force
majeure, and "issues caused by Customer". The
counterparty has weaker SLAs than the prose suggests.

## What favours the counterparty

Less common but worth recognising:

- Mutual indemnification / liability caps.
- Either-side termination for convenience.
- Mutual confidentiality.
- Choice of forum / law that's neutral or favours the
  weaker party.
- Service levels with teeth (credits / penalties).
- Modification only by mutual written consent.
- Cure periods before termination for breach.

If you're seeing these, you have a contract
negotiated by competent counsel for both sides — or a
drafter who's particularly fair.

## Adhesion contracts

A contract offered take-it-or-leave-it. The counterparty
can't negotiate; the only choice is sign or not.

Examples:

- Software ToS.
- Apartment lease (often).
- Employment for low-power roles.
- Insurance policies.
- Bank account terms.
- Most consumer agreements.

For adhesion contracts:

- The asymmetry is by design and pervasive.
- Negotiation is rarely possible at the individual
  level.
- Some jurisdictions limit enforceability of
  particularly one-sided clauses in adhesion contracts
  (especially consumer protection law).

If you can't negotiate, the choice is sign and accept
the risk, or don't sign and find an alternative.
Knowing what you're accepting is still valuable.

## Negotiable contracts

When you have leverage:

- Senior employment.
- Significant B2B vendor agreement.
- Custom services contract.
- Large lease (commercial; sometimes residential).
- Investments / financing.

For negotiable contracts:

- Identify the asymmetric clauses.
- Decide which to push back on.
- Often a few specific clauses can be made mutual or
  capped.

Common successful pushbacks:

- **Mutual indemnification** (instead of one-way).
- **Liability cap** (raised, or excluding specific
  categories like indemnification obligations).
- **Termination for convenience** added (reciprocal).
- **Forum / law** moved to neutral.
- **Cure period** before termination for breach.
- **Cap on consequential damages exclusion.**

What's negotiable depends on the relationship and
your leverage. Asking generally costs nothing;
sometimes wins something.

## How to identify asymmetry

For each substantive clause:

1. **What does this clause do?** (state in your own
   words).
2. **For whom?** (drafter / counterparty / mutual).
3. **Is there a corresponding clause for the other
   side?** (search for the mirror clause).
4. **If not, is the asymmetry justified?** Sometimes
   one-sided protection is appropriate (you're paying
   them; they need protection). Sometimes it's just
   one-sided.

The list of asymmetries is the negotiation list.

## Specific patterns

### "Customer" / "Vendor" / "Service Provider"

These role-defined terms often hide asymmetry. Read
substituting "you" and "them":

- "Customer shall pay all costs" — you pay everything.
- "Service Provider may modify the Service" — they
  can change what you bought.

Translating to "you" and "them" surfaces the
asymmetry.

### Term negotiation

The starting "we'll do business for X years" is
negotiable. So is the auto-renewal mechanic. So is
the notice period.

### Acceptance criteria

For services / deliverables, "what counts as
acceptable" can be subtle. Drafter may define
acceptance as "use without complaint within 5 days" —
silence equals acceptance. Pay attention.

### Rate increases

Long-term contracts often have annual rate increase
clauses. The mechanism (CPI / fixed-percentage /
"as determined by [Drafter]") matters. "As determined
by [Drafter]" is unilateral pricing power.

## When the asymmetry is appropriate

Not all asymmetry is bad. Some flows from the
relationship structure:

- **Employer protections in employment:** non-
  disclosure of trade secrets, IP assignment for
  work-for-hire — appropriate within reason.
- **Vendor protections in services:** liability caps
  proportionate to fees received, exclusions of
  consequential damages — common and often
  reasonable.
- **Lender protections in loans:** acceleration
  clauses, default rate increases, security interest
  — fundamental to lending.

The asymmetry is unfair when it's disproportionate to
the relationship's risk allocation. Unbounded
indemnification by an employee for any company harm
isn't proportionate; bounded indemnification for
specific employee misconduct may be.

## Anti-patterns

### Believing "It's standard"

"It's our standard contract" — true; doesn't make it
fair to you. Standard is a starting point, not an
assertion of fairness.

### Refusing to negotiate

You assume you can't push back; you don't try. For
non-adhesion contracts, the asking often gets
something. The drafter's first version is rarely
the most favourable they can offer.

### Pushing back on everything

The opposite. You demand changes to every clause.
You appear unreasonable; the drafter walks. Pick
your battles — the substantively important ones.

### Pseudo-mutual clauses

"Each party shall indemnify the other for..." — sounds
mutual; sometimes the triggering events are
asymmetric. Read what each indemnification
actually covers; the form may be mutual but the
substance one-sided.

### Trusting the explanation

The drafter explains away your concern. Their
explanation may be technically true but miss the
practical effect. Trust the text more than the
explanation.

### Negotiating without attorney input

For substantial contracts, layperson negotiation can
miss issues. Attorney review before pushing back
catches what you'd miss.

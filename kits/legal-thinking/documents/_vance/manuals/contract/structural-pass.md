# Structural Pass — first read for shape

Before scrutinising clauses, get the contract's overall
shape. A 30-page contract with 14 sections is
intimidating; a 30-page contract with "5 sections that
matter and 9 boilerplate" is tractable.

This manual is the structural orientation pass. It
takes 10-30 minutes; you do it before anything else.

## What you're extracting

By the end of the structural pass, you should be able
to answer in one sentence each:

1. **Who are the parties?** Names, roles (e.g. "Tenant"
   and "Landlord"; "Employee" and "Employer";
   "Provider" and "Client").
2. **What's the term?** When does it start, when does
   it end, can it be renewed?
3. **What's being exchanged?** Money for services,
   space for rent, work for compensation.
4. **What are the key obligations on each side?**
5. **How does it terminate?** End-of-term, breach,
   notice, mutual consent.
6. **What's the consequence of breach?** Damages,
   liquidated damages, injunction, fees.
7. **What jurisdiction / law / forum?**

If you can't answer these after a structural pass, the
contract is poorly drafted, missing pieces, or both.
That's information.

## Sections common to most contracts

Different contract types share structural elements:

### Recitals / preamble

The "WHEREAS" section. Background, parties' intent.
Usually not legally binding (depends on jurisdiction)
but contextualises.

Read briefly. Useful for understanding why; doesn't
change what.

### Definitions

Defined terms used throughout. Capital-letter words
("the Service", "the Property", "the Effective Date").

Read carefully — definitions are where ambiguity gets
hidden. A contract might define "Confidential
Information" broadly enough that anything you ever
discuss is "Confidential" — affecting other clauses.

### Term and termination

Start date, end date, renewal mechanics, termination
rights for each side.

- **Term length:** how long is the commitment?
- **Auto-renewal:** does it renew unless cancelled?
  How much notice required?
- **Termination for cause:** what counts as cause?
- **Termination for convenience:** can either party
  end without reason?
- **Notice period:** how much notice required?

This is one of the most important sections. Read
carefully.

### Payment / consideration

What's being exchanged.

- Amounts, due dates, payment method.
- Late fees, interest on overdue amounts.
- Increase mechanics (annual increases, market-rate
  resets).
- Refund / proration on early termination.
- Taxes, fees, who pays what.

### Performance obligations

What each party is supposed to do.

- Service levels, deliverables, deadlines.
- Specifications and quality standards.
- Cure periods (time to fix breaches before
  termination).

### Representations and warranties

Statements of fact each party makes. Usually about
authority to enter the contract, ownership of what's
being transferred, accuracy of representations.

Sometimes "no warranties" disclaim what looks like
implied warranties (especially in software / service
contracts).

### Indemnification

Who pays for what kinds of trouble. One party agrees
to defend / hold harmless the other against specified
claims.

This is a high-attention area. See `red-flag-clauses`.

### Limitation of liability

Caps on damages. Often: "in no event shall liability
exceed [X]". And: exclusions of consequential / lost
profits / punitive damages.

Common in commercial contracts. Sometimes very
favourable to the drafter; sometimes mutual.

### Confidentiality

What information is confidential, how it's protected,
how long the obligation lasts.

Watch for:

- Asymmetric confidentiality (you protect theirs;
  they don't protect yours).
- Very broad definitions of "confidential".
- Long tail (confidentiality outlives the contract by
  many years).

### Intellectual property

Who owns what. Especially in employment, services,
collaborations.

Clauses to watch:

- Work-for-hire (employer owns everything).
- Assignment of pre-existing IP.
- Background IP carve-outs.
- License-back provisions.

### Dispute resolution

How disputes are handled.

- Mandatory arbitration (vs. court).
- Choice of forum (which jurisdiction's courts).
- Choice of law (which jurisdiction's law applies).
- Class action waivers.
- Mediation requirements before litigation.

Often dramatic effect on practical recourse. See
`red-flag-clauses` for arbitration specifics.

### Boilerplate / general provisions

Standard clauses near the end:

- Severability (if one clause fails, rest survives).
- Entire agreement (this contract is the whole deal).
- Modifications (must be in writing).
- Notices (how do parties communicate formally).
- Force majeure (what counts as an excuse for
  non-performance).
- Survival (which obligations continue after
  termination).
- Counterparts / signature.

Often skipped; sometimes contains traps. Read at least
once.

### Schedules / exhibits / addenda

Attached documents. Service descriptions, rate
schedules, statement-of-work, lists of products.

These are part of the contract. Read with the same
attention as the main body.

## How to do the pass

### 1. Read the recitals

5 minutes. Get the big picture.

### 2. List the sections

Note section headings. Which sections are short
(usually less risk) and which are long (more
content)?

### 3. Read term and termination first

Single most important section after defining the
parties. How long am I committed? How do I get out?

### 4. Read payment / consideration

What's the financial exposure?

### 5. Skim the rest

Note section names; spot the big ones; flag what
warrants Pass 2 attention (`red-flag-clauses`).

### 6. List your concerns

After the pass, you should have a short list of
"things to dig into". 3-5 items typical.

## Tools

- **A printed copy with margin notes.** Hard to beat
  for first pass. Highlight + write questions.
- **Annotated PDF.** Same idea, digital. Comment
  threads as questions.
- **A note doc** with section-by-section summary in
  your own words. Forces processing.

## Quick reference — typical contract types

### Lease (rental)

Key sections to read carefully:

- Term, automatic renewal.
- Rent, increase mechanics.
- Security deposit handling.
- Maintenance / repair responsibility.
- Subletting / assignment.
- Pet / smoking / occupancy rules.
- Right to enter (landlord access).
- Termination notice required.
- Local tenant rights (varies enormously by
  jurisdiction — research separately).

### Employment

Key sections to read carefully:

- Salary / compensation structure (base, bonus,
  equity).
- At-will or term?
- Termination and severance.
- Non-compete (often unenforceable; often included
  anyway).
- Non-solicit (usually more enforceable).
- Confidentiality.
- IP assignment (especially for engineers,
  designers, writers).
- Arbitration / class waiver.
- Choice of law.

For senior roles or tech: substantial attorney review
warranted.

### Services agreement

Key sections to read carefully:

- Scope (what's included; what's not).
- Deliverables / deadlines / acceptance.
- Payment terms.
- Termination (often for-convenience favours one side).
- Liability caps.
- IP ownership.
- Indemnification (asymmetry common).
- Warranties / disclaimers.

### NDA / confidentiality

Key sections:

- Definition of "confidential".
- Permitted uses.
- Term of obligation (often 2-5 years; sometimes
  unlimited).
- Mutual or one-way?
- Carve-outs (publicly known; independently
  developed; required by law).

### Loan / financing

Key sections:

- Principal, interest rate, term.
- Payment schedule.
- Prepayment rights / penalties.
- Collateral / security interest.
- Default events.
- Acceleration.
- Personal guarantees.

For substantial loans: attorney review warranted.

### Settlement agreement

Almost always: attorney review warranted. Settlement
agreements are dispositive of legal claims; mistakes
can foreclose options forever.

### Software / SaaS terms

Key sections:

- Subscription term and auto-renewal.
- Data usage and ownership.
- Termination and data return.
- Liability limits (often very low).
- IP ownership of customisations.
- Service-level commitments.
- Indemnification and exceptions.

## What you can do post-pass

After the structural pass, you have:

- A summary you can show others.
- A list of specific concerns for deeper review or
  attorney consult.
- Awareness of what's clear vs. what's ambiguous.

What you don't have: a verdict on whether to sign.
That's a separate decision involving:

- Other terms (job, deal, opportunity).
- Negotiation possibility.
- Attorney input on substantial concerns.
- Your own risk tolerance.

## Anti-patterns

### Skip-and-sign

Signing without any read. Common with employment, ToS,
adhesion contracts. Sometimes the only practical
option (you can't negotiate ToS); for substantial
agreements, signing-without-reading is a real risk.

### Skim-without-action

You read it; you don't note anything; you don't act.
The reading produced no artefact. Force a list of
concerns or summary.

### Reading the boilerplate first

Boilerplate is "standard" and rarely the deal-breaker.
Read parties / term / payment / performance first;
boilerplate later.

### Treating this manual as advice

This is structural reading help. The advice level —
"should you sign this" — is attorney territory. Don't
conflate.

### Pretending complexity is malice

Most contracts are complex because the situation is
complex. Some clauses are written by lawyers for
lawyers. The complexity isn't always a trick.

### Trusting the "standard form"

"It's our standard contract" doesn't make it fair to
you. Standard forms are typically drafted in favour
of whoever uses them most. Read the same way.

# Jurisdiction and Scope — why "where" always matters

Almost every legal answer depends on where you are.
The same fact pattern produces different legal
results in different jurisdictions. Treating "the
law" as universal is one of the most common
layperson errors.

## Why jurisdiction matters

Different jurisdictions have:

- **Different statutes.** Same area of law; different
  rules.
- **Different case law.** Courts interpret the same
  statute differently.
- **Different procedural rules.** How you bring a
  case; what notice is required; what discovery is
  available.
- **Different remedies.** What you can win and how.
- **Different time limits.** Statute of limitations
  varies.
- **Different professional rules.** Who's licensed to
  do what.

A "yes" in jurisdiction A may be a "no" in
jurisdiction B for the same question.

## Layers of jurisdiction

For a single matter, multiple jurisdictions can apply:

### Country / federal

National laws (e.g. US federal copyright; EU GDPR;
country-level criminal codes).

### State / province

Major variation. Most consumer protection, employment
law (for non-federal aspects), real estate, family
law, contract enforcement.

### Municipality / local

Often: zoning, local taxes, some employment
ordinances, rental regulations.

A single matter can be governed by multiple layers.
Example: an employment dispute in San Francisco can
involve federal anti-discrimination law (US national)
+ California Labor Code (state) + San Francisco
ordinances (city) — all simultaneously.

## "Choice of law"

Some contracts specify which jurisdiction's law
applies. This isn't always honoured (some
jurisdictions limit enforceability of foreign-law
choice for consumers / employees / weaker parties).

Example: a contract specifies Delaware law for
parties not in Delaware. Delaware law applies if the
choice is enforceable; some jurisdictions might
impose their own law on certain matters anyway.

For substantial matters, jurisdiction questions are
attorney territory.

## Examples of jurisdiction-dependent answers

### Non-compete enforceability

- **California (US):** generally unenforceable.
- **Florida (US):** enforceable with conditions.
- **Texas (US):** moderate enforcement.
- **Germany:** enforceable with strict conditions
  (compensation required during the period).
- **UK:** enforceable if "reasonable".

The same non-compete clause produces very different
legal outcomes depending on where you are.

### Tenant rights

- **New York:** strong rent control / stabilisation in
  some areas; strong tenant protections.
- **Texas:** weaker tenant protections; landlord-
  friendlier laws.
- **Most of Germany:** very strong tenant protections;
  hard to evict; rent caps in many areas.
- **Australia:** varies by state; generally moderate.

"Can my landlord evict me for X?" answer depends
heavily on where.

### Privacy

- **EU:** GDPR — strong consumer rights.
- **California:** CCPA / CPRA — strong (in US
  context).
- **US (most other states):** weaker; sectoral
  protections only.
- **Various countries:** range from strong (Germany,
  Canada) to weak.

"Can the company use my data for X?" depends.

### Marriage / divorce

Family law is one of the most jurisdictionally-
variable areas. What constitutes valid marriage,
grounds for divorce, custody standards, property
division rules — all vary.

For any active family matter: attorney in your
specific jurisdiction.

### Criminal liability

- Some acts are crimes in some jurisdictions, not
  others.
- Same act can produce vastly different sentencing.
- Defences available vary.

For any criminal matter: attorney immediately.

### Healthcare / medical

Medical privacy, consent rules, prescription rules,
end-of-life rules — all vary.

### Employment

At-will employment in most US states; protections in
EU; wide variation in dismissal rules.

Discrimination protections, minimum wage, overtime
rules, paid leave — all vary.

### Tax

- Federal vs. state vs. local.
- Different countries have entirely different
  systems.
- Treaty provisions matter for cross-border.

For tax: tax professional, jurisdiction-aware.

## "I read on the internet that..."

The web's biggest legal-mistake source.

A blog post / Reddit thread / AI summary describes
"the law" without specifying jurisdiction. The reader
assumes it applies to them. It often doesn't.

Internet legal information:

- Often US-specific without saying.
- Often state-specific within US without specifying
  which state.
- Often outdated.
- Sometimes wrong even for the implied jurisdiction.

If you're researching legal information online:

- **Check the jurisdiction.** If the source doesn't
  specify, treat as unreliable.
- **Check the date.** Laws change.
- **Check the source's authority.** Government /
  bar association / law school / established legal
  publisher = better. Random blogger = worse.
- **Don't act on web legal information for
  substantive matters** without attorney
  confirmation in your jurisdiction.

## Cross-border matters

Particularly fraught:

- **International contracts.** Which jurisdiction
  governs?
- **International data flows.** GDPR + local laws.
- **International employment.** Where you work; where
  the employer is.
- **Estates with international elements.** Beneficiaries
  in multiple countries.
- **Online services across borders.** Tax, IP, ToS
  enforceability.

For any cross-border matter: attorney experienced in
the specific cross-border combination. Domestic
attorneys often don't know foreign law (and shouldn't
opine on it).

## Specific jurisdictional traps

### "It's my apartment / car / etc."

Sometimes legal status differs from common-sense
ownership. Real property laws (especially marital
property in family law) can produce surprises.

### "We agreed verbally"

Some agreements require writing in some
jurisdictions (statute of frauds — varies). Real
estate, multi-year contracts, certain other
categories often need writing.

### "It's posted publicly"

Posting something doesn't always make it usable.
Copyright, privacy, defamation rules apply.

### "I'm a corporation, so I'm not personally
liable"

Corporate veil can be pierced; personal guarantees
common; certain duties extend personally.

For any business / corporate questions: attorney
specific to business law in your jurisdiction.

### "Common law"

Common-law jurisdictions (US, UK, Canada, Australia,
others) have different rules from civil-law
jurisdictions (most of EU, Latin America, Asia).

The legal frameworks differ fundamentally; principles
that apply in one don't always translate.

## How to use jurisdiction information

Before answering any legal-flavoured question:

1. **Identify the relevant jurisdiction(s).** Where
   does the matter occur? Where are the parties?
   Where would litigation occur?
2. **Acknowledge the jurisdictional dependency.** The
   answer depends on jurisdiction.
3. **Avoid generic answers.** "X is illegal" without
   specifying where is misleading.
4. **Direct to local attorney for substantive
   answers.**

In the layperson reading mode this skill operates in:

- Acknowledge jurisdictional dependency.
- Don't claim a specific answer for a specific
  jurisdiction unless it's well-established and
  flagged.
- Default to "this varies; consult attorney for
  specifics in your jurisdiction".

## When jurisdictional knowledge is part of layperson
reading

Some questions can be addressed at general level:

- **What's a non-compete?** General concept,
  jurisdiction-independent.
- **What does indemnification typically mean?**
  General.
- **What's the typical structure of [common
  contract]?** General.

These are reading help; layperson-appropriate.

But:

- **Is my non-compete enforceable?** Jurisdictional;
  attorney territory.
- **Am I bound by this indemnification clause?**
  Specific; attorney territory.

Recognise the line between general (lay-handleable)
and applied (legal).

## Anti-patterns

### "The law says..."

Without specifying jurisdiction, this claim is
incomplete at best, wrong at worst.

### Cross-jurisdictional generalisation

"In the US, X" — but rules vary by state. "In Europe,
Y" — varies by country.

### Trusting source without jurisdictional check

Reading something authoritative-looking without
verifying it applies to you.

### Treating online legal info as comprehensive

The internet has substantial legal information; it
rarely substitutes for jurisdiction-specific
attorney consultation for substantive matters.

### Assuming continuity

"It used to be X" — laws change. Recent statute /
regulation / case decision can shift the answer.

### Treating one attorney's opinion as universal

Even within a jurisdiction, attorneys have different
views. For substantial matters, second opinions are
sometimes worth getting.

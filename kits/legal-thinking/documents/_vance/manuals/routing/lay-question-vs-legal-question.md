# Lay Question vs. Legal Question — distinguishing the two

Some questions look legal and aren't. Some look simple
and are deeply legal. The distinction matters for
routing.

## Lay question

A question you can reasonably think through with
information available to a layperson. Examples:

- "What does this clause typically mean in plain
  English?" (reading help)
- "What kind of contract is this?" (categorisation)
- "What questions should I ask my attorney?"
  (preparation)
- "Is this a topic where attorney input is usually
  warranted?" (routing)

These don't require an attorney to answer well. They
require general knowledge + careful reading.

## Legal question

A question whose answer requires:

- **Application of law to specific facts.**
- **Knowledge of jurisdiction-specific rules.**
- **Assessment of how a court / regulator would
  interpret.**
- **Strategic / tactical legal judgement.**
- **Authority to advise** (which laypeople don't
  have).

Examples:

- "Is this contract enforceable in [jurisdiction]
  given [facts]?"
- "Do I have a case for [type of claim]?"
- "How should I respond to this complaint /
  subpoena?"
- "What's my exposure if I do X?"
- "Can I be sued for Y?"

These require attorney input. Layperson conclusions
on these are unreliable.

## The key distinction

**Lay questions are about understanding.**
**Legal questions are about action.**

If the question's answer changes what the user does in
a way that has legal consequences, it's a legal
question. If the answer increases understanding without
prescribing action, it's a lay question.

Examples in tension:

- "What does 'consideration' mean in contract law?" —
  lay (definition).
- "Was there sufficient consideration in this
  contract for it to be binding?" — legal (applying
  to facts).

- "How does mandatory arbitration work?" — lay
  (general understanding).
- "Should I sign this contract with mandatory
  arbitration?" — borderline — leans legal because
  the answer depends on the specific contract,
  jurisdiction, your situation.

## Common questions and their categorisation

### Lease / rental questions

- "What does this lease clause say in plain English?"
  → lay (reading).
- "Is this lease enforceable in my state?" → legal.
- "Can my landlord evict me for X?" → legal.
- "What is reasonable wear-and-tear vs. damage?" →
  borderline; jurisdiction-specific definitions.
- "How do I write a complaint about my landlord?"
  → procedural; some lay-handleable, some not.

### Employment questions

- "What does this non-compete clause say?" → lay
  (reading).
- "Is this non-compete enforceable in my state?" →
  legal.
- "Can I be fired for X?" → legal (depends on
  jurisdiction's at-will rules + protected classes).
- "What does 'at-will employment' mean?" → lay.
- "What do I do if I'm being discriminated against?"
  → legal (process; rights; remedies).
- "How do I document a workplace concern?" → lay
  with attorney involvement increasing with stakes.

### Contract questions

- "What's an indemnification clause?" → lay.
- "Is this indemnification enforceable / fair?" →
  borderline; depends on stakes.
- "Should I sign this contract?" → legal (adviser
  territory).
- "What are common red flags in [type of] contract?"
  → lay.

### Privacy / data

- "What does GDPR require generally?" → lay.
- "Is my company GDPR compliant?" → legal.
- "What rights do I have under [privacy law] in my
  jurisdiction?" → lay (general).
- "Has my [specific privacy right] been violated?"
  → legal.

### Disputes

- "How do small claims court work generally?" → lay.
- "Do I have a case?" → legal.
- "What's my likely best outcome?" → legal.
- "How do I prepare to talk to a lawyer about
  this?" → lay.

### Family law (note: most family law leans
attorney-territory immediately)

- "What does prenup typically contain?" → lay.
- "Is this prenup fair / enforceable?" → legal,
  attorney territory.
- "How does custody / divorce work in my
  jurisdiction?" → very jurisdiction-specific;
  attorney consult warranted for any active
  matter.
- "Is X grounds for divorce?" → legal in many
  jurisdictions.

### Tax

- "What's a tax deduction?" → lay.
- "Can I deduct X?" → tax-professional territory.
- "Is X taxable?" → tax-professional.
- "How does [tax concept] work?" → lay (general
  understanding).
- "What's my tax liability for this transaction?" →
  professional.

### Criminal

- "What does X charge mean?" → lay (definition).
- "What should I do if I'm being investigated?" →
  legal, attorney NOW.
- "Is X a crime?" → legal, jurisdiction-specific.
- "Can I be prosecuted for Y?" → legal, attorney
  NOW.

For any criminal question with personal exposure:
attorney immediately. No exceptions; no
layperson handling.

## How to decide

For any question, ask:

### Does the answer affect what you do legally?

Yes → legal question.
No → lay question.

### Does the answer depend on facts of your situation?

Yes → legal question (or at least requires attorney
to apply general principle to your facts).
No → lay question.

### Does the answer depend on jurisdiction?

Yes → almost always legal (or at least demands
jurisdiction-specific knowledge).
No → could be lay.

### Are stakes substantial?

Yes → attorney even if technically a lay question.
No → lay handling may be enough.

### Is anyone asking you to take action / sign /
respond?

Yes → legal lean; attorney consult before action.
No → can be lay.

## When lay handling is enough

For genuinely lay questions:

- Layperson reading + thinking is appropriate.
- Skills like contract-reading and tos-and-policy-
  parsing apply.
- Output is "I now understand" not "I have a legal
  answer".

When lay handling doesn't suffice:

- Don't pretend it does.
- Direct to attorney.
- Don't extract a specific recommendation when the
  basis is layperson speculation.

## The "I'll just look it up" trap

Modern temptation: search the internet; find an answer;
treat as authoritative.

Problems:

- **Jurisdiction.** Most online answers don't specify
  which jurisdiction; advice for one place is wrong
  for another.
- **Generality vs. specificity.** General principles
  often don't apply cleanly to specific facts.
- **Currency.** Laws change; old answers may be wrong
  now.
- **Authority.** Random forum answers / blogs are not
  authoritative.
- **AI assistants.** Can be confidently wrong; not
  attorney; not authority.

Online research is often a starting point; rarely an
endpoint for substantial legal questions.

## Affordability concerns

A common reason people try to lay-handle legal
questions: cost.

Free / low-cost options exist in most jurisdictions:

### US

- **Public defenders** (criminal cases; means-tested).
- **Legal Aid** (means-tested; civil matters).
- **Law school clinics** (free; narrow scope).
- **Bar association lawyer referral** (low-cost
  consults).
- **Pro bono programmes** (lawyers donating time;
  often through bar association).
- **Free 30-minute consultations** (many attorneys
  offer).

### Other jurisdictions

Similar structures exist:

- **Citizens Advice Bureau** (UK).
- **Public Legal Aid** (varies).
- **Bar association referrals** (most jurisdictions).
- **University law clinics** (most countries with
  law schools).

For substantial legal questions and limited budget:
research these. Don't substitute layperson handling
for attorney consult on real legal questions just
because of cost.

## Bias toward attorney

The default lean: when in doubt, attorney.

Reasons:

- Attorneys are experts; you aren't.
- The cost of a 30-60 minute consult is much smaller
  than the cost of mishandling a substantial legal
  matter.
- Many attorneys offer free initial consults.
- Even if the answer turns out to be "you don't
  need attorney further", you've gotten the routing
  right.

The cost of unnecessary attorney consults: modest.
The cost of unhandled legal matters: often
catastrophic.

Bias toward attorney is rational.

## Anti-patterns

### "It's just a simple question"

Often it isn't. Simple-sounding questions often have
complex jurisdictional + factual dependencies.

### "I read on the internet that..."

Internet legal advice is not authoritative. Use as
starting point for understanding; not as basis for
action.

### "AI told me..."

Same. AI assistants can be confidently wrong on
legal questions.

### "I'll just call an attorney later"

Some legal matters have time limits. Statute of
limitations runs; deadlines pass; rights are forfeited.
"Later" can be too late.

### "I've handled similar before"

Each legal matter has its own facts. "Similar before"
isn't authority for this specific situation.

### "It's not worth attorney costs"

Sometimes true. Often costs are smaller than expected
(free consults; limited engagements; legal aid). The
"too expensive" assumption shouldn't be made without
checking.

### "If I lose, I'll just deal with it"

For some matters: yes. For others: losing is
catastrophic + non-recoverable. Distinguish.

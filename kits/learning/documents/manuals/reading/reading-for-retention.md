# Reading for Retention — active reading techniques

Default reading is passive: eyes track text; mind
processes superficially; nothing sticks. Active reading
deliberately interrupts the passive flow with
engagement that consolidates memory.

This manual is about the specific techniques.

## Pre-reading

Before opening to page 1:

### Read the metadata

- **Title and subtitle.** What does the author claim
  this is about?
- **Table of contents.** What's the structure? How long
  is each section?
- **Preface / introduction.** Author's framing, scope,
  intended audience.
- **Conclusion.** Often summarises the main points. Read
  before reading the body — the body's specifics land
  better when you know where they're going.
- **Index.** What concepts are emphasised (lots of
  index entries)? What's mentioned briefly?

For papers:

- **Title.** Specific claim?
- **Abstract.** Full summary.
- **Conclusion.** Often more honest than abstract about
  limitations.
- **Figures and tables.** Where the actual results
  live; often comprehensible without reading the
  body.

5-10 minutes pre-reading often saves 30 minutes of
unfocused reading.

### Set a reading goal

What do you want to walk away knowing?

- "I want the main argument."
- "I want the specific data on X."
- "I want to learn the technique in chapter 3."
- "I want to understand whether this is worth deeper
  engagement."

The goal drives technique selection.

## During reading

### Marginalia

Notes in the margin (or in the digital equivalent).

Useful marginalia:

- Question marks where you don't follow.
- Summaries of dense paragraphs (one or two words in
  the margin).
- Connections to other things you've read.
- Disagreements / objections.
- "!" or "★" for memorable passages.

The act of writing forces engagement; the marginalia
become navigation aids on re-read.

Don't:

- Highlight without comment. Highlights without notes
  rarely produce action later.
- Write notes that summarise the section verbatim. If
  the marginalia is the same as the text, you're
  just re-writing.

### Periodic recall pauses

Every section / chapter / 20 minutes:

1. Pause.
2. Close the book (or look away).
3. Try to summarise what you just read in 2-3
   sentences.
4. Open and check.

Catches places where you "read" but didn't process.

### Summary after sections

For longer reads, write a 2-4 sentence summary at the
end of each section / chapter, in your own words.

The summary:

- Forces processing.
- Becomes the version-you'll-remember.
- Is reviewable later (you don't have to re-read).

### Question generation

While reading, generate questions:

- "What does this mean?"
- "Why does this matter?"
- "How does this connect to X?"
- "Is this true?"
- "What's an example?"

The questions go in marginalia or notes. Some get
answered later in the text; some don't (and become
research items or question-cards).

Question-generation is the antidote to passive flow —
each question is an engagement event.

### Discussion mode

Read with someone (real or imagined). After each
section, "tell" them what you read.

The act of explanation forces processing (Feynman-
adjacent). With a real partner, you get questions back;
with an imagined partner, you generate questions
yourself.

### Recurring themes

Notice when an idea appears multiple times. Authors
often have core themes that recur in different chapters.
Spotting the recurrence is part of understanding the
book's argument.

Mark themes with consistent notation (e.g. T1, T2 in
margin). Re-look at where each appears.

## After reading

### Spaced summary

Within 24 hours:

1. Close the book.
2. Write a 1-2 paragraph summary of the whole.
3. List three takeaways.
4. List remaining questions.

This is the single highest-leverage post-read activity.
What's not in this summary will fade fast.

### Convert to cards / notes

Specific facts you want to remember → flashcards (see
`spaced-repetition-design`).

Conceptual content → permanent notes in a Zettelkasten /
notes system (see basic / research kit).

Open questions → research tasks.

The conversion is the durability mechanism. Without it,
the material decays at standard forgetting-curve rate.

### Discuss / write about it

Tell someone what you read. Or write a short post / note
about it.

The discussion / writing forces deeper processing than
the reading itself. Material discussed once is much
more retainable than material read once.

### Re-read in 1 week

For important material, a brief re-read in a week (skim
the marginalia + summaries) consolidates.

Don't re-read in entirety; re-engage with your notes.

## Specific patterns

### Reading the dense paragraph

Some paragraphs are intellectual brick walls. Don't
power through; engage:

- Read once for shape.
- Read again, breaking into clauses. What's the subject
  of each? What's the verb?
- Re-state in plain English in the margin.
- If still hard: leave a question; come back later
  with more context.

Sometimes the dense paragraph is unimportant; the
author was being precise about a side point. Sometimes
it's the load-bearing claim of the chapter. The
pre-read tells you which.

### Reading citations / references

For papers especially: who does the author cite? Who
does the author *not* cite? Both informative.

You don't have to read every citation. Note ones that
seem central; chase only when they matter.

### Reading example-heavy material

Books with lots of worked examples: read the example;
attempt to predict the next step before reading. Active
prediction — much higher retention than passive
following.

### Reading conceptual / dense theory

Slower; more pre-read; more pause-and-summarise.
Sometimes one paragraph per session is right.

If you're reading for an exam or specific application,
you can be more selective; if you're reading to deeply
understand, slowness is the work.

### Reading argumentative essays / opinion pieces

Track the argument:

- What's the thesis?
- What evidence supports it?
- What objections does the author address?
- What objections do they not address?

Read with the author's argument as the spine; everything
else is in service.

### Reading textbooks

Different from reading books. Textbooks are reference
works — you don't read them cover-to-cover by default.

- Read the chapter you need.
- Skim the rest of the chapter for context.
- Use the index to find specific concepts when you
  need them.

Textbook-as-reference; not textbook-as-novel.

## Anti-patterns

### Highlighting only

You highlight passages; the highlights don't get
processed. Re-read in 6 months — the highlighted text
is no easier to engage with than the un-highlighted.

Counter: highlights as candidates for cards / notes,
not as the engagement itself.

### Read-everything-once

You read every word; you finish the book; you
remember almost nothing. Reading-as-completion.

Counter: read selectively. Some chapters / sections
warrant deep engagement; others warrant skimming;
some can be skipped.

### Speed-reading deep material

You're trying to read 200 pages/hour of a dense theory
text. The processing rate doesn't keep up; the material
slides off.

Counter: slow down. The eye-tracking speed isn't the
bottleneck; comprehension is.

### "I'll take notes later"

You don't note during reading; you'll take notes
afterwards. By "afterwards", you've forgotten the
specifics. The notes you eventually take are
reconstructions, not records.

Counter: note during reading. Or designate explicit
post-section pauses for notes.

### Reading without recall

You read; you don't recall; the material decays.

Counter: recall pause every section.

### Saturation reading

You're tired; you keep reading; nothing's processing;
you finish more pages.

Counter: stop when saturation hits. Walk away. Resume
later.

### Reading for the social proof

You're reading because the book is "important", not
because you want to learn from it. Reading without
real engagement; finishing because finishing.

Counter: read what you actually want to learn. The
"important book" you're not engaging with is a wasted
afternoon; the obscure book that lights you up is
real learning.

## Calibrating effort

Some material warrants:

- 5 minutes (article skim).
- 30 minutes (paper).
- 2 hours (chapter).
- 10 hours (whole book, reading-actively).
- 50 hours (textbook with practice).

Match the time investment to the material's value and
your goal. Not every book is a 10-hour book; not every
article is a 5-minute one.

The mismatch is wasteful both ways.

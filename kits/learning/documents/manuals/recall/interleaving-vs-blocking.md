# Interleaving vs. Blocking — mixing topics in practice

Two patterns:

- **Blocked:** drill one topic deeply, then move to the
  next. AAAAAA, BBBBBB, CCCCCC.
- **Interleaved:** mix topics in the same session.
  ABCABCABC.

Blocked feels productive. Interleaved feels harder. The
research consistently shows interleaved produces better
retention and transfer. The "feels productive" is the
illusion you have to push past.

## Why blocked feels good

When you block-practice topic A:

- Each retrieval is similar to the last.
- Your brain adapts; you get fluent.
- Within the session, performance feels strong.
- You finish feeling you've mastered A.

The catch: the apparent mastery is short-lived. A week
later, blocked-A produces less retention than
interleaved-A.

## Why interleaved is better

When you interleave:

- Each retrieval requires a context-switch.
- Your brain has to re-identify which topic is being
  asked about.
- The discrimination practice itself strengthens
  memory.
- Performance within the session feels weaker — but
  long-term retention is stronger.

The discomfort during interleaved practice is the
learning. The brain is doing more work per item; the
memory traces are more robust because they're
re-activated against varying context.

## When interleaving works best

### Similar but distinguishable topics

If topics are easily confused, interleaving is
especially productive — you practice the discrimination
itself.

Examples:

- Different mathematical techniques that apply to
  similar-looking problems.
- Vocabulary words from different but related
  languages.
- Verb conjugations across tenses.
- Different design patterns that solve overlapping
  problems.

### Skill / technique acquisition

Sports practice that mixes shots / drills produces
better game performance than blocked practice — even
though blocked practice produces better drill-session
performance.

Same in music: practising several pieces or
techniques across a session beats deep blocks of
one.

### Long-term retention goal

Interleaving's benefit is in retention, not in
session-performance. If your goal is long-term
mastery, interleave even if session feels worse.

## When blocked might fit

### Initial encoding

Brand new topic; no memory yet. Blocking gives time to
form the initial encoding before context-switching
demands kick in.

After one or two blocked sessions for new material,
switch to interleaved.

### Truly unrelated topics

If you're practising spanish vocabulary and
trigonometry in the same session — the topics are too
distant to interleave meaningfully. The
context-switching cost outweighs the benefit.

### Specific exam prep with blocked exam structure

If the exam itself is blocked (one section all of topic
A, next all of B), some blocked practice for the format
may help.

Most exams aren't strictly blocked though; even
"section A" usually has variation within.

### Fatigue / cognitive overload

When you're tired, interleaving's cognitive cost
exceeds your capacity. Block-practice may be the
realistic choice; the alternative is no practice.

Better still: practice when you're rested.

## How to interleave

### Mixing within a session

Pick 3-5 related topics; cycle through them.

Example for software engineering:

- 5 minutes of algorithm cards.
- 5 minutes of system design.
- 5 minutes of language-specific (Python / Java).
- Rotate.

### Mixing across sessions

Hour-long session: switch topics every 15-20 minutes.
Different topics keep the brain alert; reduces fatigue.

### Mixing within a topic

Even within one topic, mix sub-topics. Studying calculus:
mix integration techniques rather than drilling one.

### Mixing question types

Mix recall / application / explanation across the
session. Different cognitive modes; good interleaving.

## How not to interleave

### Random switching mid-thought

You're partway through a problem; you switch to a
different topic. Bad — you didn't finish the cognitive
arc; both topics suffer.

Switch *between* completed retrievals, not mid-retrieval.

### Too-distant topics

Spanish vocabulary, trigonometry, history dates, in 5-
minute blocks. The brain spends most of its energy on
context-switching, little on retrieval.

Group topics that are similar enough to benefit from
discrimination practice.

### Too-frequent switching

Switching every 30 seconds — context-switch overhead
dominates. Switch at retrieval-completion intervals (a
few minutes per item).

## Specific patterns

### "Mixed flashcard deck"

Multiple subjects in one deck; shuffled. The
shuffling does the interleaving for you.

Most spaced-repetition tools (Anki, etc.) shuffle by
default — keep this rather than override.

### "Problem set with variety"

Math homework with mixed-topic problems beats
single-topic homework for retention. If you're
designing your own practice, mix the topics.

### "Mixed session for exam prep"

Practice exams with mixed-topic questions match exam
format and produce interleaving benefit.

### "Pomodoro with topic-rotation"

25-minute pomodoros, rotating topic each pomodoro. The
rotation is interleaving at session-level.

## When the advice contradicts

Some learners feel they retain better with blocked
practice. A few possibilities:

- **The illusion of within-session mastery.** Feels
  better; isn't.
- **Genuinely different individual responsiveness.**
  Some research suggests variation; effect size
  smaller for some learners.
- **Mismatch with goal.** Blocked may suffice for
  short-term retention; interleaved for long-term.

The honest test: actually compare. Block-practice for a
week; interleave-practice for another week with similar
material. Test retention at week 4. Whichever produces
better retention is your answer.

For most learners, interleaved wins. But variation
exists.

## Anti-patterns

### Pseudo-interleaving

You "mix topics" but practice them in long blocks
within the session. Real interleaving is item-level
mixing, not block-level.

### Interleave-paralysis

Decision overhead about what to practice next consumes
the session. Solution: pre-shuffle the practice items;
don't decide each time.

### Interleave-comfort

You interleave easy items; block hard items. Defeats
the principle. The hard items are exactly where
interleaving's discrimination-benefit applies most.

### Blocked-as-comfort

Sticking with blocked because it feels productive.
Recognise the discomfort of interleaved as the
learning; don't avoid.

### Conflating spacing with interleaving

Spacing (intervals over time) and interleaving (mixing
topics within session) are related but distinct. Both
help; use both.

# Desirable Difficulty — calibrating struggle

A learning principle: too easy is wasted; too hard is
frustrating; the productive zone is *just* hard enough
to require effort but not so hard that you fail
completely.

The instinct is to make practice comfortable. The
discipline is to keep it slightly uncomfortable.

## The 85% rule

A heuristic from learning research: the productive
zone is around 85% success. You retrieve correctly
about 85% of the time; you fail about 15%.

- **Above 85%:** material is too easy. Move to harder
  material or longer intervals.
- **Below 85%:** material is too hard. Reduce
  difficulty or shorter intervals.
- **At 85%:** sweet spot. Continue.

The number isn't sacred — different sources cite 70-90%.
The principle holds: optimal learning happens at the
*edge* of what you can do, not in the comfort zone.

## What "harder" looks like

When you're succeeding too easily:

### Longer intervals between practice

Wait two weeks instead of two days. The longer interval
forces deeper retrieval; the previously-easy material
becomes appropriately challenging.

This is the spaced-repetition principle (see
`spaced-repetition-design`).

### Less context / fewer cues

You've been practising "given the chapter title, recall
the key points". Switch to "given a vague topic, recall
the chapter contents". Less cue → more retrieval work.

### Generation instead of recognition

If you've been answering multiple-choice, switch to
free-form. If free-form has become routine, generate
your own questions.

### Apply, don't just recall

If you've memorised the formula, apply it to a new
problem. Application is harder than recall and reveals
gaps recall didn't.

### Cross-context

You learned the material in one context; test in
another. "I memorised this in study; can I apply during
the actual problem?".

## What "easier" looks like

When you're failing too often:

### Shorter intervals

You waited too long; nothing's there. Retrieve sooner;
build up before stretching.

### More context

If "recall the chapter points from title" yields
nothing, switch to "recall the chapter points after
reading the section headers". Cues bridge the gap.

### Smaller chunks

If "explain the whole protocol" fails, break to
"explain step 1; explain step 2; ...". Each piece is
manageable; the whole emerges.

### Re-expose to the source

You're failing because the encoding wasn't solid.
Re-read carefully (don't speed-read); retry retrieval.

### Lower stakes

You're failing because pressure makes retrieval
harder. Practice in a low-stakes setting (alone; no
timer; nobody watching). Build up to higher-stakes.

## Difficulty is per-card / per-topic

The 85% rule is across the practice set, not per
session. Some cards will be at 95%; some at 50%; the
average should hover around 85%.

If most cards are at 95% and a few at 30% — fine. The
hard cards get more drilling; the easy cards spaced
out. The average can stay healthy.

If everything's at 50% — material is too hard
overall; retreat.

If everything's at 95% — material is too easy
overall; advance.

## Why slightly-too-hard is productive

Counter-intuitive but well-established:

- **Failure followed by correction strengthens memory
  more than success.** The "test effect" + the
  productive struggle.
- **Retrieval failure activates the surrounding
  context.** The gap is more salient than the
  fluent recall.
- **Spacing produces retrieval failure as a feature.**
  You'd never push intervals long enough to fail if
  failure was avoided; the failure-tolerance is what
  enables long intervals.

The "make it hard" instinct goes against the comfort-
seeking instinct. The discomfort is the work.

## When too-hard is genuinely too-hard

The 85% rule has limits. Some failures are productive;
some are pure frustration.

Tells of unproductive difficulty:

- **Repeated failure with no insight.** You fail; you
  see the answer; nothing connects; you fail again.
  No memory formation.
- **Cognitive overload.** Too many things at once;
  attention fragmenting.
- **Foundational gap.** You're failing because you
  don't have the prerequisite, not because the
  current material is hard.
- **Frustration spiral.** Each failure compounds; you're
  losing focus; performance degrades.

In these cases:

- Drop difficulty.
- Build up the prerequisite first.
- Take a real break.

## Scaffolding into difficulty

For new material:

1. **Easy** (cues, examples, low stakes). Build basic
   familiarity.
2. **Medium** (fewer cues, longer intervals,
   self-quizzing). Solidify.
3. **Hard** (no cues, application, novel problems).
   Test depth.

Don't start at hard. The instinct can be "I should be
able to handle this" — pride before practice. Hard
practice on un-encoded material produces frustration
without learning.

## Different tasks have different sweet-spots

### Vocabulary / facts

The 85% rule fits well. Flashcards naturally calibrate.

### Procedures / multi-step

You may need higher success rate (95%+) before moving
to harder application. Procedures with errors are
worse than not knowing.

### Conceptual understanding

Hard to measure success/failure cleanly. Use Feynman
test (see `feynman-method`) instead of pure
recall stats.

### Skills (sports / music / arts)

Different optimum — skill practice often warrants
lower success rate (deliberate practice at the edge of
ability). The "learning" is muscle memory, not just
cognitive recall.

## Calibrating in practice

Track:

- Success rate per session.
- Per-topic difficulty (some are hard; some easy).
- Whether session feels productive vs. frustrating.

Adjust:

- If success rate too high: extend intervals, switch
  to harder format.
- If success rate too low: more frequent practice,
  smaller chunks.
- If frustration without progress: take break,
  examine prerequisite gaps.

The calibration is ongoing. Material's difficulty
shifts as you learn it; what was sweet-spot last week
is too easy now.

## Specific situations

### Cramming for exam

Not a great context for the 85% rule. The temptation
to keep difficulty low (so you "feel ready") is high;
the actual exam will be at full difficulty.

Honest practice: simulate exam conditions; deliberate
overload; calibrate down only after confronting the
real difficulty.

### Long-term retention

This is where 85% rule shines. Practice over months;
each session at the edge; spaced intervals doing the
work.

### Skill acquisition (sport / instrument / craft)

Deliberate practice principle: practice at the edge of
ability with rapid feedback. Calibrate by skill-
component; some need fluency-level practice (high
success), some need stretching (lower success).

### Re-entry after time off

You learned X a year ago; haven't practised. Re-entry
practice should start easy — even if you "should know
this". The calibration recovers quickly.

## Anti-patterns

### Optimising for ease

Practice that always succeeds. Comfortable; not
learning. Push harder.

### Optimising for failure

"More failure means more learning." False — failure
without insight is just frustration. Aim for productive
struggle, not maximal struggle.

### Generic difficulty

Setting a universal "hard" level for all material.
Different topics warrant different difficulties.

### Avoiding measurement

Practising without tracking success rate. Calibration
becomes vibes-based. Tracking even simple binary "got
it / didn't" gives data.

### Pride-driven difficulty

Picking harder than you should because you "should be
able to handle it". Result: frustrated practice with
poor retention.

Honest calibration: where am I right now, not where I
think I should be?

### Lowering difficulty when frustrated

Sometimes correct (genuinely too hard). Sometimes
avoidance — the frustration was on the verge of
breakthrough. Distinguish; don't always retreat.

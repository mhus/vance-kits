# Format by Goal — different reading for different formats

A book is not a paper is not a textbook is not an
article. Each has different conventions, density,
intent. Reading them all the same way produces
mismatched effort — too much on what doesn't earn it,
too little on what does.

This manual is about matching technique to format.

## Books

Long-form, sustained argument or narrative. Variable
density.

### Non-fiction book

Most non-fiction books expand a thesis that could
be a 30-page essay. The book length serves: examples,
evidence, alternative perspectives, repetition for
emphasis.

Read with this in mind:

- The thesis is usually in the introduction +
  conclusion. Often clear.
- Each chapter develops one aspect of the thesis.
- Examples and stories are illustrative; can be
  skimmed if you grasp the point.
- Sometimes one chapter is the whole book; the rest
  is repetition / examples.

Reading approach:

- Pre-read intro + conclusion + TOC.
- Identify which chapters matter.
- Deep-read those; skim or skip the rest.

A 300-page book often has 80 pages of substance; the
rest is supporting material. Match your effort to the
substance.

### Academic non-fiction

Tighter; less repetition; more dense. Treat more like
a paper.

Pre-read: introduction + conclusion + each chapter's
intro / abstract. Decide chapters that warrant deep
reading.

### Textbook

Reference + sequential learning. Different default.

If using as reference: pass 1 is index lookup; pass 2
is the specific section.

If using for sequential learning: chapter-by-chapter,
multi-pass per chapter, exercises if any.

Don't read a textbook like a non-fiction book — too
dense; you'll exhaust before retention.

### Fiction

Different goals — pleasure, narrative, character. Reading
for retention isn't usually the point.

If you want to retain (e.g. for analysis):

- Read once for the experience.
- Re-engage with notes / discussion / second read for
  analysis.

Multi-pass for fiction is appropriate but the passes
serve different purposes (experience vs. analysis), not
shallow-then-deep.

### Memoir / biography

Specific events, character development. Often facts
worth retaining (dates, places, decisions).

Read like creative non-fiction: enjoy the narrative;
note the substantive specifics in margins or after
sessions.

## Papers

### Empirical paper

Specific structure: abstract, intro, related work,
methods, results, discussion, conclusion.

Reading approach (the "three-pass" approach by Keshav
is the canonical version):

- **Pass 1 (5-10 min):** title, abstract, intro,
  conclusion, section headings, glance at math /
  figures, references. Decide: relevant?
- **Pass 2 (30-60 min):** read in detail but skip
  proofs; understand at the level of "what was done
  and what was found".
- **Pass 3 (4-5 hours):** virtually re-implement;
  challenge every assumption; understand why each
  decision was made.

Most papers warrant pass 1 + selective pass 2. Pass 3
is for papers central to your work or of unusual
significance.

### Theoretical paper / proof

Slower than empirical. Engagement is at the proof
level.

- Pass 1: theorems / claims; not proofs.
- Pass 2: proof outlines.
- Pass 3 (if warranted): line-by-line proofs.

For learning: try to prove the theorem yourself before
reading the proof. Then read; compare.

### Survey / review paper

Different purpose: orient you in a subfield.

- Pass 1 thoroughly: the survey's structure is the
  field's structure.
- Skim sub-areas not relevant to you.
- Use as a map to find primary papers worth deeper
  attention.

Don't deep-read a survey end-to-end; that's both
exhausting and inefficient.

### Position / opinion paper

Argument structure. Read like an essay, looking for
thesis + evidence + objections.

### Industry / company technical reports

Variable. Often promotional. Read pass 1 carefully
(intro, claims); pass 2 only on credibility-supporting
sections (methods, data).

## Articles

### Long-form essay (think LRB, Atlantic, Aeon)

Sustained argument; well-edited; often substantive.

Pass 1: title + lead + first paragraph + last
paragraph. Decide.

Pass 2: full read with engagement. Often 30-60 min.

Notes / discussion afterwards if it's important.

### Magazine article

Mixed quality and density. Pass 1 (5 min) usually
sufficient; pass 2 only for genuinely meritorious
ones.

### News article

Mostly shallow; mostly recent-events context. Pass 1
typically all you need.

The "5 W's" — who, what, when, where, why — are
usually in the lead. Read the lead; decide if you need
more.

### Op-ed

Argumentative; one author's view. Read once; consider
who else has written on this; calibrate.

### Blog post / Substack

Variable. From technical-deep-dive to weekly-musing.

Length isn't a guide to depth. Some 5000-word posts
are shallow; some 800-word posts are dense.

Match attention to substance, not length.

### Twitter / threaded social

Single point per tweet. Threads sometimes work as
articles in disguise.

Don't multi-pass; once is enough. If something seems
substantive, find the long-form version.

## Reference materials

### Documentation

Look up specific information; don't read cover-to-
cover. Index / search is the entry point.

When reading: take notes on what you commonly look up.
Common lookups become flashcards or mental notes.

### Manual / how-to / reference book

Same as documentation. Reference-mode reading; rarely
sequential.

### Wiki / Wikipedia

Pass 1 is often the whole engagement: intro + relevant
section. Click through citations for detail.

For research-oriented use: Wikipedia as starting point
to find primary sources, not as primary itself.

### Cheatsheet

Quick reference. Read once for the structure; then use
as lookup.

For learning, convert key items to flashcards.

## Specific genres

### Self-help / productivity

Often:

- Big idea (worth knowing).
- Anecdotes (skimmable).
- Application advice (variable usefulness).

Pass 1 + selective pass 2. Don't expect dense substance
across the whole book.

### How-to / tutorials

Sequential; designed to be followed. Reading without
*doing* often produces no skill transfer.

Read with hands-on; alternate read-and-do.

### Code repositories / source code

Different: reading like exploration.

- Start at README / main entry point.
- Trace key paths.
- Skip what's not relevant to your investigation.

Reading source code linearly is usually wrong. Reading
by tracing-the-path is usually right.

### Specifications / RFCs

Dense; precise. Reading approach:

- Pass 1: structure (sections, examples).
- Pass 2 on relevant sections; skim or skip parts that
  don't apply.
- Reference back as needed.

Don't read an RFC cover-to-cover unless implementing
the whole spec.

### Legal documents (contracts, terms, policies)

Slow; precise; skip-resistant in important parts.

For high-stakes: read fully. For low-stakes: read
relevant clauses (highlights, change-from-prior-version).

Don't multi-pass; once-deep is the default. Re-read
specific clauses as needed.

## Calibrating to your goal

For each piece of reading, ask:

### What do you want?

- Specific information → reference / search.
- Quick orientation → pass 1.
- Substantive understanding → pass 2.
- Mastery / retention → multi-pass with notes / cards.
- Entertainment → read the way you enjoy.

### How much time?

5 minutes = pass 1 only.
30 minutes = pass 1 + selective pass 2.
2 hours = full pass 2 + start of pass 3 if warranted.
A weekend = multi-pass with notes.
Months = textbook-level engagement.

### What format?

The format constrains technique. Don't fight the
format.

### How important?

A casually-discovered article ≠ a paper central to
your project. Calibrate effort.

## Anti-patterns

### Same-approach for every format

Reading every paper, book, article, blog post the same
way. Mismatch in effort vs. content; some you over-read,
some you under-read.

### Cover-to-cover by default

For books especially. Most books don't warrant cover-
to-cover. Selective beats complete.

### Skim-everything by default

The other extreme. Some material warrants depth;
skimming everything misses the substance.

### Reading for completion

The book is on your list; you finish even though it
stopped serving the goal. Sunk-cost reading.

Counter: stop when value drops below cost.

### Reading without goal

You're reading "to learn" without a specific learning
goal. You read; nothing sticks; you can't articulate
what you learned.

Counter: even loose goal ("orientation in this
field"; "main arguments of this thinker") helps.

### Format-mismatch on technical sources

You try to read a paper like a blog post; you skip
methods. You miss whether the claim is supported.

You try to read a blog post like a paper; you spend
30 min on a 5-min piece.

Recognise format; calibrate.

### Lecture-mode reading

Reading at the pace and engagement level of attending
a lecture. Reading allows your own pace and active
engagement; using lecture-mode wastes the medium's
advantage.

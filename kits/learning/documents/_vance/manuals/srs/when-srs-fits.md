# When SRS Fits — and when it doesn't

SRS is great for some material and a bad fit for
other material. Applying SRS to material it doesn't fit
produces frustration and bad cards. Recognising the
fit avoids that.

## Where SRS fits well

### Vocabulary

The natural home of SRS. Foreign-language vocab,
technical vocab, names of things. Each item is
discrete, atomic, has a clear cue and answer.

### Specific facts

Dates, numbers, formulas, definitions, classifications.
Material that's a discrete chunk with no internal
ambiguity.

Examples:

- Boiling point of water.
- Capital of countries.
- Atomic number of carbon.
- Definition of "eventual consistency".
- HTTP status codes.

### Procedures with discrete steps

If a procedure can be broken into named steps and
each step is recallable independently, SRS works.

Example: "Steps of the citric acid cycle" — can be
made into discrete cards for each step.

Limit: long procedures (8+ steps) are brittle as
single cards. Break or use cloze deletions.

### Recognition material

Identifying things — paintings, plants, kanji
characters, faces, logos. Image-based cards are
naturally suited.

### Concepts that have specific names

Specific design patterns; specific algorithms;
specific named principles. The name-to-concept
association is SRS-friendly.

## Where SRS partially fits

### Conceptual understanding

Cards can support understanding but can't substitute
for it. Trying to memorise concepts you don't
understand produces parrot-cards that fail in
application.

Use:

- After understanding (Feynman-tested), SRS to retain
  the technical vocabulary surrounding the concept.
- For specific named concepts that need fast recall.

Don't use:

- As a substitute for understanding.
- For complex multi-step reasoning.

### Procedural skills

If the skill has discrete components, those can be
SRS-ed. The integrated skill itself can't.

Example: chess. Specific opening moves: SRS-able.
Tactical patterns: partially. Whole-game strategy: no.

Use SRS for the components; deliberate practice for
the integration.

### Languages — beyond vocab

Vocabulary: SRS gold-standard.

Grammar rules: partial — specific rules can be cards;
the *application* needs practice in context.

Listening / speaking fluency: not SRS material.
Practice with real / spaced exposure.

Writing in target language: not SRS — output skill.

A complete language-learning plan uses SRS for vocab +
some grammar; everything else is practice and
exposure.

### Reading retention (research / non-fiction)

You read a book; you want to remember what was in it.

Two approaches:

- **Granular**: extract specific facts / claims / quotes
  as cards.
- **Conceptual**: use Feynman-method to verify
  understanding; rely on note-taking + retrieval-
  practice rather than SRS.

Often a mix: SRS for specifics; notes for the
narrative.

## Where SRS doesn't fit

### Skills

Tennis serve, public speaking, drawing, code
architecture decisions, leadership judgement. These are
*output* skills built through practice with feedback,
not retrieval.

Cards on "what's a good code architecture?" don't
produce architectural skill. Practice doing it does.

### Creativity / synthesis

Generating new ideas; combining known elements in
novel ways; creative writing. SRS works on retrieval,
not generation.

You can SRS specific writing techniques or vocabulary;
you can't SRS the act of writing well.

### Deep reasoning

Mathematical problem-solving, philosophical reasoning,
strategic thinking. The *substance* might have
SRS-able components; the *reasoning process* is
practice-based.

### Tacit knowledge

Things you know how to do but can't articulate
("knowing what's going to happen", "knowing when
something's off"). SRS requires articulation; tacit
knowledge resists it.

### Quickly-changing fields

Technical material that changes every six months.
Cards built today are stale by next year. SRS works
best for stable knowledge.

For volatile fields: lighter notes; rebuild as field
evolves.

### Material you'll only need once

Studying for an exam where the material won't matter
again. SRS pays off over months / years; for one
exam, traditional cramming may be more efficient
(though worse for long-term retention).

If you genuinely won't need the material after, don't
build durable cards. If you might need it later, SRS
and accept the up-front cost.

### Personal / emotional learning

Self-knowledge, relationships, life lessons. The
"learning" isn't recall; it's integration into
behaviour. SRS doesn't drive that.

## How to recognise the wrong fit

Tells:

- **Cards that fail repeatedly** despite the underlying
  understanding being clear. The card's a mismatch
  for the material.
- **Cards that succeed in review but you can't apply**.
  You've memorised the answer; you don't have the
  underlying knowledge.
- **Cards on material that keeps shifting**. You're
  rewriting cards every month.
- **Cards that feel pointless**. You can articulate the
  card's content; you just don't see the value of
  reviewing it.

When the fit is wrong, switching tools:

- For skills → deliberate practice.
- For understanding → Feynman / re-reading / discussion.
- For volatile material → notes + occasional reread.
- For tacit knowledge → experience + mentorship.

## Hybrid use

SRS as one tool in a broader learning toolkit:

### SRS + reading

Read deeply; understand the material via Feynman; SRS
the specific facts you want long-term.

### SRS + practice

Practice the skill; SRS the underlying knowledge that
supports the skill.

### SRS + notes

Notes capture the narrative; SRS captures the discrete
facts. Notes are the structure; SRS is the
durability.

### SRS + spaced practice (non-card)

Some "practice" you can space without cards:
periodically returning to a project, periodically
rebuilding a concept from scratch. Same spacing
principle; different mechanism.

## Specific use cases

### Medical school

Massive vocabulary; massive fact load. SRS-classic.
Anki decks like AnKing exist for this reason.

The conceptual understanding (pathophysiology) needs
parallel non-SRS work.

### Language learning

Vocab via SRS (gold-standard).

Grammar mostly via SRS; exceptions practiced in real
sentences.

Listening / speaking via spaced exposure to native
content.

### Programming

Specific syntax / library calls: SRS works.

Algorithms / data structures: discrete components can
be SRSed; the *application* is practice.

System design / architecture: practice-based, not SRS.

### Math

Theorems / definitions: SRS.

Worked-example recall: works moderately for technique
patterns; doesn't substitute for problem-solving
practice.

Problem-solving itself: not SRS.

### Music

Reading notation: partial SRS (chord names, key
signatures).

Playing: practice.

Music theory concepts: SRS for the vocabulary; theory
in application is practice.

### Trivia / general knowledge

SRS-friendly; specific facts, discrete cards.

Whether this is worth doing: personal preference.

## What to do when material doesn't fit SRS

Use the right tool:

- **Skills** → deliberate practice.
- **Understanding** → Feynman, discussion, writing.
- **Application** → real problem-solving.
- **Tacit knowledge** → mentorship, observation, time.
- **Creative output** → make things; iterate.

SRS is one tool among many. Mismatch makes you waste
effort on the wrong activity.

## Anti-patterns

### SRS-everything

Trying to put all learning material into SRS because
SRS works for some learning. Wrong tool for some
material; the misuse degrades the SRS habit too.

### SRS-as-procrastination

Building cards feels productive; reviewing existing
cards feels productive; doing the actual harder work
(understanding, application, practice) gets deferred.

Counter: SRS supplements other learning; doesn't
replace it.

### Big-deck syndrome

Massive deck with thousands of cards; you can't keep
up; the daily-review pile grows; you eventually quit.

Counter: smaller deck of cards you actually use.
Quality over quantity.

### Permanent SRS

Cards that have been mature for years; you keep
reviewing; the time invested produces nothing new.

Counter: burn (suspend / mark as known) cards that
have done their work.

### Importing without curating

You import a 5000-card deck; most cards aren't relevant;
you slog through anyway.

Counter: filter aggressively; suspend / delete what
you don't need.

## Output line

- "**Material fits SRS** — proceed with card design."
- "**Partial fit** — use SRS for ⟨specific subset⟩;
  use ⟨other tool⟩ for ⟨specific subset⟩."
- "**Material doesn't fit SRS** — use ⟨specific
  alternative tool⟩."
- "**Mismatch detected post-card-creation** — suspend
  or delete; switch tool."

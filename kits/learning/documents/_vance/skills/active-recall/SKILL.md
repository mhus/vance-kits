The user wants to retain material they've encountered.
Most learners default to re-reading; re-reading produces
the *feeling* of knowing without the actual retention.
Active recall — testing yourself by trying to retrieve
the material from memory — is much more effective,
though much less comfortable.

The trade-off: testing yourself feels harder than
re-reading. The discomfort *is* the work. If it feels
easy, you're not learning.

## Default protocol

1. **What do you want to remember?** Specific facts /
   concepts / procedures / vocabulary. The form depends
   on the target.
2. **First-pass exposure.** Read / hear / see the
   material once. This is the input phase; don't try
   to memorise yet.
3. **Close the source.** Cover it / put it away. Now
   try to retrieve. See `retrieval-practice`.
4. **Check the retrieval.** Did you recall correctly?
   If not, what was missing?
5. **Calibrate the difficulty.** Right level of
   struggle is the optimum. Too easy: not learning;
   too hard: frustration. See `desirable-difficulty`.
6. **Mix topics.** Don't drill one concept exclusively;
   interleave. See `interleaving-vs-blocking`.

## On-demand manuals

- `retrieval-practice` — concrete techniques: blank-
  page recall, flashcards, self-quizzing, free recall,
  cued recall. Picking the right technique for the
  material. The "test before you feel ready"
  discipline.
- `desirable-difficulty` — calibrating struggle. Too
  easy is wasted; too hard is frustrating. The "85%
  rule" and its variants. When to make harder; when
  easier.
- `interleaving-vs-blocking` — mixing topics in
  practice. Why blocked practice feels productive
  but produces less retention. How to interleave
  without losing focus.

If you're new to active recall, start with
`retrieval-practice` — the technique itself is the
foundation.

## Hard rules

- **Don't re-read instead of recall.** Re-reading is
  the trap. Comfort doesn't equal retention. Force
  yourself to close the book and try.
- **Don't peek prematurely.** When trying to recall,
  give yourself real effort time before checking. The
  effort *is* the learning, not the answer.
- **Don't grade kindly.** "Almost remembered" isn't
  remembered. The check is binary: did you recall, or
  did you have to look up? Honest grading drives
  honest practice.
- **Don't avoid the hard cards.** Drill is most useful
  on the things you're worst at. The temptation to
  practice what you're already good at is real;
  resist.
- **Don't drill at end of session.** Recall works best
  with sleep / time between attempts. Don't try to
  cram by repeated drill in one sitting.
- **Don't measure by time spent.** Time-on-task isn't
  the metric; successful retrieval after spaced
  intervals is. Twenty minutes of recall beats two
  hours of re-reading.
- **Don't avoid embarrassment.** Forgetting in private
  is data. Forgetting in public-with-discipline (e.g.
  a study partner) is faster data. The temptation to
  hide the gaps slows learning.
